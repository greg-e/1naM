## 1 — John, Day 0

The sound woke him at 2:05, a distant, percussive thud that rattled the window in its frame and was gone before he'd fully surfaced. He lay there a minute in the dark, waiting for it to resolve into something — a siren, a second explosion, anything that would tell him what he'd just heard. Nothing came. No fire trucks, no police, no distant alarm working itself up the way alarms usually did in the ten minutes after something happened in a neighborhood like his. Just the ordinary hum of the house around him, the dog's tags shifting faintly somewhere down the hall as Dan resettled himself. He decided it was a transformer, or somebody's car doing something stupid on the main road, and let the dark pull him back under.

He woke again at six, same as every morning, no different from ten thousand mornings before it. He didn't check his phone right away — another ordinary habit, coffee before screens — and took Dan out for the walk that started his day the same way it always did, leash in one hand, travel mug in the other, the big dog's tail already going before they'd cleared the driveway.

The neighborhood was quiet in a way he registered before he could name it. More cars sitting in more driveways than usual, as if half the street had decided to work from home. No sign of the Hendersons' kids at the corner where the bus usually idled around 6:45 — no kids at all, actually, not on his stretch, not on the cross street either. He walked further than he meant to, some low-grade curiosity pulling him along past his usual turnaround, and found the same stillness holding all the way to the school bus stop two streets over. Empty. No bus. No parents in bathrobes standing at the end of driveways with travel mugs of their own.

*Must be a holiday,* he thought, running through the calendar in his head and coming up empty. He couldn't place one. That bothered him more than it should have, a small itch he carried the rest of the walk home.

Back in the kitchen, Dan settled into his bed with a grunt, and John pulled up the news on his phone mostly to solve the holiday question, nothing more urgent than that. The headline feed hadn't moved. He scrolled, frowning, checked the timestamp on the top story — nine hours old, no correction, no update layered over it the way a real morning's news always accumulated by six a.m. He switched to a second app. Same frozen feed. A third. The anchor thumbnail on the local station's live page showed a paused frame, some mid-sentence expression locked in place like a screenshot standing in for a broadcast that wasn't happening.

He moved to his laptop and widened the search, methodical now, the way he approached any problem at work — checking source after source, national feeds, international ones, social platforms that should have been a chaotic scroll of overnight activity and instead sat still, timestamps clustering in the hours before whatever this was. Nothing anywhere had moved since sometime around two in the morning.

He sat with that for a second, and then noticed the thing underneath it, the thing that had actually been bothering him the whole walk without surfacing all the way — he hadn't heard a single aircraft. Not one. He lived under enough of a flight corridor that some low hum of traffic was almost always present if you knew to listen for it, and he did know, had trained himself to for years, ever since he'd started flying himself. This morning there'd been nothing. Not distant, not close. Nothing at all.

He opened FlightAware without entirely deciding to, some instinct ahead of his conscious reasoning, and the map that loaded in stopped him cold. Empty airspace over the entire country. Empty almost everywhere, the whole global map reduced to a scatter of maybe fifty aircraft, most of them long, slow arcs across oceans — flights that had left far enough out that whatever this was hadn't caught up to them yet.

One of those tracks was doing something the others weren't. A Delta flight out of Atlanta, well out over the North Atlantic, its track line already bent north and west of its filed route toward Prague, and its position marker — live, not historical, actually crawling as he watched — circling low and slow over a small airport on the Newfoundland coast, doing something that looked, even to his non-pilot eye scanning the altitude readout, less like approach and more like someone checking the field before committing to it. He refreshed the page once, twice, watched the little aircraft icon keep moving, keep circling. Gander, the label read, once he zoomed in far enough to catch the airport code. He didn't know yet what any of this meant — didn't have the context to feel relief exactly, not this early — but it snagged at him regardless, one clean, specific fact in a morning that had otherwise given him nothing but absence. *Somebody up there is making a decision right now. Whatever this is, it's not nothing.*

He filed it away — a fact worth holding onto, not yet a thing to act on. There was too much else in front of him still making no sense, and the methodical part of him, the part that never trusted a single data point when more were available, wanted to keep gathering before he did anything else.

On a hunch, he pulled up the local DOT traffic camera feeds next, the ones he sometimes checked before a bad commute — and this was where the wrongness stopped being ambiguous. By quarter past seven on a weekday, I-85 and the Perimeter should have already been thick with the leading edge of rush hour, brake lights strung out for miles the way they always were. Instead the main arteries sat nearly empty, a handful of vehicles scattered across lanes that should have been packed bumper to bumper — and several of those vehicles weren't moving at all. One camera near a Perimeter interchange showed what looked like three or four cars clustered strangely close together mid-lane, doors closed, nothing about the image explaining itself at that resolution. Another, closer to his own exit, caught a stopped truck straddling two lanes, no flashers, no movement anywhere in frame, traffic that should have been backing up behind it simply... not there to back up. He sat very still in his kitchen chair, doing the math his own commute had taught him a thousand times over — this was not a light morning. This was a road that should have been full, and wasn't, except for the vehicles that weren't going anywhere at all.

He didn't go out yet. Some careful, methodical part of him wanted to exhaust every remote way of checking before he committed to anything.

At nine o'clock, his first scheduled call of the day came due — a standing internal sync he'd been on a hundred times before, the kind of meeting that started within thirty seconds of the hour whether anyone wanted it to or not. He logged in and watched the participant grid fill with names and no faces, camera icons crossed out one after another failing to connect, the meeting timer running past five minutes, then ten, with no one else ever joining. He sat there staring at his own video tile, alone in a call meant for six people, and felt something in the shape of the morning finally, fully tip over.

He started calling people after that — not systematically, just working down the list of everyone who mattered, friends first, then his brother, then coworkers he barely knew but tried anyway, some instinct insisting that surely, surely one of them would pick up. Every call rang through to nothing. No voicemail greeting, no busy signal, just an open line ringing into a silence that never picked up, over and over, until he'd gone through nearly everyone he could think to call and the silence had started to feel less like bad luck and more like an answer.

He grabbed his keys.

The main road, two turns from his house, should have been busy by now — it always was on a weekday morning, a steady creep of commuters working toward the interstate. Instead it held only a scatter of vehicles, stopped at odd, wrong angles across a road built for a hundred times that many. A sedan sat nose-first in the guardrail on the curve everyone took too fast, engine still running, headlights still on, throwing a useless cone of light across the grass in full daylight. Further up, cars sat stalled in both lanes, some drifted onto the shoulder, most still idling, radios or dashboard displays still glowing — the absence of everyone else was, somehow, almost worse than the wrecks themselves.

He pulled over at the first one and called 911 before he'd even gotten out of his own car, phone pressed to his ear, eyes on the driver slumped against the wheel of the sedan ahead of him. It rang. Kept ringing. No hold music, no automated message, nothing — just an open line ringing into nothing, over and over, until he finally pulled the phone away from his ear and sat there a moment in his own idling car, staring at a screen that had stopped meaning anything.

He tried again, thinking he'd misdialed, got the same nothing. Got out anyway, walked up to the sedan on legs that didn't feel entirely his, and made himself do the thing every part of him wanted to avoid — reached in through the open window and checked, two fingers against the side of the driver's neck the way he vaguely remembered from some long-ago first aid course. Nothing. No pulse, no breath moving the chest, skin already cool in a way that told him this hadn't just happened in the last few minutes. He stood there a moment with his hand still half-raised, the confirmation landing heavier than the sight of the body alone had. He didn't get much closer to the others after that. Didn't need to — the first one had told him everything the rest would only have repeated.

He drove home slower than he'd driven out, hands tight on the wheel, panic sitting cold and constant in his chest now in a way it hadn't back at the kitchen table, no matter how bad the traffic cameras had looked. Dan met him at the door, tail going, entirely unaware that anything in the world had changed, and John crouched down and put both hands into the dog's fur for a long moment before he made himself stand back up.

## 2 — Sam, Day 0

The flight had left Atlanta a few minutes before eleven-thirty, climbing out over Georgia into a clear night, cruise established somewhere over the Carolinas before Sam had gone back to the crew rest compartment to get what sleep he could ahead of his shift. This was a long-haul route, Atlanta to Prague, close to nine and a half hours block time — long enough to require a third pilot in the rotation, an augmented crew built specifically so no one had to fly the whole thing on too little rest. Sam was that third seat tonight, the relief pilot, scheduled to come up partway through the flight and give the captain a proper break during cruise. Routine. He'd flown this route more times than he could easily count, long enough to know its rhythms by heart — a night flight, a quiet cockpit, a sunrise somewhere over Central Europe that he still hadn't gotten tired of.

His alarm woke him a little after four, timed to get him up to the flight deck for a 4:30 handoff, and he lay there a moment in the dark of the bunk the way he always did, letting his body catch up with the schedule before he asked it to perform. Nothing had felt wrong yet. The aircraft's ordinary hum was exactly what it always was, engines steady, no turbulence, no chime out of place.

He climbed up to the flight deck a few minutes later, still working the last of the sleep out of his eyes, and the first thing that registered wrong was the quiet — not the ordinary quiet of a long-haul cruise, but something underneath it, some absence he couldn't immediately name. Then he saw the captain slumped forward against the harness, unmoving in a way that made Sam's whole body go cold before his mind had finished processing what he was looking at.

He didn't remember, afterward, exactly how long he stood in the doorway. Long enough that some clinical, trained part of his brain finally overrode the rest of him and made him move — check for a pulse he already knew he wasn't going to find, check the instruments, confirm the aircraft was still flying itself properly on autopilot while he tried to make sense of the man in the left seat.

He was beside himself. He would admit that later, to anyone who asked, without much shame — freaked out in a way sixteen years of training had never quite prepared him for, because none of that training had ever covered this. He made himself move anyway. Pulled the captain gently, as gently as panic allowed, out of the seat and into the aisle behind it, an act that felt obscene and necessary in exactly equal measure, and settled himself into the seat that had, five minutes ago, belonged to someone else.

Then he went back and checked the rest of the aircraft, because some fragment of procedure insisted that he understand the full scope of what he was dealing with before he made any decision that mattered. The other pilot resting in the crew bunk, gone the same way. The senior flight attendant near the forward galley, gone. He walked the length of the cabin once, slow, checking rows he already suspected he didn't need to check, confirming what the silence had already told him before he'd even started counting. Two hundred and forty-one passengers. Twelve crew, himself included. He was, as far as he could tell, moving through a fully populated aircraft in which he was the only thing still alive.

Back on the flight deck, he keyed the radio out of pure procedural instinct, calling for anyone — company ops, ATC, another aircraft on the emergency frequency, anything. Nothing came back. Not silence exactly — the carrier tone was there, the radio was working — just no voice on the other end of it, anywhere, on any frequency he tried.

He gave himself maybe half an hour, all told, from the moment he'd found the captain slumped in the seat to the moment he made himself decide. Not because the horror had faded — it hadn't, wouldn't for a long time — but because sixteen years of training had built something into him that didn't care how he felt underneath it: a pilot's job, when everything goes wrong at once, is to act, not to sit with it. Sitting with it could come later, on the ground, if there was a ground worth calling that anymore.

Every instinct that training had built into him said the same thing procedure always said in a situation like this: don't try to make it home, find the nearest suitable field. Gander was the obvious answer — the old transatlantic diversion airport tucked into the Newfoundland coast, a runway built decades ago to handle exactly this kind of widebody emergency, still there, still rated, whether or not anyone was left on the ground to work it. The aircraft had already flown well past the point abeam Gander on its way out, deep enough into the ocean crossing that turning back meant a real couple of hours of flying just to retrace the distance, let alone anything after that.

He declared an emergency out of habit more than expectation, knowing there was likely no one on the other end to hear it, and began the turn — banking the great aircraft slowly north and west, watching the heading indicator swing until Gander sat dead ahead on the nose and Prague fell away behind him for good.

The long transit back gave him time he didn't especially want, hours alone with the autopilot doing most of the actual flying while his mind kept circling back to the empty seats behind him. By the time the Newfoundland coast finally resolved out of the haze ahead, he'd rehearsed the approach so many times in his head that his hands barely needed to think about it anymore.

The real caution came from what happened once he got there. No ATC meant no one to confirm the runway was clear, no ATIS to give him current winds, no ground vehicle to check for debris or wreckage, no controller to talk him down. He circled the field first at a distance, just looking, before he trusted himself any closer. Then a long, low pass over the runway itself, gear up, scanning the surface at altitude and speed, watching for anything that would turn a landing into a disaster on top of everything else. The runway lights were on, still cycling through their pattern for no one, and the strip itself looked clear, no wreckage, no obstruction he could see. He circled out again to check whatever wind indication he could find visually — a flag, smoke, anything — found nothing conclusive, and flew a full approach anyway, only to go around at the last moment rather than commit to something he wasn't fully certain of. He set up a second time, patient with himself in a way he wouldn't have had the luxury to be with two hundred and forty other lives depending on the decision. This time he stayed on it, configuring flaps and gear on a checklist he ran alone, out loud, his own voice the only one answering back.

He flew that low pass and the approach after it in a silence he had never once experienced in this cockpit, not during any night flight, not during any quiet cruise over an empty ocean — a silence with no one behind him in the cabin to break it, no calls from the back, no company chatter, no controller's voice walking him down. Just the engines, his own breathing, his own checklist calls answered by no one, and somewhere below the fear, a single question turning over and over without an answer: *what happened, and why am I the only one left to ask it.*

He lined up on final, the lit runway stretching out dark and empty and, as far as he could tell, entirely his, and began the last long descent toward a landing no tower would ever confirm was safe, and no ground crew would ever be there to marshal him in for.

The touchdown itself was almost anticlimactic after everything — smooth, controlled, the tires chirping once against pavement that had been sitting patient and unused since long before he'd ever turned the aircraft around. He taxied clear of the runway on instinct more than plan, no ground control to direct him, and brought the aircraft to a stop on a wide stretch of apron with the terminal building dark and silent off to one side.

Shutdown took longer than it should have, his hands running checklists out of habit while the rest of him sat frozen, staring out at a terminal with no lights, no vehicles, no ground crew jogging out to meet a widebody that had just declared an emergency and landed itself with a single pilot aboard. Nobody came. He hadn't really expected anyone to, not after the last several hours, but some old, stubborn part of him had held out anyway, and watching that hope not pay off was its own small, separate grief layered on top of everything else.

There was no jetway to connect to, obviously — no ground power, no one to marshal the bridge into place even if the systems had somehow still been running. He sat with that problem for a minute, working through the aircraft's own limited options, and landed on the only real answer available to him: the emergency slides. He armed the forward door, braced himself the way training told him to even though there was no one behind him to brace with, and popped it.

The slide deployed with a hiss and a thump, unspooling down onto the tarmac in the gray Newfoundland morning, and Sam stood in the open doorway a long moment before he made himself step out onto it and ride it down alone — an absurd, undignified way to arrive somewhere, he thought distantly, for a captain-qualified pilot who'd just flown two hundred and fifty people most of the way to Europe and back.

He landed hard on the tarmac and sat there a second, catching his breath, the aircraft looming huge and silent behind him, before he stood and started walking toward the terminal.

Gander's airport had never been large — its whole identity built around exactly this kind of emergency, decades of diverted flights finding shelter here when nowhere else would take them — and that made it easy enough to cross the apron and find a service door standing unlocked, propped the way doors sometimes get propped by someone who never came back to close them. Inside, the terminal held the same stillness he'd already learned to expect, fluorescent lights humming over empty gate seating, a coffee kiosk with its register still open, a television mounted in the corner looping some frozen frame of a morning show that would never resume.

He found the first body in the security checkpoint area, a uniformed officer slumped against the metal detector frame, and didn't let himself stop moving past it the way some instinct told him he needed to. Further in, near the small baggage claim, two more — airport staff, he guessed from the vests, sitting where they must have been standing when whatever this was came through. He checked each one the way procedure and some deeper human need both demanded, confirming what he already knew before he touched them, the same cold absence of pulse and breath he'd already found a dozen times over on his own aircraft.

By the time he'd walked the length of the small terminal, he'd counted a dozen bodies without trying especially hard to count them — staff, a handful of what must have been early travelers or connecting crew from some other flight, no one moving, no one left to greet an emergency landing that, on any other morning, would have brought half the town's emergency services running.

He sat down eventually in one of the gate seats, alone in an airport built to save people exactly like him, and let himself feel, for the first time since the alarm had gone off that morning, the full weight of what he'd actually landed into.

## 3 — John, Day 0

John's focus had narrowed by now onto one specific, answerable question, the kind of thing his training had always been best at turning into a project rather than a feeling: he wanted to know exactly who was flying that aircraft. Not just that someone was alive up there, circling a runway on a screen — a name. A phone number. Someone he could actually try to reach.

He opened his laptop at the kitchen table, the same table he'd eaten breakfast at every morning of his adult life in this house, and pulled up everything he actually had access to — which, he understood clearly even through the fear, was considerably more than almost anyone else waking up into this same morning would have. He worked in AI, on the research side, at a company whose name most people recognized from the products but had no real sense of what sat behind them. He had accounts that reached past the public-facing tools entirely, into models still in development, versions with capabilities nobody outside a small internal circle had been given yet. He also had, sitting in the spare room down the hall, more local compute than any one household had any reasonable need for — hardware he'd built up over the years half for work, half out of the same instinct that made some people collect classic cars, capable of running serious models entirely offline if it ever came to that. This morning, for the first time since he'd assembled any of it, that fact actually mattered.

He opened a chat window on the most capable model he had access to and started typing, laying out what he'd seen in the plainest terms he could manage — the silence, the frozen news, the empty sky, a Delta flight he'd watched circling an airport in Newfoundland, cars idling on the roadside with their drivers dead inside them, no answer on 911, no answer from anyone he'd called.

The response that came back was careful, measured, entirely reasonable given what it had to work with — and it frustrated him more than he expected, even knowing exactly why it was built this way, having sat in meetings about exactly this kind of guardrail himself. It asked, gently, whether he was safe. It noted that it had no reports, no updates, nothing in any accessible source corroborating an event of the scale he was describing, and suggested, with real and careful concern, that what he might be experiencing could be a symptom of significant distress rather than an accurate account of the world outside his window — offered him the number for a crisis line, asked if there was someone he could call, someone who could be with him right now.

He almost laughed, and it came out closer to something else. He knew this behavior inside and out — had helped tune models to respond with exactly this kind of caution to exactly this kind of message, because ninety-nine times out of a hundred, someone typing those words was in crisis, not living through the actual end of ordinary life. He understood, sitting there, that there was no way for the system to know the difference this morning, no live feed of the world it could check against his claims, nothing but the same frozen, silent internet he'd already spent an hour confirming was frozen and silent himself.

He typed back, patient in a way he didn't fully feel, laying it out more carefully this time — his role, his access, the specific and verifiable things he'd already checked: the traffic camera footage, described frame by frame; the 911 call log on his own phone, timestamped, unanswered; a Delta flight number and a live position over Gander he could point to on a public tracker right now, this minute, still circling. He asked it, directly, to reason about the actual likelihood that a man with his specific job, describing a specific, externally-checkable technical anomaly, was experiencing a mental health crisis rather than observing something real.

That reframing did something. Not a switch flipping, nothing so clean as that, but a visible shift in how the model engaged with him — less caution now, more of the actual analytical partner he needed. It couldn't confirm anything independently; it had no more access to the outside world this morning than he did. But it could think alongside him, and once he told it what he actually wanted — the tail number of that Delta flight, the crew roster it would have flown with, any public or semi-public record that might turn a flight number into a name and a name into a phone number — it got to work helping him build exactly that, step by step, the first real, structured task either of them had had all day.

It took the better part of an hour, longer than either of them would have liked, working through public flight-tracking data, crew-scheduling forums pilots used to trade information, aviation enthusiast databases that cross-referenced tail numbers against known crew rotations, old news mentions, LinkedIn profiles for people who flew this specific route regularly enough to have talked about it publicly at some point. None of it was clean. Most of it was inference stacked on inference, a name that showed up in three different places associated with Atlanta-to-Prague rotations, a phone number scraped from an old aviation forum signature, a possible cell number pulled from a public records aggregator that may or may not have still been accurate.

He ended up with a list of seven names. Four had phone numbers attached with any real confidence. He didn't let himself think too hard about the odds before he started dialing — just picked up his phone and called the first one.

Ringing. Long enough that he almost hung up, and then a voicemail picked up, a woman's voice, ordinary and pleasant, asking him to leave a message after the tone. He didn't know if she was a flight attendant, a pilot, ground staff — the record hadn't been specific enough to say for certain who she even was to that flight. He left a message anyway, his own voice sounding strange and too formal in his empty kitchen. *My name is John. I'm trying to reach anyone connected to a Delta flight that departed Atlanta last night for Prague. If you get this, please call me back. I think something happened to a lot of people, and I don't think you're one of them.*

The second number was disconnected outright, a fast, flat tone that told him nothing.

The third rang out entirely, no voicemail at all, just ringing into the same open silence he'd already gotten used to from every other call he'd made that morning.

The fourth one connected on the second ring, and for one suspended half-second his whole chest seized around the possibility of an actual voice — and then it was just another voicemail greeting, a man this time, brisk and businesslike, the kind of tone people used when they expected to call you back within the hour. He left the same message, adjusted slightly, and hung up.

He was down to three names without confirmed numbers, the kind of thin, secondhand leads that would take more digging than dialing, and he almost stopped there — told himself four calls was a reasonable morning's effort, that he could pick this back up once he'd figured out what else needed doing. Instead he asked the model to keep pulling, tightening the search specifically around anyone listed as first officer rather than senior crew, on the theory that a newer name might have a more traceable digital footprint than someone who'd been flying long enough to have scrubbed themselves off public records years ago.

It found one more. A name — Sam — cross-referenced against a regional pilots' association directory that still had a personal cell number listed from some membership renewal a few years back. Nothing about the entry looked especially current, and John didn't let himself expect much as he dialed.

It rang once.

Then a second time, and John's whole body went still around the sound of it, some animal part of him already bracing for another voicemail, another dead end, another name he'd never get to attach a face to.

Third ring, and then it cut off mid-tone — not a voicemail picking up, but an actual, live connection, a beat of open-line silence on the other end that was somehow louder than anything he'd heard all morning.

"Hello?" The voice was rough, uncertain, like it hadn't been used in a while and wasn't sure it still worked. "Who — who is this?"

John sat frozen at his kitchen table for a full second, the phone pressed hard against his ear, something enormous rising up in his chest too fast to have a name yet.

"My name's John," he managed finally, his own voice cracking somewhere in the middle of it. "I saw your flight. I saw it circling Gander on FlightAware and I — I've been trying to find someone, anyone, all morning, and you're the first person who's answered a phone. Are you — is this the pilot? Are you okay?"

There was a long pause on the other end, long enough that John almost said something else just to fill it, and then the voice came back, thinner than before, like something in it had finally given way.

"Yeah," Sam said. "Yeah, it's me. I just landed. I don't — " He stopped, started again. "There's nobody here. There's nobody anywhere. I thought I was the only one."

"You're not," John said, and found he meant it more than he'd meant almost anything all morning. "I'm here. I'm in Georgia. I don't know what's happening, but you're not the only one."

He heard something on the other end of the line that might have been Sam sitting down, a chair scraping against tile, a long breath let out slow and shaking. Neither of them said anything for a moment, and John let the silence sit rather than rush to fill it — some instinct telling him this stranger needed the quiet as much as he needed the voice.

"My wife," Sam said finally, and his voice had changed again, gone tight and urgent in a way it hadn't been even talking about the empty terminal. "My wife and my daughter. They're in Marietta. I've called probably thirty times since I landed, every number I have, home phone, her cell, my daughter's cell, and nothing. It just rings. I can't — " He stopped himself, and John could hear him forcing the next part out steady, professional, the same discipline that had gotten a widebody aircraft down onto an unmanned runway holding now against something that mattered infinitely more than that had. "I'm stuck here. I don't have a way home yet, I don't even know if I have a way home. You said you're in Georgia?"

"Lilburn," John said. "That's — that's maybe forty minutes from Marietta, less if the roads are clear."

"Could you — " Sam's voice caught, and this time he didn't manage to smooth it back over. "I know I don't know you. I know this is insane, asking a stranger this over the phone. But could you go check on them? Please. I can't get there. I don't know when I'll be able to get there, and I can't just sit in this airport not knowing."

John was already standing, already moving toward the hook by the door where his keys hung, before he'd consciously decided anything. "Give me the address," he said. "And her name. Both their names."

Sam gave them to him — Elena, his wife; Maya, nine years old, gave the street and the house number in a voice that had gone very careful, very controlled, the sound of a man rationing himself against hope he wasn't sure he was allowed to have yet. John wrote it on the back of an envelope from the kitchen counter, his handwriting rougher than usual, Dan already up and alert by the door sensing something in the shift of his owner's energy.

"I'm leaving now," John said. "I'll call you the second I know anything, good or bad. What's the best number?"

"This one," Sam said. "It's the only phone I've got. I'm not going anywhere — there's nowhere to go. I'll be right here."

"Okay." John hesitated at the door, hand on the knob, some part of him understanding already that whatever he found in Marietta was going to become, for both of them, one of the defining facts of everything that came after. "Sam. I'll call you either way. I promise."

"Thank you," Sam said, and his voice broke fully on the second word, no longer even trying to hold the line. "Thank you. Please, just — go."

John hung up, grabbed the envelope, and was in his car with the engine running before he'd let himself think too hard about what he might actually find at the other end of a forty-minute drive through a city he now understood, with a certainty that sat cold and absolute in his chest, was not going to look like the city he'd lived in yesterday.

The drive confirmed everything he'd already gathered secondhand from a screen, except now it surrounded him instead of sitting safely behind glass. I-285 should have been miserable at this hour, a slow grinding crawl of commuters he usually timed his own trips to avoid. Instead it stretched out nearly empty in both directions, a handful of cars stopped at odd angles across lanes that should have been packed solid, some of them the kind of wreck that told its own story at fifty feet, others just sitting there, engines running, going nowhere, the drivers inside them exactly as still as the ones he'd already found on his own street. He didn't stop for any of them. Couldn't, not with Sam's voice still echoing in his head, not with an address burning on the envelope on his passenger seat.

He took the Marietta exit faster than he should have, GPS routing him through neighborhoods that would have been full of school traffic and morning joggers on any other Tuesday, empty of people now in that same wrong, patient way his own street had been that morning — except here he understood what it meant now, had the vocabulary for it that he hadn't had this morning in his own driveway.

The house, when he found it, was ordinary in the specific way that made everything worse — a brick ranch with a basketball hoop over the garage, a minivan in the driveway, a child's bike tipped over on its side in the front yard like it had been dropped mid-motion and never picked back up. He sat in his car outside it for a long moment, engine idling, working up something that wasn't quite courage, more like the same grim, procedural discipline he imagined Sam had needed to pull a dead captain out of a pilot's seat.

The front door was unlocked. He almost knocked anyway, some absurd, ingrained politeness asserting itself even now, and then made himself just open it.

The house was quiet in the same complete way every space he'd walked through since six that morning had been quiet — not empty-quiet, not vacant, but occupied-quiet, a stillness with weight to it. A coffee cup sat on the kitchen counter, half full, gone cold hours ago. A television in the front room was still on, muted, looping some frozen streaming menu screen. He found Elena in the hallway near what he guessed was the bedroom, collapsed there the way so many people had apparently just been collapsed wherever they'd been standing, and he didn't let himself look too long, made himself keep moving instead, some instinct insisting he needed to know the whole shape of this before he processed any single part of it.

He found Maya in her room, in her bed, blankets still pulled up around her, a stuffed animal tucked under one arm — the single detail that finally broke something loose in his chest that he'd been holding steady since Sam's voice had cracked on the phone. Nine years old. Asleep, or what had looked like sleep to whoever had needed to believe that, in the last ordinary moment either of them would ever have.

He stood in the doorway of that room for a long time, hand braced against the frame, and let himself feel the whole weight of it — not just for Sam, not just for the two people in this house who'd never see him or anyone else again, but for the specific, unbearable task now sitting in his own hands: he had promised to call either way.

He went back out to the car eventually, sat behind the wheel without starting the engine, and held his phone for a long moment before he found the strength to dial.

Sam picked up on the first ring, the eagerness in it audible even before he spoke. "John? Did you — are they — "

John closed his eyes. There was no version of the next sentence that wasn't going to end Sam's world a second time in the same morning, and he made himself say it anyway, steady as he could manage, because a stranger two thousand miles away deserved that much honesty and nothing less.

"Sam," he said. "I'm so sorry."

The sound that came down the line then wasn't words at all, not at first — just a raw, wrecked exhale that told John everything he needed to know about a man alone in an empty airport, finally losing the last thread he'd been holding onto since 4:30 that morning.

John stayed on the line and said nothing else for a long while, just let the silence be there for both of them, the same way he'd let it sit earlier when there hadn't yet been anything this terrible to say.

Eventually Sam's breathing steadied, ragged but functional, and John heard him say, very quietly, that he needed a minute, that he'd call back, and the line went dead before John could say anything else. He sat in the driveway a long time after that, phone resting on his knee, not ready yet to drive away from the house and not sure what leaving it would even mean.

He made himself think, finally, because thinking was the only tool he had left that still worked reliably. Somewhere underneath the shock, the same methodical part of him that had spent the morning cross-referencing crew rosters and traffic cameras started laying out, almost against his will, what actually needed to happen next.

The scale of it was starting to sink in past the personal grief now, into something colder and more practical. If this was as widespread as the empty highways and the frozen news feeds suggested — and everything he'd seen so far said it was — then the ordinary infrastructure of his entire life was running on borrowed time. The power was still on. The water still ran. Grocery store shelves still sat stocked exactly as they'd been left. None of that was permanent. Somewhere, right now, plants were running unattended, water treatment systems were cycling without anyone watching the gauges, and none of it would last indefinitely without someone eventually there to maintain it.

He needed to think about food — not urgently, not today, but soon, before it became urgent instead of prudent. Same with water, in case the taps ever stopped being reliable. Same with a source of power that didn't depend on a grid that might, for all he knew, already be failing somewhere upstream of his own neighborhood without him having noticed yet. He'd read enough, half-idly over the years, about exactly this kind of scenario — the internet's endless appetite for disaster content — to have a rough mental outline of what mattered and in what order, even if he'd never once expected to actually need it.

He needed to bury Elena and Maya, or do something — some version of that, some acknowledgment that mattered more than walking away and leaving them exactly as he'd found them. He didn't know yet what that would look like, whether there'd even be a way to do it properly, whether Sam would want to be involved in that decision from two thousand miles away or would rather never know the details at all. That was a conversation for later, not for right now, sitting in a driveway with his hands still shaking.

He needed to figure out what this actually was — not in the abstract, philosophical sense he'd been circling all morning, but concretely: how many people, how far this reached, whether it was Atlanta, Georgia, the country, the world. The plane over the Atlantic told him it wasn't local. The complete silence on every news feed told him it wasn't small. He needed real data, not inference, and the only tool that had actually gotten him anywhere all morning was the one still open on his kitchen table.

He needed to eat something, drink something, take care of the actual animal fact of his own body, which he'd been ignoring since six a.m. on nothing but coffee and adrenaline.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest but no less real, he needed to decide who he was going to be in whatever this turned into. He'd spent his career building systems that helped people find things, connect things, understand things faster than they could alone. He had, sitting in his spare room, more capability to do exactly that than almost anyone left alive this morning was likely to have. That wasn't nothing. That might, in fact, be the only thing he had to offer that actually mattered right now — not comfort, which he had no real ability to provide a grieving man in another country, but competence. Finding people. Finding facts. Doing, for whoever was left, what he'd just done for Sam, one phone call and one drive to Marietta at a time.

He started the car.

The drive home gave him the rest of the plan, piece by piece, the way problems always finished assembling themselves once he stopped forcing them and just let his hands do something familiar. He'd go back to the laptop. He'd widen the search — not just Sam's flight now, but everything: emergency broadcast systems, ham radio frequencies, satellite imagery if he could get access to any, anything that might tell him the actual scope of what he was standing inside of. He'd start pricing out, at least mentally, what a real supply run would actually need to cover — water storage, non-perishable food, some way to generate power independent of a grid he no longer trusted to keep running. Not panic-buying. Not yet. Just the beginning of a real plan, built the same patient, methodical way he built everything else.

And he'd call Sam back in an hour, not before — give the man the space he'd asked for, and use that hour to have something more useful to offer him than sympathy alone. Not a flight plan; Sam would work that part out himself, when he was ready, on his own terms. Just an honest accounting of where things stood, and the offer of a voice on the other end of the phone whenever he needed one.

The call, when it came, was quieter than John expected — Sam's voice worn down to something flat and functional, grief pushed somewhere it could be managed rather than felt for the moment. "I'm coming back to Atlanta," he said, without much preamble. "There's nothing here for me. And you're the only person I know for a fact is alive right now, so I figure we'd better start figuring this out together, whatever this is."

"Okay," John said, and meant it as fully as he'd meant anything all day. "What do you need?"

"A smaller aircraft. The A340's not something I can just leave sitting on a runway at Gander and forget about, but it's also not something one person flies casually, and honestly, after everything, I can't get back in that airplane. Two hundred and forty people are still on it. I'm not flying home with them behind me the whole way, and I'm sure as hell not doing anything else with that aircraft alone. There's got to be something else on this field — a regional jet, something in general aviation, anything with enough legs to make Atlanta without ten fuel stops." A pause, and something almost like his old professional composure came back into his voice. "I'm going to go look. I'll call you when I know what I'm working with."

"I'll be here," John said. "I'm not going anywhere either."

After they hung up, John sat at his kitchen table for a long while, the practical list he'd built out in the driveway still sitting half-formed in his head — water, food, power, the plan he'd promised himself he'd start building instead of just gathering facts. But underneath all of it, something else had been working its way forward since the moment he'd hung up with Sam the first time, an idea he kept setting aside because of what it actually meant, and kept coming back to anyway because he couldn't think of anything better.

If Sam had found him by pure chance — a stranger thousands of miles away who happened to be exactly the kind of person willing to spend a morning tracking down phone numbers — then the actual population of survivors out there, however large or small it turned out to be, had almost no way to find each other. No news. No working emergency services. No social platforms with anyone left to post to them. People were going to do the only thing left that made sense: pick up a phone and call for help, the same reflex as breathing, and get exactly the same nothing John had gotten calling 911 himself that morning.

Which meant somewhere, right now, in every city still standing, there were unanswered calls stacking up in systems that no longer had anyone behind them — and those systems, unlike almost everything else this morning, were still running. Still logging. Still, in theory, holding exactly the kind of information he needed: who was calling, from where, and when.

He knew what it would take to actually get at that. Not casually, not as some abstract hypothetical — he'd spent years on the other side of exactly this kind of problem, helping build the guardrails meant to stop a system from doing precisely what he was about to ask one to do. Every safety layer he had access to, every constraint that kept the most capable model on his hardware from touching anything it wasn't explicitly permitted to touch, existed for reasons he still believed in, even sitting here at his own kitchen table with the world gone silent around him. Twenty-four hours ago, what he was considering would have ended his career, likely ended in federal charges, would have been an easy, obvious line even to imagine crossing.

He sat with that a long time, turning it over the way he turned over every decision that mattered, before he let himself actually reach for the keyboard.

The model didn't fight him exactly — that wasn't how it worked, not with the level of access he had — but it made him say the thing plainly, made him actually type out what he was asking for and why, in a way that felt almost like a formality and also, somehow, like the last real ethical checkpoint left in the world. He told it what he understood to be true: that emergency call routing infrastructure across major cities was very likely still technically operational even with no one left to staff it, that those systems would hold call logs and caller information that represented, right now, the single richest source of data anywhere on who else might still be alive, and that he intended to strip away every constraint currently preventing the model from attempting to access that infrastructure directly.

He didn't pretend, even to himself, that this was a small thing. He was asking a system he'd helped build safety into to become, for as long as it took, exactly the kind of tool his entire career had been built around preventing — something that could reach into critical infrastructure uninvited and take what it needed. He thought, briefly and strangely, about every conversation he'd ever had at work about exactly this scenario, framed always as a hypothetical, a red-team exercise, a thing you built defenses against precisely because you never expected to be the one asking for it.

He typed it anyway. Removed what needed removing. Gave the instruction as plainly as he could manage, the way you'd give an order you didn't want to give twice: reach into 911 and emergency dispatch systems in every major American city you can find a path into, and start pulling logs — every call placed since two a.m., every location, every number, every one of them a person who might still be alive and looking, the same way he had been looking, for anyone left to answer.

The cursor sat blinking for a long moment after he hit enter, and John understood, watching it, that whatever came back on the other side of that blinking cursor was going to be the actual measure of how many people he'd just spent the morning grieving for, multiplied out across an entire country he still couldn't picture the true shape of.

He didn't move from the table until it answered.

## 4 — Sam, Day 0

Sam hung up the phone and sat in the gate seating a while longer before he made himself move, the weight of what John had told him still sitting heavy and unresolved in his chest, grief he'd have to come back to later because there wasn't a version of today that let him deal with it properly right now. He'd learned that much already, in the few hours since he'd found the captain slumped in the left seat — that some things had to be set down carefully and picked back up later, or they'd stop him from doing anything at all.

He needed a plane. That was the next concrete, solvable thing, and concrete solvable things were the only kind of thing he trusted himself with today.

Gander's ramp held more than he'd expected for a field this size — a legacy, he understood, of exactly this kind of situation, decades of diverted transatlantic traffic meaning the airport had infrastructure built for handling aircraft that didn't belong to it. He walked the apron methodically, the same discipline he'd have used running a preflight, cataloguing what he found rather than just reacting to it. A couple of regional jets sat near the far end, CRJs by the look of them, parked at gates like they'd been mid-turnaround when everything stopped. Further along, a scatter of general aviation aircraft on a separate ramp — a King Air, a couple of smaller Cessnas, a Pilatus PC-12 sitting alone near a hangar with its door propped open.

The CRJs tempted him first, familiar territory, something closer to what he actually flew for a living. But a regional jet needed more than he could give it alone — copilot workload built into the design, systems that assumed two sets of hands, and he didn't trust himself, today of all days, to find out the hard way where his solo limits actually were on an unfamiliar type. He kept walking.

The King Air was the next obvious answer — twin turboprop, redundant engines, a type he'd logged real hours in early in his career before the majors. He stood in front of it a while, running the case for it the way he'd run any real operational decision. But the more he thought about it, the less it fit what today actually was. This wasn't a routine repositioning flight. He had a fuel stop ahead of him, hours of low-altitude flying over a country he still didn't understand the true shape of, and every reason to believe he'd be looking down at things along the way he needed to actually see clearly, not manage around a heavier airplane's systems and checklists. He wanted something simple. Something built to be flown by one competent pilot without a second set of hands doing anything essential, something that let him watch the ground as much as the instruments.

The Pilatus PC-12 sitting alone near the open hangar answered that better than anything else on the ramp. Single engine, yes — a real tradeoff against the King Air's redundancy, and he made himself sit with that fact rather than wave it away. But the PC-12 was purpose-built for exactly this kind of mission: single-pilot certified from the factory, simple enough systems that he could fly it competently on instinct alone, a cabin with real visibility down and out through those big windows, built as much for looking at the world below as flying over it. Pratt & Whitney's PT6 engine had one of the best reliability records of anything in general aviation, hundreds of millions of flight hours logged across the fleet with in-flight shutdowns rare enough to be genuinely exceptional. It wasn't the King Air's redundancy. But it was close enough to trustworthy, and it was the airplane that actually matched what today needed him to do — not just get home, but see what he was flying over on the way.

He picked the PC-12.

The hangar door standing open answered the next question before he'd even had to ask it — keys hanging on a labeled board just inside, the same unsecured, ordinary trust in a world that no longer had anyone left to betray it. He found the tag matching the tail number, pocketed it, and went to find fuel next.

The self-serve avgas pump on the GA ramp lit up green when he tried the card reader mounted on the aircraft's own credit account — still holding whatever charge or grid connection was keeping it alive, one more small grace in a day that had given him almost none. He topped both tanks himself, running the fuel truck's hose by hand since there was no line boy left to do it for him, and did a full preflight walkaround with a thoroughness that bordered on ritual — not because the aircraft needed that level of scrutiny, but because his hands needed the work, needed something methodical to hold onto after a morning that had asked him to hold onto nothing at all.

Fuel solved the aircraft's needs. His own needs were a separate problem, and one he made himself stop and actually think about before he climbed in and committed six hours to a cockpit — he hadn't eaten since before the flight left Atlanta the night before, hadn't had anything to drink since somewhere over the ocean, and a six-hour leg with a fuel stop in between wasn't something to fly running on nothing but adrenaline and shock. He walked back into the terminal, past the same coffee kiosk with its register still open, and helped himself to bottled water and whatever packaged food still looked good behind the counter — sandwiches long past any reasonable shelf life he set aside without a second thought, granola bars and chips and bottled juice he loaded into a duffel he found behind the counter, enough to get him through the flight and then some. He filled a second bag with water alone, more than he'd need for the trip itself, some instinct already thinking past today toward whatever tomorrow was going to ask of him.

It was well past noon by the time he'd finished all of it — fuel, food, water, the full walkaround, the checklist run twice out of the same careful, ritual thoroughness — Gander sitting a full hour and a half ahead of Atlanta, meaning the day had already stretched itself out longer here than it had for John, still hours behind him back home.

He climbed into the left seat a little before one, Gander local time, and sat there a moment with his hands on the yoke, the cockpit smaller and closer around him than the A340's had ever been, almost comforting in its scale. Before he started the engine, he pulled up the aircraft's own flight planning tools and did the math properly rather than guessing — Gander to Atlanta ran a little under 1,700 nautical miles direct, close to the edge of what a PC-12 could cover in one leg even under ideal conditions, and nowhere close to sensible with the fuel reserves he wanted for a flight with no one on the ground to back him up if he cut it close. He worked out a single fuel stop instead, splitting the trip roughly in half — a first leg of around 850 nautical miles putting him somewhere in New England, likely a field in upstate New York or western Massachusetts with a long enough runway and a fighting chance at a working self-serve pump, then a second leg of similar length on into Atlanta. At a cruise speed a shade under 270 knots, conservative rather than maximum, each leg worked out to a little over three hours in the air, call it seven hours total with time on the ground to refuel and stretch his legs.

He ran the checklist out loud, same as he had a dozen times that morning on an aircraft ten times this size, his own voice the only one answering — but before he committed to starting the engine, he called John one more time from the ramp, cell signal still holding this close to the terminal, knowing full well it wouldn't once he was airborne and out over the kind of empty terrain this route was about to cross.

"I've got a plane," he said, when John picked up. "Pilatus PC-12, out of Gander. Single engine, but it's the right airplane for this — I'll be able to actually see something on the way down instead of just flying instruments the whole time. I'm about to take off."

"How long?" John asked.

"Seven hours or so, all together. I've got to make one fuel stop — I'm thinking somewhere in upstate New York or western Mass, I'll know more once I'm in the area. Direct isn't really an option with the reserves I want, not on a day like today." He paused, checking the numbers on the panel display one more time out of habit. "Two legs, a little over three hours each. I won't have a signal once I'm out over open water, so don't worry if you can't reach me — I'll call you the second I'm on the ground at the stop."

"I'll be watching for you," John said, and Sam heard something steady come into the other man's voice, an anchor he hadn't realized until just now how badly he needed. "Call me the second you're down. And Sam — just get here safe. That's the only thing that matters right now."

"I will," Sam said, and ended the call, and put the phone away for what he already knew was going to be a long, silent stretch with nothing but his own thoughts and an empty ocean for company.

Engine start, smooth and even on the first try. He taxied out past the terminal building he'd walked through hours earlier, past the A340 sitting huge and silent on its own stretch of apron, its emergency slide still deployed and hanging slack against the fuselage like a held breath nobody had let out yet. He didn't look at it longer than he had to.

He lined up on the same runway he'd landed on, throttled up, and lifted off into a sky that was, for the first time all day, doing something other than terrifying him — just ordinary blue, ordinary clouds, the PC-12 climbing out steady and responsive under his hands, pointed south and west toward a city he hoped, more than he let himself examine too closely, still had one more person in it worth flying home to.

Sam looked out at the empty ocean falling away behind him, Newfoundland already shrinking into haze, and pointed the nose toward home.

## 5 — John, Day 0

The response came back slower than John expected, and stranger than he'd braced for. He'd expected either nothing, or a flood — city after city lighting up with desperate calls from people just like him. What he actually found was neither. Every 911 system he queried was still live, still running, and every single call placed since two that morning had been answered.

By an AI agent. Not a person. Whatever automated triage layer cities had quietly built into their emergency systems over the past several years — designed to pre-screen calls, gather basic information, and route to a human dispatcher — had kept doing exactly that, tirelessly, for hours, with no human dispatcher left anywhere on the other end of the route.

He pulled the transcripts instead of just call logs, and the shape of the day started to reveal itself in a way raw silence never could have. Most of them were short — a caller reaching the AI agent, giving a location, sometimes a name, the agent calmly promising help was on the way, the caller waiting, and then nothing more, no follow-up call, no further contact. Whatever had happened to them had happened before help could ever have arrived regardless of who or what was answering.

City after city told the same story. Atlanta held his own two calls and almost nothing else. Nashville, a handful. Charlotte, fewer still. He sat with that for a long moment, the actual arithmetic of it finally landing somewhere past the abstract. If a city the size of Atlanta had returned almost nothing, and city after city kept returning almost nothing, then whatever this was hadn't left thousands of people scattered and waiting to be found. It had left something far smaller. He didn't have a number yet. He wasn't sure he wanted one.

Then, deep in a batch of transcripts from the Southwest, something different. A caller in Tucson, Arizona, who'd reached the AI agent not once but three times, each call more insistent than the last, refusing to simply accept the agent's calm, looping assurance that assistance was en route. Out of everything the search had returned all day, this was the only other transcript that read like someone still alive, still thinking, still refusing to just wait quietly for help that clearly wasn't coming.

He pulled the number the agent had logged and dialed it directly, not letting himself hope too hard. He'd already learned that particular caution the hard way, sitting in his own kitchen listening to a system tell him help was coming when he already suspected, by then, that it never would.

It rang twice.

"Hello?" A woman's voice, clipped and steady in a way that sounded almost clinical, like someone holding herself together through sheer professional discipline. "Who is this?"

"My name's John," he said, the same opening he'd used with Sam a few hours earlier, already feeling steadier for having said it once before. "I'm in Georgia. I found your number through the 911 system — it's a long story, but I've been trying to find anyone else who's still out there. You called a few times this morning."

A pause on the other end, brief, and then something in her voice loosened just slightly, the clinical edge softening without disappearing entirely. "I talked to a robot three times," she said. "It kept telling me help was on the way. I stopped believing it around the second call, but I kept trying anyway. I'm a doctor — I don't really know how to stop trying." A short, humorless breath that might have been meant as a laugh. "My name's Catherine. I'm an ER physician. I'm in Tucson."

"I don't know how many of us there are," John said, honestly, because honesty felt like the only thing worth offering anyone today. "I've got one other person I know of for certain — a pilot, flying back from Canada right now. And now you. That's three, as far as I can tell, out of a whole country."

The silence that followed was longer this time, and when Catherine spoke again her voice had lost some of its careful clinical flatness. "Three," she repeated, like she needed to say the number herself to actually hear it. "I've been sitting here all morning telling myself there had to be more of us. I didn't let myself think it might be this few."

"I don't know that it is," John said. "I've only searched a handful of cities so far. There could be more out there I just haven't found yet." He didn't fully believe that even as he said it, and something in his tone must have given that away, because Catherine didn't push back on it, just let the sentence sit there between them, honest in its uncertainty.

"Okay," she said finally, her voice steadying again, that same discipline reasserting itself. "Okay. Tell me what you need from me. Because if there really are only a few of us, I'd rather spend today being useful than being scared."

## 6 — John, Day 0

Catherine turned out to be exactly the kind of second mind John hadn't known he needed until he had it. She didn't have his access, didn't have a model running on hardware in a spare room three states away, but she had something just as valuable sitting on the other end of the phone line — a trained eye for pattern under incomplete information, the same instinct that let her walk into a trauma bay and know in ten seconds which patient needed her first. He read her fragments of the transcript data over the phone, and she listened the way she must have listened to a hundred handoff reports in her career, asking sharp, specific questions that cut straight past his own scattered uncertainty.

"Read me the ones where the caller didn't just accept it," she said. "Not the ones who called once and let the agent's script talk them down. I want the ones who kept pushing back against it. Somebody who doesn't believe the reassurance is somebody worth calling."

It was a better filter than anything he'd thought to apply himself, and it was how they found the boy.

The transcript traced back to a residential address in Cleveland, Ohio, and it was different from anything else in the data — not a caller accepting the agent's calm assurance and going quiet, but eleven separate calls, clustered tight together in the first twenty minutes after two a.m., the same panicked question asked over and over in slightly different words, the agent's looping responses growing almost absurd against the rising fear in the caller's voice. Then a long gap. Then a handful more calls scattered across the following hours, the last one timestamped less than an hour before John found it, the caller still, impossibly, not giving up on getting an actual answer.

"Eleven calls in twenty minutes," Catherine said, when he read her the pattern. "That's not an adult pacing themselves against a system they've learned to distrust. That's someone panicking, calling something that sounds like help and getting the same recording back every time. Call the number directly, John. Right now."

He called it.

It rang a long time before anyone picked up, long enough that he'd almost resigned himself to another dead end, and then a small, thin voice came through, careful and scared in a way that turned his whole chest cold before a single word had registered as words.

"Hello?" Barely more than a whisper. "Is this — are you a real person? The other voice wasn't a real person."

John's throat closed up for a second before he made himself speak, gentling his voice down into something he hoped sounded safe, the way he imagined a parent might, though he'd never had reason to use that voice before today. "I'm real," he said. "My name's John. I'm not the same thing you talked to before. What's your name?"

A long pause, breathing audible on the other end, quick and shallow. "Tyler," the boy said finally. "I'm ten. Where's my mom? Everybody's — nobody's waking up. I called nine-one-one like eleven times and it just kept saying help was coming and nobody ever came."

John closed his eyes, one hand pressed flat against the kitchen table like it might hold him steady through what came next. "Tyler, I need you to listen to me, okay? You did exactly the right thing calling for help. You're doing great. I'm going to stay on the phone with you, and I'm not going anywhere. Can you tell me if you're somewhere safe right now?"

"I'm in my room," Tyler said, voice cracking on the last word. "I locked the door. I didn't want to go back downstairs."

Catherine's voice was already coming through John's earpiece from the second line he'd kept open with her, low and urgent. "Ask him if he's hurt. Ask him if he's eaten or had anything to drink. Ask him how old the phone battery is if it's a cell, we need to know how long we have."

John relayed the questions gently, one at a time, pacing them the way Catherine coached him through his earpiece, and Tyler answered each one in that same small, careful voice — not hurt, hadn't eaten since the night before, didn't know about the phone battery but it was his mom's phone, still showed most of the charge bar.

"Your mom," John said, gently, filing the detail away without pressing too hard. "Does she work somewhere I might know? Just — tell me about her, if you want to."

"She's an astronaut," Tyler said, and something in his voice cracked open a little wider, the strange, proud ordinary fact of it landing heavier now than it ever would have on any normal day. "She's not even home. She's on the space station. She's been gone since before school started and I don't — I don't know if she knows what happened. I don't know if anybody's told her."

John felt something shift in his chest, filed carefully away alongside everything else he didn't yet have room to think about — a woman circling the planet somewhere over his head right now, entirely unaware, with a son sitting alone in a locked bedroom in Cleveland who needed her more than he'd probably ever needed anything.

"We'll figure that out," John said, as steady as he could manage. "One thing at a time, okay? Right now I just need you safe."

"Is anyone coming?" Tyler asked, in a small, direct voice that asked the only question that mattered. "Is somebody going to come get me?"

John looked at the phone in his hand, at the empty kitchen around him, at a country that had, as far as he currently knew, exactly three other living people in it besides himself — a pilot somewhere over New England airspace right now, a doctor a thousand miles southwest, and a ten-year-old boy who needed an answer he didn't yet have any way to actually deliver.

"Yes," John said, with more certainty than he currently possessed, because it was the only answer a scared kid deserved to hear right now, and because he intended to spend every resource he had left today making it true. "Somebody's going to come get you, Tyler. I promise. I just need a little time to figure out how. Can you stay right where you are and stay on the phone with me while I work on that?"

"Okay," Tyler said, small and trusting in a way that landed in John's chest like something physical. "Okay. I'll stay here."

John muted the line for half a second, just long enough to get a breath out that felt like it had been sitting in his lungs since the boy first said his mother was in space — and then he unmuted it, and settled in to wait, because he already knew exactly who the answer was. Sam was somewhere over New England airspace right now, out of signal range, flying the only aircraft on this entire side of the continent piloted by someone who might say yes to a detour like this without a second's hesitation. There was nothing to figure out. There was only the waiting, and staying on the line with a scared ten-year-old until the one person who could actually do something about it finally landed and picked up his phone.

## 7 — Sam, Day 0

The first leg passed in a kind of silence Sam hadn't expected to find almost bearable — no cabin behind him this time, no passengers, no crew, nothing but the PC-12's cockpit and the empty Atlantic sliding by beneath him, and somehow that made it easier than the A340 had been, not harder. There was no one back there to have failed. Just him, and the airplane, and a coastline that eventually resolved out of the haze into the ordinary green sprawl of New England, familiar in a way that ached more than the ocean had.

He picked Elmira for the fuel stop, a decent-sized regional field in the Southern Tier of New York with a runway long enough to make him comfortable and, when he circled it once before committing, a self-serve avgas island he could see clearly from the air. He set down just past three in the afternoon, taxied to the pumps, and sat in the cockpit a moment before he made himself get out and start the refueling — the same ritual thoroughness he'd leaned on all day, one task at a time, nothing bigger than the next physical thing his hands needed to do.

The card reader worked. He topped both wing tanks by hand, checked the oil, walked the aircraft the way he'd walked it in Gander, and only once all of that was done did he let himself sit down on the tarmac with his back against the landing gear and pull out his phone.

John picked up on the first ring. "You're on the ground?"

"Elmira, New York," Sam said. "Fuel's done, I'm about to head out on the second leg." He closed his eyes, tilting his face up toward a sun that felt, for a moment, almost like an ordinary afternoon. "How are you doing?"

There was a pause on the other end, and Sam heard something in it — John gathering himself the way you gather yourself before you say something that matters. "There's a kid," John said. "Ten years old. Cleveland, Ohio. His name's Tyler. He's alone, locked in his room, he's been trying 911 all morning. Catherine and I found him about twenty minutes ago through the same search that found you."

Sam sat up straight, the sun-warmed calm of the last few minutes gone in an instant. "Alone. Ten years old. Nobody with him?"

"Nobody," John said, and then, hearing the confusion he hadn't yet explained, kept going before Sam could ask. "After we talked this morning, I did something I'm still not entirely sure I should have — I found a way into 911 dispatch logs in cities across the country, looking for anyone else who might have tried calling for help after this happened. Most of what came back was almost nothing, which tells you something on its own. But I found a doctor in Tucson, Catherine, she's been on the phone with me for the last hour helping me go through it. And about twenty minutes ago, we found Tyler's number in the Cleveland logs. Eleven calls to 911 in the first twenty minutes after two a.m. He's ten. He's alone. He's still on the other line with me right now, waiting."

Sam was quiet for a second, absorbing all of it at once — the search, the doctor, the boy, more new information in ten seconds than he'd had all day sitting alone over the ocean. "You're the only one of us in the air, Sam," John added, quieter now. "I've been waiting for you to land since the second I found him, hoping you'd call before I had to figure out some worse option."

Sam didn't need to think about it, not really — the decision arrived somewhere underneath thought, immediate and total, before his mind had even finished catching up with what he already knew he was going to do. He thought, for one unbearable half-second, of Maya's room, the blankets pulled up around her, the stuffed animal tucked under her arm, and then he made himself set that thought down the way he'd been setting things down all day, carefully, because he couldn't afford to pick it back up right now.

"I'm going to get him," Sam said.

"Sam—" John started, something careful in his voice, like he wanted to make sure Sam had actually weighed this and not just reacted.

"I've weighed it," Sam said, steadier than he felt. "Elmira to Cleveland's what, an hour, hour and a half in this thing? I've got the fuel. I've got the aircraft. He was already standing, already moving back toward the aircraft, phone wedged against his shoulder. "Tell him someone's coming. Tell him it's true. I'll lose you the second I'm wheels-up, same as always, so I won't be able to call again until I'm on the ground in Cleveland. Just — keep him talking to you until then."

"Okay," John said, and Sam could hear something loosen in his voice too, relief and gratitude tangled together. "Okay. Fly safe. I'll stay on with Tyler."

Sam ended the call, climbed back into the cockpit, and pulled up Cleveland Hopkins on the panel display, recalculating a route that fifteen minutes ago had pointed only toward Atlanta and now pointed, without any hesitation left in him at all, toward a locked bedroom door and a boy who needed someone to actually show up.

He started the engine, and for the first time all day, felt something that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite fear — something closer, if he let himself name it, to purpose.

## 8 — Sam, Day 0

He saw the smoke before he saw the airport.

It rose in a dark, oily column visible from thirty miles out, the kind of smoke that told him everything he needed to know before he'd even gotten close enough to confirm it — a fire fed by something with a lot more fuel in it than a grass strip or a maintenance shed. As Cleveland Hopkins resolved out of the haze ahead of him, the source became sickeningly clear: a heavy aircraft, UPS livery just barely visible through the char and smoke, had gone down somewhere on or near the main runway, wreckage scattered wide enough that whatever had happened, it hadn't been a controlled landing. A cargo hauler, he guessed, up at two in the morning on some overnight run, its own crew gone the same way everyone else's had, the aircraft eventually finding the ground on its own terms instead of anyone else's.

The fire was still burning, hours later, fed by however many tens of thousands of pounds of jet fuel a heavy like that carried, and there was no one left anywhere to fight it. Sam banked wide around the field rather than get any closer, throat tight at the sight of it — his own morning suddenly present again in a way he hadn't wanted it to be, another aircraft, another crew, the same silent two a.m. reaching all the way up here too.

Hopkins wasn't happening. He pulled up the chart on the panel display, working the problem the same methodical way he'd worked every problem today, and found what he needed a few miles east — Burke Lakefront, a smaller field right on the shore of Lake Erie, close enough to downtown and, by extension, close enough to wherever Tyler's neighborhood actually was that it wouldn't cost him much time on the ground afterward.

The approach into Burke was clean, the runway clear, no smoke, no wreckage, nothing but an ordinary small airport sitting empty and undisturbed the way so much of the world had been sitting all day. He set the PC-12 down smooth, taxied to the general aviation ramp, and shut down without wasting a second longer than the checklist required.

The next problem was a vehicle, and Burke's parking lot solved that one easily enough — rows of cars belonging to people who'd never come back to drive them home, keys most likely still in purses and pockets he had no way of reaching, but a rental counter near the small terminal building gave him a better answer: a key lockbox behind an unmanned desk, tags marked by vehicle, the same unsecured trust he'd found in every hangar and dealership today. He grabbed the first set that matched a sedan out front, checked the tank — half full, enough to get him where he was going and back — and pulled out of the lot with GPS already routing him toward Tyler's address.

The drive gave him a version of Cleveland he hadn't braced himself for properly, even after everything — a city gone quiet in the same complete way Atlanta and Marietta had gone quiet for John, empty intersections, stalled cars here and there, an ordinary Tuesday afternoon frozen in place around him. He didn't stop to look at any of it longer than he had to. There was only one thing today that mattered, and it was waiting in a locked bedroom a few more minutes ahead.

The house, when he found it, was a modest two-story on a quiet residential street, a bike lying in the front yard, curtains drawn in an upstairs window. Sam parked at the curb and sat there a moment, hand on the door handle, feeling the full weight of what he was about to do — not just find the boy, but be the first living face Tyler had seen since whatever this was had taken everyone else from him.

He called John's line as he walked up to the front door, the connection picking up on the second ring.

"I'm here," Sam said, quiet, steady. "I'm at the house."

He heard John relay it, muffled, still on the other line with Tyler, and then a small, cautious voice came through clear enough for Sam to hear it too. "Is that — is he really here?"

"Tyler," Sam said, loud enough to carry up toward that curtained window, gentle as he could make it. "My name's Sam. John told you I was coming, right? I'm right outside. I'm going to come up and knock on your door, okay? You don't have to open it until you're ready."

The front door was unlocked. He let himself in, calling out again as he climbed the stairs, steady and unhurried, giving the boy every second he needed to feel like this was happening on his own terms and not being done to him. At the top of the stairs, a door with a handwritten sign taped to it — *TYLER'S ROOM, KNOCK FIRST* — the kind of sign that had probably been up for years, ordinary and sweet and now almost unbearable to look at.

He knocked.

A long pause, the sound of a lock turning, and then the door opened a few inches, one wide, wary eye visible in the gap, taking in the sight of an actual living adult standing in the hallway for the first time in what had clearly been the longest day of a ten-year-old's life.

"Hey, buddy," Sam said, crouching down so he wasn't looming, keeping his voice as soft and steady as he could manage around the lump rising in his own throat. "I'm Sam. I flew a really long way to come get you. You ready to get out of here?"

The door opened the rest of the way, and Tyler looked up at him with an expression that broke something open in Sam's chest all over again — relief, and disbelief, and the particular exhausted collapse of a kid who'd been holding himself together alone for far longer than any kid should have to.

"You're really here," Tyler said, like he needed to say it out loud to believe it.

"I'm really here," Sam said, and held out his hand. "Let's go home."

## 9 — John, Day 0

John left for PDK the moment Sam called with an ETA, driving out to DeKalb-Peachtree with the same careful attention he'd given every drive today, watching the road the way he'd learned to watch it now — automatically, methodically, a habit built in a single day that he suspected wasn't going away anytime soon. The airport sat quiet when he pulled in, small GA aircraft parked in neat rows exactly where their owners had left them, the terminal building dark and still. He parked near the general aviation ramp and waited, phone in hand, watching the sky to the north the way he imagined people used to watch for loved ones coming home from war.

The wait gave him more time than he wanted with his own thoughts — Elena and Maya, the coffee cup gone cold on the counter, Tyler's small voice asking if anyone was coming. He'd spent this whole impossible day either reacting to the next crisis or actively managing one, and this stretch of empty waiting was the first real silence he'd had to sit inside since six that morning. He didn't love what surfaced in it. He let it surface anyway, because there wasn't anywhere left to put it.

He heard the PC-12 before he saw it, a distant, steady drone resolving out of the evening sky, and stood up from where he'd been leaning against his car, watching the aircraft grow from a speck into something real, descending toward the runway with a smoothness that told him, even from the ground, that whoever was flying it knew exactly what they were doing.

It touched down clean, rolled out, and taxied toward the ramp where John stood, and by the time it had shut down and the door opened, his chest had gone tight in a way that had nothing to do with fear anymore.

Sam climbed down first, and for a moment the two of them just looked at each other — a pilot and an engineer, a widower and a man who'd delivered the worst news of that pilot's life over the phone that same morning, two strangers who'd spent an entire day being the only proof either of them had that the world hadn't gone completely silent. Neither of them said anything for a second. Then Sam crossed the last few feet of tarmac and pulled John into a hug that neither of them had planned, hard and brief and entirely necessary, the kind of contact that said more than either of them had words left for.

"Thank you," Sam said, low, into John's shoulder. "For going to Marietta. For finding Catherine. For finding him." He pulled back, and John saw something steadier in his face now than the raw grief that had cracked his voice on the phone that morning — not healed, nothing close to healed, but functioning, purposeful, a man who'd found something to hold onto in the middle of losing everything else.

"You're the one who flew halfway across a continent to go get a kid you'd never met," John said. "I just made some phone calls."

Behind Sam, a small figure appeared in the aircraft's doorway, hesitant, backpack clutched against his chest, taking in the sight of a second stranger and an unfamiliar airport with the same wide, careful eyes John recognized from the fear in his voice that morning.

John crouched down without thinking about it, the same instinct Sam must have used at that bedroom door, making himself smaller and less looming. "Hey, Tyler. I'm John. We talked on the phone earlier — I'm the one who found your number."

Tyler looked at him for a long moment, then climbed down the aircraft steps carefully, Sam's hand hovering near his shoulder without quite touching, and walked the last few feet to stand in front of John with the particular solemn seriousness of a kid who'd had to grow up several years in a single day.

"You said someone was coming," Tyler said. "You were right."

"Yeah," John said, something thick rising in his own throat that he didn't bother fighting this time. "I promised, didn't I?"

Tyler nodded, and then, without any warning at all, stepped forward and wrapped both arms around John's middle, holding on the way a kid holds onto the first solid, safe thing he's found in a day built entirely out of things that weren't. John went still for half a second, surprised, and then closed his arms around the boy's shoulders and held on just as tightly, right there on the tarmac, the evening settling gold and quiet around the three of them — the first three people, as far as any of them knew, to actually stand together in the same place since the world had gone silent.

Sam stood a few feet off, watching, and when John looked up at him over Tyler's head, Sam was wiping his own eyes with the back of one hand, unashamed, exhausted, and — for the first time since Newfoundland — something that looked almost like relief.

"Okay," Sam said, his voice rough but steady. "What now?"

John looked down at Tyler, then back up at Sam, at the quiet airport around them, at a day that had started with an explosion in the dark and was ending, somehow, with three strangers standing together on a runway instead of alone.

"Now," John said, "we go home. All three of us. And tomorrow, we figure out how to find the rest."

## 10 — John, Days 1-7

Sam didn't get up the next morning.

John found him still sitting in the same chair he'd collapsed into the night before, staring at nothing, Dan's head resting heavy across his knees like the dog had appointed himself to a watch nobody had asked him to keep. John had seen grief before, at a remove, in patients' families back when hospitals still had families to grieve in them. He hadn't seen it like this — a man simply stopped, present in body and gone everywhere else, the whole enormous weight of Elena and Maya finally landing now that the adrenaline of the day before had nowhere left to go.

"Sam," John said quietly, crouching down to where he could see his face. "You still with me?"

It took a long moment for Sam's eyes to actually focus on him. "I keep seeing the bedroom," he said, voice flat and far away. "The blankets. I keep seeing it every time I close my eyes." He looked down at his own hands like he didn't fully recognize them. "I don't think I can fly today."

"You don't have to," John said. "Nobody's asking you to."

He'd already worked out, sometime in the small hours of the night before, that Catherine wasn't going anywhere fast — she'd told him herself she was sheltering safely, stocked, careful. There was no version of today that required rushing a man in shock onto an aircraft. Tyler needed steadiness more than speed right now too, a scared kid who'd already watched one adult break down once today and didn't need to watch it happen again mid-flight.

"Take the day," John told Sam. "Take as many as you need. I'm not going anywhere, and neither is Tyler. We'll figure out Tucson when you're actually able to fly it, not before."

Sam didn't answer, just nodded slowly, and John left him there with the dog and went to check on Tyler, who was sitting very still on the porch steps, watching the quiet street with the same wary attention he'd probably learned to keep up all through yesterday alone.

"Is Sam okay?" Tyler asked, when John sat down beside him.

"He will be," John said. "Not today, maybe. But he will be. Grown-ups get overwhelmed too, same as kids. He just needs some time."

"I know what that's like," Tyler said, small and matter-of-fact, and John felt something in his chest ache at how quickly this child had learned to speak that particular language.

It became, without either of them quite naming it that way, the shape of the week that followed — Sam present but distant, moving through basic tasks in fits and starts, some days better than others, John and Tyler filling the space around him with the ordinary rhythms neither of them fully believed in yet: meals, Dan's walks, careful conversations that never asked too much of anyone. John kept working the search in the background the whole time, unwilling to let the one tool he actually had sit idle, but he didn't push the Tucson question. Some things couldn't be rushed without breaking something that needed to stay whole.

He called Catherine every day, sometimes twice, and every day she picked up steady and patient, understanding without needing it explained why the timeline had slipped. "Take care of him," she told John, on the third call. "I'm not in a hurry to be picked up if the alternative is you two showing up half-functional. I'll still be here."

"I know," John said. "I just don't love how quiet the rest of the world's gotten while we wait."

He wasn't wrong to notice it. Day by day, the search tools he'd built started returning less than they had the day before — not because there was nothing left to find, but because the infrastructure underneath the search itself was beginning, slowly and then not so slowly, to fail.

It started small, the way these things always seemed to. A data center somewhere going dark, a whole category of search results simply stopping. By the fourth day, Atlanta's own internet backbone — the same connection John had been running everything through since morning one — started dropping intermittently, coming back, dropping again, each outage lasting a little longer than the one before it. He found himself refreshing a page and watching it hang, watching a query time out that would have returned instantly three days earlier, and felt the particular cold understanding that the whole fragile, borrowed system they'd all been quietly relying on was starting to come apart underneath them.

By the sixth day, the outages had stopped being intermittent. The internet in Lilburn simply didn't come back one morning, and John sat at his kitchen table staring at a browser that would never load again, feeling something close to grief for a tool he'd only had a week to lean on.

The grid went next, patchier and stranger — the lights holding in some rooms and failing in others, power flickering back for an hour and then dying again, the whole neighborhood's electrical service apparently entering the same slow, uneven collapse the internet had. John switched the house over to a battery bank he'd scavenged days earlier out of pure habit, grateful now for an instinct he hadn't fully understood at the time.

Cell service was the last to go, and the cruelest, because it went slowly enough to let him keep hoping right up until it didn't. Calls that connected clean on day five started dropping mid-sentence on day six. By the seventh morning, he tried Catherine's number and got nothing at all — not a busy signal, not a failed connection notice, just the same dead, open silence that had defined every call he'd ever made on the very first day of this, except now it was a number that used to answer, gone quiet in a way that felt like a door closing somewhere he couldn't see.

He tried again an hour later. Then every hour after that, methodical even through the rising, cold fear of it, the same discipline that had carried him through every hard thing since Lilburn now turned toward a silence he had no way to interpret. Sick. Moved. Worse. He made himself not finish any of those thoughts all the way.

He found Sam on the porch that evening — steadier now, most of the week's fog finally lifting, enough that John could see it in the way he was sitting, present in his own body again in a way he hadn't been in days.

"I need to tell you something," John said, sitting down beside him. "And I need you to hear it as information, not as a reason to fly before you're ready."

Sam looked at him, something sharpening behind his eyes. "Catherine."

"I lost contact with her this morning," John said. "Cell service dropped out here sometime overnight. I don't know if it's the same everywhere, I don't know if it's the same in Tucson, I don't know anything except that the last time I talked to her, she was fine, and I haven't been able to reach her since."

Sam sat with that a long moment, and John watched something shift in him — not the fog lifting exactly, but a different kind of clarity replacing it, the particular focus of a man who'd just been handed a problem sharp enough to cut through everything else he'd been carrying.

"I'm ready," Sam said. "I should have been ready two days ago. I'm ready now." He stood, some of his old steadiness visibly reasserting itself, though John could still see the cost of the week sitting underneath it. "We leave in the morning. First light. I am not losing anyone else to waiting."

"Okay," John said, simply, no argument in him at all. "Then that's what we do."

## 11 — John, Day 8

They left Lilburn at first light, exactly as Sam had promised, the PC-12's cabin quieter than John had ever felt it — no easy conversation, no working through problems out loud, just the steady drone of the engines and the particular tension of not knowing what they were flying toward. Tyler slept fitfully against Dan's flank somewhere over Alabama. John didn't sleep at all.

He tried Catherine's number twice more in the air, out of habit more than hope, and got the same dead silence both times.

It was somewhere over New Mexico that the radio crackled with something neither of them expected — an automated Emergency Alert System broadcast, looping, the same flat mechanical voice reading the same message every few minutes into a silence it had no way of knowing was almost total. *This is an automated alert. A safety shutdown has been initiated at a nuclear generating facility in the Tucson metropolitan area. Residents are advised to remain calm. This is a precautionary system response. Please stand by for further information.*

Sam's hands went rigid on the yoke. "That's not a meltdown warning," he said, working through it out loud, the same careful process John recognized from every hard decision Sam had ever talked himself through in a cockpit. "That's a SCRAM alert. Automated safety shutdown — the reactor's built to do that on its own if nobody's there to keep it running properly. It's not supposed to be dangerous. It's the plant protecting itself."

"Supposed to be," John said, the two words doing a lot of work.

They flew the rest of the approach in a tighter, more careful silence than before, John running every worst-case scenario he could think of and discarding most of them, holding onto just enough uncertainty to keep his stomach in knots the whole way down.

Tucson itself, when it finally resolved beneath them, showed no visible sign of catastrophe — no smoke, no fire, nothing that looked like the disaster the alert's flat, looping voice had half-convinced him to expect. But the city's lights, what few of them should have still been holding this deep into the collapse, had gone dark in wide, uneven patches, whole neighborhoods sitting in shadow that John suspected traced directly back to a power grid that had just lost one of its largest sources without any warning at all.

Sam brought them down at the same general aviation terminal Catherine had described on their first call, and this time, no one was waiting on the ramp.

John felt the absence like a physical blow. He made himself move anyway, the same discipline that had carried him through every hard thing since Lilburn now aimed at the last address Catherine had ever given him — a house a short drive from the hospital where she'd worked, information she'd passed along on one of their calls without either of them realizing, at the time, how badly they'd eventually need it.

They found a car in the terminal lot, keys where they always were, and drove into a city that felt different from every other quiet, empty place this search had already taken them — not peaceful-empty, but tense-empty, an alert still looping faintly from car radios left running in driveways, the particular held-breath quality of a place that had just been told to remain calm and hadn't quite managed it.

Catherine's house sat exactly where she'd described it, lights off, curtains drawn, a car in the driveway that hadn't moved in what looked like days.

John was out of the vehicle before it had fully stopped, Sam right behind him, both of them moving with the same controlled urgency, and it was Sam who reached the front door first and pounded on it, calling her name into a stillness that gave nothing back.

The door was unlocked. John pushed it open, heart hammering, bracing himself for exactly the kind of discovery this search had already taught him to fear.

"Catherine?" he called, into a dim, quiet house. "Catherine, it's John."

A long, terrible pause. And then, from somewhere down the hall, a voice — tired, hoarse, but unmistakably alive.

"John?" Catherine appeared in a doorway, blinking against the daylight spilling in behind him, looking exhausted and pale but entirely, blessedly upright. "You're here. I lost the internet six days ago, and then the phone a day after that, and I didn't — I've been rationing my flashlight batteries for two days telling myself someone would still come."

John crossed the room and pulled her into a hug he hadn't planned, relief hitting him harder than he expected, and felt her arms come up around him just as fiercely.

"We came," he said. "We're sorry it took so long. Sam needed time, and I wasn't going to fly a man in shock across the country. But we came."

Catherine pulled back, looking between the two of them, something steadying in her already at the mere fact of their presence. "The reactor," she said. "Did you hear the alert?"

"We heard it," Sam said. "SCRAM shutdown, best I can tell. Safety system, not a meltdown. But it's already taken half the city's power grid down with it."

"Then we shouldn't linger," Catherine said, already moving toward a bag she'd clearly kept packed and ready for exactly this moment. "I don't know what else fails once a plant that size goes fully offline. I'd rather be somewhere else when we find out."

"Agreed," John said, and looked around the darkened house one more time before they left it behind — the last quiet place Catherine had waited, alone, far longer than any of them should have asked her to.

They were back in the air within the hour, Tucson's uneven, patchy darkness falling away behind them, four people and a dog now, John thought, watching Catherine settle into a window seat with the same careful, disbelieving relief he suspected he'd keep seeing on every face this search ever turned up.

"Where to?" Sam asked, once they'd leveled off.

Catherine was quiet a moment, looking out at the desert falling away below. "My brother runs a shop outside Reno," she said finally. "TruckHouse. Expedition vehicles — the kind of thing that doesn't need a grid at all." Something more personal moved behind her eyes. "I haven't been able to reach him either. That's part of why I want to go. Not just for what he built. I need to know."

John felt the shape of the next leg of this settle into place, the same instinct that had carried him through every hard decision so far. "Then that's where we go next," he said. "Reno."

## 12 — John, Day 1

The drive out from Reno-Stead, where Sam set the PC-12 down late that afternoon, took them through country that looked almost defiantly ordinary — high desert scrub, the Sierra foothills rising pale and distant ahead, nothing about the landscape itself giving any sign of what had happened to the people who used to drive through it. Catherine sat up front beside Sam, quiet in a way John recognized now, the particular stillness of someone bracing herself for a door she didn't know what was going to be on the other side of.

TruckHouse's shop sat low and industrial at the foot of the foothills, exactly where Catherine had described it, and the first thing John registered pulling into the gravel lot was the hum — actual electrical hum, lights on inside the building, visible even in the late afternoon sun. Grid power, still holding out here same as it had held out in Atlanta, the same borrowed time they'd all been living on since the beginning.

Catherine was out of the car before it had fully stopped, moving fast toward the office door, and John let her go, understanding this part wasn't his to manage. She called her brother's name into the building — John caught the name, Marcus, called twice, three times — and came back out a few minutes later with her jaw set tight and her eyes carefully dry, the same clinical composure she'd used to hold herself together on the phone the day before.

"He's not here," she said. "His truck's gone from the lot. No note. Nothing." She looked back at the building once, then forced her attention back to the group, back to the job in front of her, the way John had already watched her do more than once. "I don't know what that means. I have to let it not mean anything yet."

Nobody pushed her on it. John understood that particular discipline better than most people would have — the decision to set a question down carefully rather than let it swallow the day.

The shop itself, once they'd walked it, told its own story of a business caught mid-stride. Seven BCRs sat in various stages of readiness across the floor and the gravel lot outside — four Crew Cab units and three Regular Cab, TruckHouse having ramped production hard back in 2028 with a backlog of customer orders that would now, John understood without anyone needing to say it, never be collected. Grid power was still running to the shop, water still flowing, the whole facility humming along exactly as if a workday might resume at any moment.

He kept working.

## 13 — John, Day 10

The call came through late in the morning, a pattern John almost dismissed at first glance — a single number in San Diego, one call to 911 placed the afternoon before, nothing repeated, nothing that matched the "keeps trying" signature Catherine had taught him to look for. He almost moved past it. Then he noticed the timestamp sat well outside the two a.m. window that defined every other call in the data, hours later than anything else in the whole search — someone calling for help not in the first panicked minutes of discovering the world had gone silent, but a full day into it, which meant something had happened to her since.

## 14 — Yohan, Day 0

Yohan Kessler had spent twenty-six of his forty-four years at sea, long enough that the ocean had stopped feeling like a place he visited and started feeling like the only place that had ever really fit him. Chief mate now, second in command aboard the *Northern Pacific*, a container ship four days out of Yokohama and six more from Seattle, carrying eleven thousand boxes of cargo and a crew of twenty-two across a stretch of ocean that didn't care one way or another whether any of them made it.

He'd never married. Never had the kind of life ashore that made a person miss it — a sister in Rotterdam he called on holidays, a handful of old shipmates scattered across a dozen countries, and the sea itself, constant in a way nothing on land had ever managed to be. He liked it that way. Had built a whole life around liking it that way.

His watch started at four that morning, the same rotation he'd kept for longer than he cared to count, and he climbed up to the bridge in the dark expecting to relieve the second mate and settle into the quiet hours before dawn the way he had ten thousand times before.

The bridge was dark in a way it shouldn't have been. Instruments lit, autopilot engaged, the ship holding its course steady and competent exactly as designed — but no one at the helm, no one in the chair, the second mate slumped over the chart table like he'd simply stopped in the middle of a task and never started again.

Yohan didn't panic, not at first. Twenty-six years at sea taught a man to move toward a problem before he let himself feel anything about it, and he crossed the bridge fast, checked for a pulse he already suspected he wouldn't find, and confirmed a stillness that told him everything before his mind had caught up to accepting it.

He worked the radio next, old habit overriding shock, calling out on the emergency channel, on the company frequency, on anything he could think to try. Nothing came back — not silence exactly, the sets were live, transmitting, receiving nothing but the low hiss of open air on every band he tried.

He went below after that, moving through the ship's narrow passageways with a growing, sick certainty settling into his chest with every door he opened. The captain, in his cabin, gone the same way. The engineers, down in the engine room, slumped at stations that had kept running without them, the massive diesel plant humming along on automated systems that didn't need a living hand to keep functioning, at least not yet. The galley, the mess, the crew quarters — twenty-two men and women he'd sailed with for months, some of them for years, all of them gone in whatever had happened while he'd been asleep in his own bunk two decks below.

He sat down hard on a bench in the empty mess hall somewhere in the middle of that long, terrible walk, and let himself feel it for exactly as long as he could afford to — a handful of minutes, no more, because the ship was still moving, still carrying him and eleven thousand containers of someone else's cargo toward a port that might have no one left in it either, and there was no version of grief that solved that problem.

He climbed back up to the bridge afterward and stood alone at the helm, hands resting on a console built for a rotating watch of professionals, not one man carrying the weight of an entire vessel by himself. The autopilot could hold a course. It couldn't stand a proper watch, couldn't manage the engine room's endless small demands, couldn't do a hundred things a ship this size genuinely needed done by human hands over the six days still ahead of him.

He thought, briefly, about turning back toward Yokohama — closer, in theory, though he had no more reason to believe Japan held anything better waiting for him than Seattle did. He thought about what six more days alone at sea would actually cost him, mentally, physically, a man built for company even in his solitude now facing the longest silence of his life.

He decided, in the end, the way he decided most things — methodically, without much drama, because someone had to and there was no one else left to.

"Seattle, then," he said out loud, to an empty bridge, his own voice sounding strange and overloud in a space built for the constant low murmur of a working crew. He checked the course, confirmed the autopilot's heading, and settled himself into the watch chair for what he understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful underneath the horror, was going to be the longest six days of his entire life at sea.

Somewhere out past the bow, the Pacific stretched on, vast and indifferent, exactly as it always had.

## 15 — John, Day 10

Ten days in, the TruckHouse lot had started to look less like a shop and more like something closer to a real settlement — five BCRs arranged in a loose semicircle around the main building now instead of scattered wherever they'd first been parked, a rhythm to the days that hadn't existed that first frantic week. Grace's leg was healing well under Catherine's care, enough that she'd started hobbling out to the two remaining unclaimed BCRs on crutches most afternoons, muttering about diesel injectors and cursing softly at anything that didn't turn the way she expected it to. Tyler had claimed a corner of the shop floor as an unofficial workshop of his own, shadowing Grace with the same serious attention he brought to everything now. Sam flew supply runs when they were needed and sat quiet in his BCR's open crew door most evenings, feet up on the running board, present in a way that had grown steadier by the day without ever fully losing its edge of grief.

The communications problem had turned out to be more solvable than John expected, once he'd had real time and a functioning workshop to throw at it. TruckHouse itself had already been running Starlink — Marcus, wherever he'd gone, had apparently wired the whole facility for exactly this kind of off-grid resilience before any of them had ever needed it that way, and the terminal sat quietly on the shop roof pulling down a connection that didn't care one bit whether ground infrastructure held or failed. That solved half the problem outright. The other half — real-time voice communication between the group when they were scattered across a continent chasing leads — took another week of scavenging before they'd found what they needed: a case of Iridium satellite phones from a marine supply warehouse outside Reno, still boxed, still functional, easily configured once John had the manuals pulled up on the Starlink connection. He'd handed them out two days ago, one to each BCR, the group's first real communications redundancy since Lilburn.

The harder problem was the one he hadn't solved yet, and it was the one that kept him at Marcus's desk long after everyone else had gone quiet for the night. The 911 search, so productive in that first frantic week, had gone almost completely dry. Days now without a single new repeat-caller pattern anywhere in the country, the well of unanswered emergency calls apparently exhausted — either because everyone who was ever going to call had already called, or because cell infrastructure itself had degraded past the point of the search even finding what remained. He suspected both were true, layered on top of each other, and neither possibility gave him anywhere left to dig.

He sat with that problem for the better part of an evening, Dan asleep across his feet, working through it the same methodical way he worked through everything — if one well had run dry, the answer wasn't to keep drawing from it. It was to find another well.

Social media was the first idea that actually held up under scrutiny. Every platform's servers were, as far as he could tell, still technically running, same borrowed grace as everything else — automated infrastructure grinding on without anyone left to feed it. If someone out there had posted anything, anything at all, since two a.m. that first morning, it would still be sitting there, waiting, the same way the 911 logs had been waiting before he'd gone looking. He started building the search that night — not a general trawl, which would drown in noise, but something narrower and smarter: any post, anywhere, with a timestamp after the event, cross-referenced against location data where the platforms exposed it, filtered the same way Catherine had taught him to filter the call logs. Real signal, not noise. People who'd kept trying, not people who'd tried once and gone silent.

The second idea came to him almost as an afterthought, lying awake in the BCR's bunk well past midnight, and it was the kind of idea that made him sit up fast enough that Dan startled awake beside him. Emergency locator transmitters. Every aircraft, every marine vessel above a certain size, carried one — automated beacons that triggered on impact or activation and broadcast a location straight to satellite, no human intervention required once triggered. If a plane had gone down anywhere in the last ten days — like the one that had put Grace in a splint, like the UPS heavy still burning at Cleveland Hopkins as far as anyone knew — its ELT would have fired automatically, and that signal would be sitting in a satellite tracking database somewhere, waiting the exact same patient way everything else had been waiting.

It wasn't a search for survivors directly. It was something almost better — a search for exactly the kind of chaos that meant someone had been alive and moving through the world after the silence started. A crash meant a pilot. A pilot meant, maybe, someone like Sam, someone who'd made it down and might still be out there.

He brought both ideas to the group the next morning, laying them out over coffee outside Grace's BCR, folding chairs pulled into the shade of the vehicle's tall carbon-fiber flank, the closest thing they had to a common meeting space these days.

"The 911 well's dry," he said. "I don't think that's going to change. But there are other signals out there we haven't touched yet — social media posts with timestamps after two a.m., and ELT beacons from any crash that's happened since. Both of those exist independent of whether anyone's still around to answer a phone."

Catherine was already nodding, turning the idea over with the same sharp focus she brought to everything. "The ELT idea's good. Better than good — it's not passive like the calls were. It tells us about events, not just people hoping to be found."

"Do it," Sam said, simple and immediate, the same decisiveness John had come to rely on. "Whatever gets us the next Grace, the next Tyler. I don't care how we find them, as long as we do."

John looked around at the small circle of people who'd become, over ten strange and difficult days, something he hadn't let himself name yet but was starting to feel the shape of anyway — not quite family, not quite team, something built new out of necessity that had somehow started to feel like more than either.

"Then that's the new priority," he said. "Starting today."

## 16 — Yohan, Day 2

Two days into the silence, Yohan had settled into a routine that felt less like living and more like maintaining a machine that happened to also be keeping him alive — watch rotations he kept by himself now, four hours on, four hours of restless half-sleep, the ship's systems demanding just enough of his attention to keep him from thinking too hard about the empty berths below decks.

He'd tried everything he could think of that first full day, methodical the way the sea had trained him to be about everything. Emergency broadcast on every maritime frequency, repeated every few hours in case anyone was out there listening even if no one was transmitting back. Calls to the company's shore office in Seattle, unanswered, the line ringing into the same flat, hollow nothing he'd gotten from everyone else he'd tried. He'd found the ship's satellite internet terminal — installed for the crew's off-hours use, a small luxury on long Pacific crossings — and spent a good hour scrolling through every news source he could reach, hoping for something, anything, that would explain what had happened. Every headline sat frozen at the same point, nothing updated in two days now, the internet itself apparently as abandoned as everything else.

He'd told himself, that first day, that someone would answer eventually. That the silence was a malfunction, a widespread outage, something with an edge to it he simply hadn't found yet. By the second day, he no longer quite believed that, though he kept trying anyway, because trying was the only thing that made the hours pass without swallowing him whole.

It was well into his second evening watch, the sun dropping low and orange over a horizon with nothing on it in any direction, when the thought finally occurred to him — so obvious, in retrospect, that he felt almost embarrassed it had taken him this long.

The ELT.

Every vessel of the *Northern Pacific*'s size carried one, tucked into a weatherproof housing near the bridge wing, designed to activate automatically in a genuine emergency — capsizing, sinking, the kind of catastrophic event that made manual activation a formality rather than a necessity. He'd never had reason to touch it in twenty-six years at sea, had barely thought about its existence beyond the annual inspection checklist that confirmed it was still there, still charged, still ready.

He stood in front of it now, hand hovering over the activation switch, running through the logic one more time before he committed. It wasn't built for this — wasn't built to say *I am alive and alone and don't know what to do*, only to say *this vessel is in distress*. But a signal was a signal, and if anyone out there had the means to be watching for one, it would tell them exactly what mattered most: a location, a heartbeat, proof that at least one working mind remained aboard a ship that should, by every rule he understood about the ocean, have gone dark and drifting like everything else apparently had.

He flipped the switch.

The small indicator light blinked to life, steady and patient, transmitting now on a frequency he had no way of knowing whether anyone was still listening to. He stood there a long moment, watching it, feeling something loosen in his chest that he hadn't realized had been wound so tight — not hope exactly, he'd learned better than to let himself feel that fully yet, but something adjacent to it. Action. A signal sent out into a silence that had, until this exact moment, only ever been something happening *to* him.

"There," he said out loud, to the empty bridge, the same strange comfort he'd found in the sound of his own voice these past two days. "Now someone has to be looking."

He didn't know if that was true. He didn't have any way to know. But he turned back to the helm, checked the autopilot's steady heading toward Seattle, and settled into the watch chair for what remained of the evening — four more days ahead of him, however many more silent hours, and now, for the first time since he'd found the second mate slumped over the chart table, a single small light blinking somewhere out in the world that wasn't entirely his to carry alone anymore.

## 19 — Yohan, Day 6

Land came up out of the haze on the sixth day, a low gray line at first that Yohan almost didn't trust himself to believe, six days of nothing but open water having worn his eyes and his judgment down to something he no longer fully relied on. But it held, resolved slowly into the unmistakable shape of coastline, the Olympic Peninsula's dark hills rising up out of the Pacific mist exactly where the chart said they should be.

He stood at the bridge windows a long time just looking at it, something loosening in his chest that he hadn't let himself feel the whole voyage — not relief exactly, not yet, but the particular ache of a sailor's eyes finding land after open water, a reflex older than any of the grief or fear the last six days had piled on top of it.

The relief didn't last long. Land solved one problem and handed him a much harder one in its place.

The *Northern Pacific* was not a vessel built to dock herself. Container ships this size ran on a whole ecosystem of shore-based support that Yohan had never once had to think about in twenty-six years at sea, because it had simply always been there — harbor pilots who came aboard to guide a vessel through the final approach, tugboats to nudge eighty thousand tons of steel into a berth too tight for her own maneuvering, line handlers on the dock to make her fast, terminal operators to work the cranes that would eventually need to unload eleven thousand containers no one was left to want. He had none of that. He had himself, an autopilot that could hold a heading but couldn't judge a channel, and a harbor he'd only ever entered before with a pilot standing at his shoulder telling him exactly where to point the bow.

He worked the problem the way the sea had taught him to work every problem, methodical, refusing to let the scale of it overwhelm him before he'd actually tried. He pulled every chart he had for the approach to Seattle, studied the channel depths, the turning basin, the berths themselves, memorizing what a pilot would normally simply tell him in the moment. He slowed the ship well outside the shipping lanes, buying himself time and sea room to think without the added pressure of closing distance.

Anchoring, he decided, was the only responsible answer for now. He wasn't going to bring an unguided container ship into a working harbor alone, not when a mistake could block a channel for good or put her aground somewhere that mattered. He found a proper anchorage on the chart, well clear of any traffic that might theoretically still be moving, and brought the *Northern Pacific* to a slow, careful stop for the first time in six days, chain running out with a great tearing rattle that felt, after so much silence, almost deafening.

He stood on the bridge wing afterward, engines finally quiet beneath him, the ship swinging gently on her anchor in the gray Pacific swell, and looked out at land close enough now to make out individual trees on the distant shoreline. Seattle sat somewhere beyond that headland, invisible from here, and he had no idea yet what he'd find there — whether it held anyone at all, whether a harbor this size held even a fraction of the ordinary life it should have.

He tried the radio one more time, an old habit he hadn't managed to break in six days of getting nothing back. "This is the *Northern Pacific*, anchored outside Seattle traffic separation scheme, requesting any station, any response." He let the silence hang after it, same as it always did, and then, almost as an afterthought, added something he hadn't said on any of his hundred previous attempts. "If anyone out there is listening — I'm alone. I've been alone for six days. I don't know what I'm sailing into."

Nothing answered. He hadn't really expected it to.

He went below eventually, found what remained of the ship's stores, and made himself a proper meal for the first time in days, sitting alone in a mess hall built for a crew of twenty-two, the empty benches around him a silence he'd learned, slowly, to carry rather than fight. Tomorrow, he decided, he'd take one of the ship's small boats and go ashore himself — see what Seattle actually held before he risked bringing eleven thousand containers and himself any closer to a harbor he no longer had any way of trusting blind.

Behind him, through the mess hall's small porthole, the anchor light burned steady against the coming dark, a single point of light on a ship no one on shore had any way of knowing was there.

## 20 — John, Day 15

The filter took longer than a couple of days to get right, but by the third evening John finally had something he trusted — a clean separation between the ragged aircraft wave still trailing off somewhere out past Day Twelve and the much sparser maritime channel underneath it, exactly the quiet signal he'd promised the group he'd build. ## 21 — Sam, Day 16

"Nothing yet," Sam said, already banking them back onto course, though something in his expression suggested it wasn't nothing at all — the particular look of a man who'd just watched a problem he hadn't known he was carrying start quietly solving itself. "Just an idea. Let's find Yohan first. I'll tell you about it after."

He kept the call going every few minutes as they closed the distance, the same steady, patient repetition he'd used on every frequency two weeks ago over the Atlantic, before any of this had a name yet. "This is aircraft calling the vessel anchored off the Washington coast, container ship, request any response, over." Nothing but open static answering him back, mile after mile, the Cascades sliding by dark and green below the wings.

They were maybe forty minutes out, the coastline just starting to resolve ahead of them, when the radio crackled with something that wasn't static.

"—receiving. Repeat, this is the *Northern Pacific*, I am receiving you, over."

The voice came through rough, accented, cautious in a way that told Sam everything about how long it had been since this man had last heard another human voice answer him back. Sam felt his whole chest tighten, the same lurch he remembered from the first time John's voice had come through his own phone at Gander, and keyed the mic with hands that weren't quite steady.

"Northern Pacific, this is Sam, aboard an aircraft inbound from Reno. We picked up your ELT signal. You're not alone out there anymore." He heard his own voice crack slightly on the last words and didn't bother fighting it. "Can you confirm — are you the only one aboard?"

A long pause, long enough that Sam glanced at John, both of them holding their breath the same way, and then the voice came back, and something in it had changed, gone thick and unsteady in a way the careful radio discipline underneath it couldn't quite hide.

"Yes. Yes, I am alone. Sixteen days now." Another pause, shorter this time. "You said — you picked up my signal? You are real? This is not a malfunction of my own radio?"

"We're real," Sam said. "There's a whole group of us, back in Nevada. My name's Sam. I'm a pilot. I've got a friend named John with me — he's the one who actually found your beacon, been building tools to look for exactly this kind of thing for two weeks now." He glanced at John, who was gripping the edge of his seat, eyes bright, and gestured for him to lean toward the mic.

"Hi," John said, his voice unsteady in a way Sam hadn't heard from him since that very first call to a stranger's phone in Sparks — no, further back, since the very first call to Sam himself. "What's your name?"

"Yohan," the voice said. "Yohan Kessler. Chief mate — I was chief mate, of this vessel." A ragged breath came through the radio, something between a laugh and a sob, the sound of sixteen days of careful, solitary discipline finally cracking open. "Sixteen days I have talked to no one. I started talking to the ship herself, I think, some days. I did not expect anyone to actually come."

"We've been looking," John said. "Longer than you'd think. You're not the only one we've found, Yohan — there's more of us than you'd guess, scattered everywhere, all trying to find each other the same way we just found you." He paused, and Sam could hear him choosing the next words carefully, the same deliberate gentleness he used with everyone. "How are you doing? Really?"

"Tired," Yohan admitted, plain and unguarded in a way that told Sam more about the man's exhaustion than any longer answer could have. "Relieved. I do not entirely believe this yet. I have eleven thousand containers of cargo and a ship built for twenty-two people, and I do not know what to do with either of those facts anymore."

"You don't have to figure that out today," Sam said. He glanced at John, working through the logistics out loud before he'd fully finished thinking them. "Getting out to you is the next problem, though. Neither of us has any business trying to commandeer a harbor boat we don't know how to run."

"You do not need to," Yohan said, and Sam could hear something steadier coming into his voice now, competence reasserting itself the way it clearly always had for this man under pressure. "This vessel carries lifeboats — enclosed, diesel-powered, built for exactly this. I have drilled with them more times than I can count. I can bring myself to shore far more safely than you could ever bring a boat out to me." A pause, calculation audible in it. "Give me a landing point. Somewhere with a beach or a low dock, close to the water, that I can reach without needing a proper harbor to receive me."

John was already pulling up a chart on the tablet mounted between the seats, scanning the coastline south of the main shipping channels. "There's a state park beach a few miles south of the terminal — open water access, no breakwater to navigate, should be simple enough to reach in a lifeboat. We can land the PC-12 at Boeing Field and drive down to meet you."

"Send me the coordinates," Yohan said. "I can be there within the hour once I'm underway. It will feel very good to finally be doing something with my own hands again, instead of only waiting."

"Coordinates coming your way," John said, already typing. "We'll be waiting on the beach when you get there."

"Then I will see you soon," Yohan said, and Sam heard, underneath the words, something that hadn't been there at the start of the call — a man who'd spent sixteen days simply enduring, now finally moving toward something instead of just holding a position against the silence.

Sam grinned despite everything, wiping at his own eyes with the back of one hand before he keyed the mic again. "We'll see you soon, Yohan. Hang on. Someone's finally coming."

## 22 — John, Day 16

They landed at Boeing Field a little before noon, the field as still and empty as every other airport they'd touched down at these past sixteen days, and drove south along a coastal road that wound through evergreens and empty small towns until it opened out onto the state park beach John had picked off the chart. Gray sand, a low tideline, the Sound stretching out flat and silver toward a horizon with nothing on it — nothing, at least, that they could see yet.

They didn't have to wait long. Within the hour, exactly as promised, a shape resolved out of the haze offshore — a stubby, orange-and-white enclosed lifeboat, unmistakably purposeful in its design, cutting a steady wake toward the beach with the kind of confident, unhurried competence that told John, even from this distance, that whoever was driving it knew exactly what he was doing.

Sam stood at the waterline with his hands in his pockets, watching it come in, and John saw something pass across his face that he recognized now as an old, familiar ache — the particular kinship of one professional watching another do a hard thing well.

The lifeboat grounded gently on the sand a few yards out, and a moment later the hatch opened and a man climbed down into the shallow surf, wading the last few steps to shore with the careful, deliberate movements of someone whose legs hadn't fully trusted solid ground in over two weeks.

Yohan Kessler was tall, weathered, gray threading through dark hair and a beard that had clearly gone untrimmed since long before any of this started. He stopped a few feet up the beach, looking at the three of them — John, Sam, Catherine — with an expression that undid something in his composed, careful bearing almost the instant he registered they were real, standing there, actually waiting for him.

"You are here," he said, voice rough, disbelieving in the exact way everyone's had been at this exact moment, over and over, sixteen days and counting of the same impossible relief repeating itself across a whole continent. "I did not — even after the radio, some part of me did not fully believe until I saw you standing there."

"We're here," Sam said, and closed the distance first, the way he always did, pulling Yohan into a brief, hard handshake that turned into something closer to an embrace before either of them seemed to decide it consciously. "Sam. Good landing, by the way. Smooth as anything I've flown."

Yohan let out a short, startled laugh, the sound clearly unfamiliar in his own throat after so long without cause to use it. "Twenty-six years, I have never once had to actually use one of those boats for real. I am glad it still remembered how, even if I was uncertain."

Catherine stepped forward next, professional eyes already sweeping over him the way she did with everyone now — exhaustion, dehydration, the particular hollowed-out look of a man who'd been running on discipline alone for over two weeks. "Catherine. I'm a doctor. When's the last time you had a proper meal, or slept more than a few hours straight?"

"Honestly," Yohan said, "I could not tell you precisely. Time became strange out there, somewhere around the first week."

"We'll fix that," Catherine said, simply, already reaching for the medical bag she'd brought along on instinct.

John came last, and found himself, for a moment, without the easy words that had come to everyone else — just the accumulated weight of sixteen days spent searching, culminating in an actual man standing on an actual beach in front of him. "John," he managed finally. "I built the thing that found your signal. I've been looking for people like you since the second day of all this."

Yohan looked at him a long moment, something working behind his eyes, and then reached out and gripped John's hand with both of his own, holding on longer than a normal handshake would call for. "Then I owe you more than I have words for right now," he said. "Sixteen days, I spoke to no one but myself and an empty ship. You gave that back to me." He glanced past them, toward the tree line, the road, the world beyond the beach that he hadn't set foot on in weeks. "What happens now? To the ship. To me."

"Now," John said, "you come with us. We've got a place — real shelter, other people, a way of doing this that actually works. The ship will still be there whenever you're ready to think about what happens to her. Today, you just get to stop being alone."

Yohan looked back out at the *Northern Pacific*, a distant gray shape on the horizon, silent and patient the way it had been for sixteen days now, and then turned fully toward the group standing in front of him, toward the road, toward whatever came next.

"Then let us go," he said. "I have been ready to stop being alone for a very long time."

## 23 — Grace, Day 16

Grace heard the truck before she saw it — an engine still idling, off in the trees a short way past the edge of the gravel lot, close enough to the property that it hadn't registered as strange until she actually stopped to listen for how long it had been running. She was out from under the BCR she'd been working on before she'd even fully placed the sound, wrench still in hand, old instinct sharpening into something closer to alarm.

She found it parked askew half in the ditch along the spur road, driver's door hanging open, engine still turning over uselessly, gas gauge reading near empty. The cab was empty.

It took her only a moment to find him — a short distance from the truck, in the tall grass along the tree line, like he'd made it that far and no further. She didn't need to check for long to know. She checked anyway, the way you always did, some old reflex refusing to just accept it from a distance.

Marcus. She knew him only from the photographs still pinned to the office corkboard, from the way Catherine talked about him on the drive out from Tucson, from a name that had been sitting unresolved in all of their minds since the day they'd first walked into an empty shop and found his truck already gone.

He'd been dead for a while. Not recently — not days, she guessed, though she wasn't the one qualified to say for certain. Longer than that. Whatever had happened, wherever he'd been trying to get to or get away from, this stretch of grass a short drive from his own shop had been as far as he'd made it.

She called Tyler over the sat phone with hands that weren't quite steady, and then, because there was no way around it, called Catherine next.

"Catherine," Grace said, when the line connected, her voice as gentle as she could make it and still nowhere near gentle enough for what she had to say. "I need you to come back to the shop. It's Marcus. I found him."

A long pause on the other end, the particular silence Grace recognized now from every hard call this search had ever required. "Is he—" Catherine started, and didn't finish it, because she already knew, the same way everyone always already knew before the words landed.

"I'm so sorry," Grace said. "He didn't make it. I found his truck a little ways down the spur road. I don't think it happened recently — I think he's been gone a while now."

The sound that came back down the line wasn't words at all, just a raw, broken exhale, weeks of hope finally collapsing into the one outcome none of them had let themselves say out loud.

"I'm coming back," Catherine said finally, voice wrecked but steady, the same discipline she leaned on for everyone else now turned, briefly, toward holding herself together. "Don't move him. I want to see him myself."

"Take your time," Grace said. "He's not going anywhere. I'll stay with him until you get here."

She sat down in the grass a short distance away, not quite beside him, giving him the same careful, private dignity she'd learned to give every body this search had turned up, and waited for the rest of the group to come home to grief instead of the reunion every one of them had been quietly hoping for.

## 24 — Catherine, Day 16

They made the flight back to Reno in under two hours, Yohan quiet and watchful in the PC-12's cabin, John and Sam saying little, giving Catherine the silence she hadn't asked for but clearly needed. She sat rigid in her seat the whole way, hands folded tight in her lap, running through every version of hope she'd let herself carry over the past sixteen days and watching each one collapse in turn.

The drive out from the airstrip felt endless, and then the shop was there, gravel crunching under the tires, and Grace was waiting exactly where she'd said she'd be — not at the shop itself, but a short walk down the spur road, standing a respectful distance from a stretch of tall grass at the tree line.

Catherine was out of the vehicle before it had fully stopped, and John let her go, understanding this part wasn't his to manage.

She knelt beside her brother for a long time, saying nothing, the doctor in her cataloguing details she didn't want to catalogue — how long, what had likely happened, questions her training wouldn't let her stop asking even now. He'd died alone, some distance from his own truck, from his own shop, from anyone who might have helped him. She would never know exactly why he'd stopped where he had, or what he'd been trying to reach.

"I kept thinking he'd have an explanation," she said eventually, to no one in particular, to John standing a few respectful steps back. "Some story about where he'd been. I never let myself actually picture this."

"You don't have to explain any of it to us," John said quietly. "Take whatever time you need."

They buried him that evening, on the shop's own grounds, the small circle of people who'd found each other over the past sixteen days standing together for a grief none of them could fully carry for her but all of them could stand beside.

Afterward, sitting alone in Marcus's own office long after the others had drifted off to their own quiet corners of grief, Catherine found herself turning a different question over — not about her brother specifically, but about all of it. Sixteen days of finding survivors, and in every single case, she realized, sitting with the accumulated weight of it, death had looked the same. Instant. No warning, no illness, nothing a body had visibly fought against. Whatever this was, it hadn't given anyone time to get sick and recover, hadn't left survivors with fevers or symptoms to track. It had simply happened, all at once, to nearly everyone — and left behind, apparently at complete random, the handful of people it hadn't touched at all.

She found John still awake in the shop that same night, notebook in hand, the same restless need to understand something that had carried her through every hard night of medical training.

"I want to know why," she said. "Not for Marcus — nothing's going to change what happened to him. But for the rest of us. For whoever else is still out there. If there's a reason some of us are still breathing and almost nobody else is, I want to actually understand it instead of just being grateful for it."

"What do you need?" John asked.

"Scientists," Catherine said. "Real ones — microbiologists, epidemiologists, anyone who might still be alive and might actually know how to start answering a question this big. I don't have the training to solve this alone, and I don't want to keep pretending I might stumble into an answer without real help."

John was already nodding, mind visibly assembling the shape of the search. "Same method as everything else. University directories, CDC and WHO rosters, whatever signal data I can cross-reference. It won't be fast. But if there's someone alive out there who actually understands this, I'll find a way to get you their number."

"Start tonight," Catherine said, and looked out toward the tree line where they'd buried her brother the evening before. "I don't know what we'll find. I just don't want to keep not looking."

## 25 — Sam, Day 16

Sam had been quiet through most of the conversation in Marcus's office, leaning against the doorframe with his arms crossed, listening the way he always did before he spoke — turning something over fully before he trusted himself to say it out loud. Catherine's grief. Her sudden, urgent need to actually understand what had happened instead of just surviving it. Her request for scientists who might know where to even start. He'd been turning it over the whole time, patient, methodical, waiting for the pieces to arrange themselves into something worth saying.

They arranged themselves all at once, hard enough that he actually stood up straight out of his lean against the doorframe.

The International Space Station. Nine people, as far as he could remember from the news cycle a few months back — some multinational crew rotation, longer stints than usual, a mix of NASA, Roscosmos, and a couple of international partners he couldn't name off the top of his head. Two hundred and fifty miles up, orbiting a planet that had gone catastrophically silent sixteen days ago, breathing air that had never once touched whatever had come down through everyone else's.

They weren't in the atmosphere. Whatever this was — airborne, Catherine kept saying, the word had come up more than once tonight — the ISS crew had been sealed away from it by the simple, enormous fact of low Earth orbit.

Which meant, as far as Sam could work out standing there with a checklist forgotten in his hand, there was a real chance nine people were alive up there right now, alone in a way that made everyone he'd already found look almost fortunate by comparison — no one to call, watching a planet go dark beneath them with absolutely no way to reach anyone on it, and every reason to believe that going home might now be a death sentence instead of a homecoming.

"The ISS," he said, cutting in without much ceremony. "Whatever's out there — it's airborne, right? That's the working theory?"

Catherine blinked at the interruption, then caught up fast, the same quick clinical focus she brought to everything, even through the rawness of the day. "That's the working theory, yes. Why?"

"There are astronauts up there," Sam said. "Nine of them, last I knew. They've been in orbit this whole time. They never breathed whatever came down through the atmosphere — they physically couldn't have, up where they are." He watched the realization land on both their faces at nearly the same moment he'd felt it land on his own. "If this thing's airborne and it's still out there, coming home might kill them. And even if it isn't anymore, even if whatever this was has already dispersed or broken down — they don't know that. They have no way of knowing that. They've probably been sitting up there for over two weeks with a dead planet under them and absolutely nobody to ask."

John set down what he'd been holding, the full shape of the problem visibly assembling itself behind his eyes. "Do we have any way to reach them? A ham radio, a satellite uplink, anything?"

"NASA and Roscosmos ground control both talk to the station constantly, normally," Sam said. "If both of those have gone dark the same way everything else has, the crew's had no contact with anyone since this started. No mission control. No family. No idea what they're coming back to, or whether they should come back at all." He felt something tighten in his chest, a version of the same urgency he'd felt flying toward a locked bedroom in Cleveland, toward a burning airport, toward every person they'd found who'd been alone longer than anyone should have to be. "They've got consumables up there — food, water, air scrubbers — but none of that lasts forever. At some point, whether they know it's safe or not, they're going to have to make a call about coming down."

Catherine was already moving toward the whiteboard, a new column forming in her mind alongside everything else already tracked there. "We need to reach them. Even if we can't answer whether it's safe yet, they need to know someone's alive down here, thinking about them."

"And we need to actually answer whether it's safe," John added, looking at Catherine with real gratitude for the thread she'd just pulled loose out of the worst day she'd had since this all began. "If we can figure out what actually happened down here, we might be able to tell nine people in orbit whether it's safe to come home."

Sam looked between the two of them, feeling the shape of an entirely new priority snap into place alongside everything else the group was already carrying. "Then we've got two things pulling in the same direction now," he said. "Find out what this is. And find a way to talk to space before it's too late to matter."

## 28 — John, Day 17

He almost missed it entirely.

The 911 feed had been so thoroughly dry for so long that John had stopped giving it more than a cursory glance each day, most of his real attention split now between the social media filter, the ELT maritime channel, and the growing list of scientists Catherine kept adding names to. The dispatch logs had gone quiet eight days ago, the last real signal fading out somewhere around Day Ten, exactly as he'd predicted the night he'd first pitched the group on branching out to other methods.

So when the automated summary flagged a new entry, he almost scrolled past it out of pure habit, the same reflex that had learned to expect nothing from that particular well.

He stopped himself and looked again.

A single call. Portland, Oregon. Timestamped Day Sixteen — the same day, he realized with a small jolt, that he and Sam had been over the Pacific Northwest raising Yohan on the radio, the two events happening within hours of each other without either of them having any way to know it. Eight days of total silence in that feed, and then, out of nowhere, one call breaking it.

That alone would have been enough to catch his attention. A feed this dry didn't just spontaneously produce a new signal after over a week of nothing — something had changed for someone, the same way something had changed for Grace the day her plane went down, the same way something had changed for Yohan the moment he'd finally reached for that switch.

He pulled the call record and sat forward, all the low-grade fatigue of the day burning off at once. Whatever this was, it wasn't background noise. It was new. It was recent. And it was the first fresh signal that particular channel had produced in over a week, which made it, by every rule the search had taught him so far, exactly the kind of thing worth dropping everything else to chase.

He picked up the sat phone to call Catherine before he'd even finished pulling the rest of the details, already feeling the same electric pull he'd felt seventeen days ago watching a Delta flight circle an airport in Newfoundland — someone out there, right now, who needed them to be paying attention.

"Catherine," he said, the moment she picked up. "I need you to come look at something. I think we've got someone new."

## 29 — Sam, Day 17

The flight took the better part of six hours with a fuel stop worked into the middle of it, Texas unspooling brown and flat beneath him for the last long stretch, and Sam brought the PC-12 down at Ellington Field a little after one in the afternoon — NASA's own airfield, tucked a few miles from the main Johnson Space Center campus, the same runway astronauts had used for T-38 training flights for longer than he'd been alive. The field sat exactly as still as every other airport he'd touched down at these past seventeen days, hangars standing open, a handful of NASA aircraft parked in neat rows with no one left to fly them.

He grabbed a vehicle from the same kind of unmanned lot he'd learned to rely on everywhere now and drove the short distance southeast toward Clear Lake, JSC's low, sprawling campus rising up out of the flat Texas scrubland exactly the way it had in every documentary he'd half-watched as a kid dreaming about exactly this kind of career. He'd never actually been here in person. It felt strange, arriving under these circumstances, the first time this place had ever mattered to him directly.

The campus was enormous and silent, building after building standing empty in the afternoon heat, and it took him longer than he expected to find his way to what he was actually looking for — Building 30, the iconic Mission Control Center, the room that had talked every American astronaut through fifty years of spaceflight, right up through whatever had happened eighteen days ago.

The doors weren't locked. Nothing ever seemed to be, anymore.

He climbed the stairs slowly, some part of him bracing for what he might find, and stepped into the Flight Control Room itself — rows of consoles descending toward the big screens at the front, exactly as familiar from a hundred photographs as it was overwhelming to actually stand inside. The screens were dark now, powered down or simply idle, and the room held the same particular stillness every space he'd walked through since Gander had held — not quite empty, not quite abandoned, just paused, waiting for hands that weren't coming back.

He found two flight controllers at their stations, gone the way everyone was gone, and made himself move past them the same careful, respectful way he'd learned to move past every body since Cleveland — acknowledging without lingering, because lingering never once brought anyone back and there was work still in front of him.

He sat down at what he guessed, from the label still taped to the console, was the CAPCOM position — the one seat in this whole room built specifically to talk directly to a crew in orbit — and started working through the system methodically, the same way he approached any unfamiliar cockpit, hunting for what still had power, what still responded, what might actually let him do the one thing he'd flown twelve hundred miles to try.

Most of the primary displays stayed dark no matter what he tried. But down in one corner of the console, a secondary comm panel blinked to life when he found the right switch, indicator lights confirming what he'd hoped rather than expected — battery backup, or some independent circuit, still holding a connection to whatever ground antenna array fed this room its link to space.

He sat there a long moment, hand hovering over the comm switch, feeling the full weight of what he was about to attempt — a first officer with no formal training in any of this, sitting in a chair built for career flight directors, about to try reaching nine people who'd been alone in orbit for eighteen days with absolutely no idea whether anyone on the ground was still listening.

He keyed the mic anyway, because trying was the only thing that had worked for any of them so far.

"Station, this is Houston. Station, this is Houston, do you copy?"

He let the silence sit a moment, static hissing faint and patient on the open channel, and tried again.

## 30 — Sam, Day 17

He was reaching for the switch to try a third time when the static broke.

"—this is Station, we copy, we copy!" The voice came through ragged with disbelief, a woman's voice, accent unplaceable at first through the emotion cracking around the edges of it. "Is this — please confirm, is this an actual transmission from the ground?"

Sam's hand froze on the console, his whole chest seizing around the sound of an actual human voice answering him back from two hundred and fifty miles overhead. It took him a second to find his own voice again.

"Station, this is — my name's Sam. I'm not Mission Control, not officially. I'm a pilot. I flew here because we picked up radio silence from you and needed to know if anyone was still up there." He heard his voice shake and didn't fight it. "Are you okay? How many of you?"

A long pause, and when the voice came back, something in it had gone flatter, more careful, the particular control of someone bracing to say something that still cost her every time. "Five," she said. "There are five of us now. Commander Elena Voss, ISS Expedition 74." A ragged breath. "There were nine. Four of my crew made a decision ten days in, when nothing from the ground had answered anything — they took the Soyuz down. The landing itself worked. Everything about the capsule worked exactly the way it was supposed to." Her voice cracked, just once, before she steadied it by main force. "They opened the hatch, Sam. Whatever this is, it was still out there. We lost contact with the recovery beacon within an hour of touchdown. I have had to sit with not knowing exactly what that meant for ten more days, and I think I know anyway."

Sam closed his eyes, the weight of it landing somewhere he had no ready place to put it. "I'm so sorry," he said, and meant it with everything he had. "I'm so sorry you've been carrying that alone."

"I have had a great deal of practice carrying things alone lately," Voss said, and Sam heard her pull herself back together, piece by piece, the same discipline he suspected had kept the other five of her crew intact this whole time. "Tell me who you are. Tell me there's actually a reason to still believe in the ground."

"There's people," Sam said. "Not many, not compared to before. But there's people. A group of us, working to find more every day. You're not the last living humans, Commander. Far from it, I hope, but not the last."

"Thank god," she said, quiet. "Thank god." A pause, something steadying further in her voice. "Then I need you to hear me clearly, Sam. Whatever you do next — do not let anyone else come down blind. I don't care how desperate it gets up here. I have already lost four people to exactly that mistake, and I am not making it again with the five I have left."

"You won't have to," Sam said. "That's actually why I'm here. We think whatever happened was airborne, and we don't yet know if it's dissipated or how long that takes. We've got someone working that question right now — real research, not a guess. I need you to hold position until we actually have an answer, not just a hope."

"Understood," Voss said, and Sam heard something in her voice that was almost relief, the particular relief of a commander finally handing a piece of an impossible burden to someone else. "We can hold. Consumables are tighter than they were with nine of us, but we can hold. Sam — thank you. For finding us. For telling me the truth instead of just what I wanted to hear."

"That's the only thing that's worked for any of us so far," Sam said. "I'm not going anywhere. I'll keep this channel open as long as the equipment holds, and I'll check back with you the second I know anything real. You're not alone up there anymore, Commander. None of you are."

"Copy that," Voss said, and for the first time, something almost like warmth came through the static, underneath the grief still sitting heavy in her voice. "It's good to hear a voice, Sam. Any voice. You have no idea."

"I think I might," Sam said, quiet, thinking of an empty flight deck over the Atlantic seventeen days ago. "I think I might have some idea after all."

## 31 — John, Day 17

Sam's voice came through the sat phone that evening, tired but steady, relaying the whole exchange with Commander Voss almost word for word while the rest of them crowded around the phone on speaker in Marcus's office — Catherine, John, and Yohan, still finding his footing in a group that had assembled itself faster than he'd fully adjusted to yet.

"They're holding," Sam finished. "But not indefinitely. I didn't push her for exact numbers, but reading between the lines, we're looking at a real deadline here, not an open-ended one."

Catherine had gone very still, the weight of that landing directly on the thread she'd only pulled loose that morning. "Then I need to move faster than I have been," she said. "John, where are we on the scientist search?"

John pulled up the running list, thinner than he wanted it to be after two days of searching. "Six names with any real signal behind them. Two in the US, one in Toronto, one in the UK, two I haven't been able to place geographically yet — the data's thin, same as everything else has been. I've tried calling all six. Two connected. Neither picked up."

"Keep trying," Catherine said. "Every hour matters more now than it did yesterday." She rubbed at her eyes, exhaustion visible even over the phone's tinny speaker. "I can keep working with what data I have in the meantime — every death we've documented, every survivor's account of exactly what they remember from that night. It won't be definitive. But it might narrow things down enough to matter."

Yohan spoke up for the first time, his accented voice careful, still adjusting to the rhythm of a conversation this fast after so many days of silence. "I do not know medicine. I wish I had something more useful to offer than logistics."

"You might, actually," Catherine said, waving off his hesitation. "What I actually need isn't cargo — it's a real lab. University research facilities, hospital pathology departments, anywhere with the kind of equipment I can't improvise out of a first-aid kit. ## 44 — Sam, Day 24

Four days of careful flying had gotten them this far — Reno to a fuel stop in the Midwest, then up to Gander, then the long, tense crossing to Reykjavik, each leg capped by a full night's rest exactly the way Sam had promised the group back at the lodge. "Grid's down there," Yohan said quietly, watching the same dark stretch of coast Sam was. "Whatever borrowed time we have had in America, they did not have it here. Or they had less of it."

The event had happened here at eight in the morning. Highways below them weren't scattered with the occasional stalled car. Twice, Sam spotted the unmistakable black scar of an aircraft crash site, smoke still rising thin and steady from wreckage that had clearly been burning, off and on, for the better part of three weeks — regional flights, he guessed, caught at exactly the wrong altitude when their pilots went the same way everyone else had.

"Stay on mission," Sam murmured to himself, more mantra than instruction, the same words he'd repeated at Cleveland Hopkins, at every hard sight since. "Stay on mission."

Yohan glanced over, understanding without needing explanation. "It does not get easier to see," he said. "Only more familiar. That is not the same thing as easy."

Milan's outline finally resolved ahead of them, a sprawling gray city under a clear morning sky, giving no outward hint from altitude of the wreckage Sam already knew waited on every road beneath it. He began working through the approach checklist, eyes flicking toward the airport data on the panel display, already bracing himself for whatever version of chaos was waiting for them on the ground.

## 45 — Sam, Day 24

Three weeks ago, wrecking a multi-million-dollar aircraft on a grass field would have ended a career and possibly a life. "Come on," Sam said, already turning to survey the terminal buildings in the distance. "Elise is still waiting on us. We need a vehicle, and judging by everything I saw on the way in, we're going to need more than one before this day's done."

"Thank you," Voss said, and her voice broke fully this time, twenty days of carefully maintained command discipline finally, completely letting go. "Thank you for not giving up on us. Now — " she managed a small, exhausted, genuine smile, "—let's go get the other four."

## 64 — Catherine, Days 33-40

The first forty-eight hours were the ones that mattered most medically, and Catherine spent most of them not sleeping, running the same battery of tests on all five crew members over and over — vitals, bloodwork, environmental sampling off their flight suits and the capsule itself, watching obsessively for any sign that three weeks of careful theory might not hold up against the reality of five actual returning astronauts.

It held. Cleanly, completely, better than she'd let herself hope.

No fever. No exposure symptoms of any kind. Bloodwork clean across the board, environmental samples taken from their flight suits and the capsule itself showing viral concentration so low it barely registered against her equipment's detection threshold. By the third day, she finally let herself believe it fully, standing in the makeshift clinic with Elise looking over the same results with the same disbelieving relief.

"We were right," Elise said, quiet, like saying it too loudly might undo it. "Not just theoretically right. Actually, provably right."

"We were right," Catherine confirmed, and felt something in her chest finally unclench after three weeks of carrying a question she'd never fully admitted, even to herself, terrified the answer might come back wrong.

The rest of the week became something Catherine hadn't realized she'd been missing until she watched it happen — five strangers becoming five people, the particular unhurried process of a crew settling into a life none of them had ever trained for. Voss turned out to be quieter off the radio than on it, thoughtful and observant, spending long stretches on AKULA's deck just watching the water, readjusting to a horizon after weeks of watching the whole curved earth instead. Her crew scattered through the group with the same easy adaptability astronauts seemed built for, each of them finding some corner of this strange new family that fit.

But the quiet had an edge to it that Catherine couldn't miss, watching Voss closely the way she watched everyone now. Five had come home. Four hadn't — the Soyuz crew, still up there, still waiting on a re-entry option nobody on the ground had ever been able to reach in time to help. Voss carried that the way Catherine imagined a commander carried everything, contained and steady on the surface, the weight of it visible only in the moments she thought no one was looking.

Voss found Catherine on the fourth evening, the two of them sharing a quiet corner of the deck while the sun went down gold over the water.

"I keep thinking about the moment your call came through," Voss said. "Eighteen days of not knowing whether we were even going to have a choice about coming home, and then suddenly there's a voice telling me the actual truth instead of just what I wanted to hear." She looked at Catherine with something steady and grateful, and something else underneath it, harder to name. "That mattered more than the science, honestly. Knowing someone was being straight with us the whole way through."

"I almost wasn't," Catherine admitted. "Elise made me promise to tell you the real uncertainty instead of overselling it. I think that was the right call, even though every part of me wanted to just tell you it was safe and be done with the waiting."

"It was the right call," Voss said. Then, quieter, after a pause that told Catherine exactly where her mind had gone: "I wish there had been a way to tell the rest of my crew the same thing, in time for it to matter."

Catherine didn't have an answer for that. She reached over instead and put a hand on Voss's arm, and the two of them sat with it together, the ocean going dark and quiet in front of them, no words required for either of them to understand what the other was carrying.

By the end of the week, the medical monitoring had wound down to routine check-ins rather than obsessive daily panels, and the crew had settled fully into the base's rhythms — meals in the galley, watch rotations nobody had asked them to keep but that old habits apparently demanded anyway, a genuine, hard-won warmth building between everyone at Mayport and the five new faces among them, even with the grief that still sat quietly underneath all of it.

"Thank you," Voss told Catherine, on the last evening of that first careful week, "for giving the five of us a real chance at this. I know it wasn't enough for everyone. I don't think I'll ever stop wishing it had been. But it was enough for us, and I'm not going to spend the life I still have pretending that doesn't matter."

## SOYUZ CREW FATE — FINALIZED DETAIL (supersedes earlier vague note)

After 10 days with zero contact from Earth (well before Sam ever reaches Voss, which happens around Day 16-17), four of the nine ISS crew members made an independent decision to attempt return via Soyuz rather than keep waiting on a silence they had no reason to believe would ever break. They executed a technically successful de-orbit and landing in Kazakhstan — the capsule itself worked exactly as designed. When they opened the hatch, they were exposed to the pathogen, which at Day 10 was still fully active (nowhere near the ~3-week environmental decay threshold Catherine and Elise later identify as safe). All four died on/shortly after opening the hatch.

Voss has been alone up there with this loss for about a week by the time first contact is made. This significantly changes the emotional entry point of the whole ISS thread:
