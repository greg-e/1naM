## 2. Port Angeles Harbor

### 2.1 Non-anchorage area `[SRC]` 33 CFR 110.230

A single non-anchorage area (no anchoring at any time; transit by the most direct route; no dragging that could foul underwater installations):

| Point | Regulation (DMS) | Decimal |
|---|---|---|
| Start, on shore | 48°07′03.83″N, 123°24′20.67″W | 48.1177°N, 123.4057°W |
| 2 | 48°07′38.43″N, 123°24′04.67″W | 48.1273°N, 123.4013°W |
| 3 | 48°07′36.03″N, 123°23′50.67″W | 48.1267°N, 123.3974°W |
| 4, on shore | 48°06′56.73″N, 123°24′08.67″W | 48.1158°N, 123.4024°W |

A small nearshore box at the harbor's southeast corner. The canon anchor position (≈ 48.1300°N, 123.4170°W) is ~0.65 nm west-northwest of its nearest corner, outside it `[DERIVED]`. Also from the Coast Pilot `[SRC]`: log booming grounds in the north part, more than 1 nm from the west shore; deadheads and sinkers inside the Hook; Puget Sound VTS asks for notification before anchoring. A "5-fathom rock at 48°07′22″N, 123°13′18″W" in the text sits ~10 nm east of the harbor; likely a typo or a different feature `[VERIFY]`. Anchorage geometry and suitability: `research-compendium.md` §2.3.

### 2.2 Anchoring alone `[SRC]` (Wikipedia *Anchor windlass*; theseafarer.com; knowledgeofsea.com)

- A bridge remote for the windlass exists, but "it is not normally possible to operate a windlass, in a safe manner, entirely from the bridge": the chain stopper and safety pin have to be worked at the forecastle.
- Normal practice is a forecastle party at the windlass in contact with the bridge. Two methods: letting go on the brake (fast) or walking back under power (controlled). At ~15 fathoms (~27 m), many masters prefer walking back `[VERIFY]`.
- **So** `[DERIVED]`: Johan stops the engines, goes forward alone, clears the stoppers, and works the windlass himself. He'd also show anchor lights or an anchor ball and leave the bridge ready to back the engines.

### 2.3 The ship as a plant `[SRC]` / `[DERIVED]`

- **Fuel:** a container ship in hotel mode (main engine stopped, generators running) burns ~3–8 t/day `[SRC]` transportgeography.org, maritimepage.com, *Scientific Reports* 2022. She still carries thousands of tonnes.
- **Boats:** SOLAS requires rescue-boat and lifeboat davits to launch by gravity or stored mechanical power, operable by one person `[SRC]` Lloyd's Register Class News 17/2023, Marine Insight, Fountom. Her actual boats are `[VERIFY]`.
- **Radio:** a GMDSS radio room (VHF, SSB, satellite terminal) and a ~40-m mast.

*Active* as an Argus-class Offshore Patrol Cutter homeported here is an authorial choice; Argus-class specs `[VERIFY]`.

### 3.2 Practical notes `[DERIVED]`

- **Who was there at 23:00 PDT:** a 24-hour SAR, watch, and alert operation: watchstanders, helicopter and boat alert crews, Transit Protection System alert forces, and small cutter duty sections.
- **Weapons:** the station holds small arms and armed force-protection boats. The story needn't use them; noted so nobody stumbles on them by surprise.
- **Distances:** the anchorage ~0.65 nm SSW by boat; Olympic Medical Center ~3–4 mi by road; KCLM ~5 mi west.
- **Exposure:** a 3-mile sand spit with one road; winter east winds, surge, and erosion. Mount Angeles ~9 nm south at ~7° elevation.

### 4.2 Decomposition `[SRC]` (cleaning-company guides and a PMC study; approximate)

Bloat from ~3–5 days (later in cool weather); active decay with the strongest odor follows; advanced decay ~10–25 days; skeletonization weeks to years by temperature. In cool, damp mid-September Port Angeles, bodies are in active decay with heavy flies and odor; by early October, advanced decay; by about November most soft tissue is gone from bodies outdoors, slower indoors `[DERIVED]`.

### 4.3 Health and handling `[SRC]` (UN 2006; PAHO/WHO; ICRC/PAHO/WHO/IFRC *Management of Dead Bodies after Disasters*)

Dead bodies pose no significant epidemic risk; the real hazards are contaminated water, failed sanitation, and psychological harm. The field manual stresses identifying the dead and dignified handling over mass burial or cremation, with cold storage or organized temporary graves.

### 4.4 Working the 22 `[DERIVED]`

- **Body bags:** OMC supply; fire-department EMS remains pouches `[VERIFY]`; funeral homes; the station's clinic stores `[VERIFY]`.
- **PPE:** N95/P100 respirators, gloves, gowns, eye protection from OMC.
- **Identification:** name tapes, ID cards, the watch bill and duty roster; a ledger of each name and where they were found; personal effects bagged and labeled.
- **Burial options considered:** the Hook is a sand spit ~13 ft above sea level with a shallow water table, a poor burial site; sea burial is a Coast Guard tradition; cremation is impractical. Canon leaves them at the lumber buildings, unresolved.

## 6. Winter

- **Solar** `[SRC]` (Washington Solar Authority; TurbineGenerator.org; Seattle as proxy): ~1.5–2.2 peak sun hours in December vs. ~5.5 in July; December output ~25–40% of summer. Pacific Northwest off-grid designers size solar for autumn through spring and run a generator for the worst 2–3 winter months.
- **Rain shadow** `[SRC]` (Peninsula Daily News and local weather sources): Sequim ~15 in/yr, Port Angeles low-20s in, Seattle 37–38 in.
- **Wind and sea** `[SRC]` Coast Pilot: the harbor is protected from all but east winds, which blow occasionally in winter; some swell in SE gales. Atmospheric-river season starts about November `[VERIFY]`.
- Power at the base is canon-settled (`CLAUDE.md`: *Active*'s generators and the station; never jeopardy), so the old solar-sizing sketch is retired.

## 8. The abandoned container ship

**Canon:** `CLAUDE.md` Places (she stays abandoned at anchor; her fate and her 22 dead are unresolved).

**Which way she drifts** `[DERIVED]`: in an east gale she drags west, toward the west shore, the log booming grounds, and the mill and port wharves (~1.7 nm). The Hook is northeast of her, upwind, and safe in that case. Moor the yacht where a dragging ship can't reach it.

| # | Worst case, most likely first | Precedent | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nothing for a long time | Laid-up ships sit at anchor for months to years | She rusts; lights and AIS go dark when her power dies |
| 2 | Drags in a winter east gale and grounds | High-windage ships drag; soft bottom, gentle shoals `[DERIVED]` | Hull damage, possible fuel leak, shifted stacks |
| 3 | Containers overboard | *Rena* (2011): toxins washing up six years later `[SRC]` Maritime NZ/TAIC | Floating debris, a hazard to small boats |
| 4 | Fire in the containers | Lithium-ion and misdeclared cargo; "a major container-ship fire roughly every 17 days worldwide" `[SRC]` Motorship/Marine Link; *X-Press Pearl* (2021) burned nearly two weeks `[SRC]` | Burns until spent; thermal runaway needs no power `[DERIVED]` |
| 5 | Grounded, holed, spill, breakup | *Rena*: 1,733 t HFO aboard, ~200–350 t spilled, ~50 km of coast oiled, broke in two after three months `[SRC]` | Years of oil on the Hook's beach; nobody to clean it |

**Securing her before leaving** `[DERIVED]`: let go the second anchor and pay out more scope; stop the engine and isolate fuel; close the watertight doors; leave battery anchor lights; record the cargo manifest, a possible later supply source.
