# DAL27: A350 incapacitation, Edmonton diversion and automation dependencies

**Research snapshot: November 7, 2026 (EST).** Novel-only preliminary technical review, not a flight plan, operating procedure or certification of a landing. Read-only repository work; no edits, copy, messages or handoff. Bruh's concurrent Della work and all held PC-12/ISS choices are untouched. ## 1. Bottom line

The central sequence works in principle: a serviceable A350 continues on engaged automation; a current, experienced first officer returns, makes the decisions, manages an incapacitation emergency and lands. It is **not** a nonpilot learning an airliner or a passenger pressing an autonomous rescue button. An official investigation documents an A350-941 first officer completing a single-pilot approach and manual landing with automation after captain incapacitation.[1] That example supports capability, not the probability of success with DAL27's much greater loss of assistance.

**Strongest documented factual problem:** the airport operator lists **full front-course ILS on Edmonton runways02,12 and30, but a back-course approach on20**.[2] The manuscript repeatedly calls the selected arrival “ILS Two-Zero.” That is not the documented baseline. A350 guidance can support other approach types; changing a label alone does not validate the present minimums, missed approach or setup.[3]

Other important gaps are aircraft-specific fuel-jettison equipment, overweight/autoland limitations, exact performance inputs and oxygen endurance. These should not overturn the intended landing, but the current precision exceeds the research foundation.

## 5. What keeps going—and what only Sam supplies

At01:27, autopilot1, autothrust, flight-plan navigation and FL360 are explicitly established. The occupants' incapacitation is not itself an avionics failure. Belts and body positions matter because interference with controls could change that premise; the text already describes arms loose rather than a sustained control input. No new jam/failure is required.

The aircraft's present state is not a standing order to descend at Seoul, configure, inspect a runway and land without supervision. Likewise, optional **automatic emergency descent** is not a general medical-incapacitation diversion system: FAA training material treats it as a distinct installed function.[9] Do not add or require it merely because nobody answers the cabin. Airframe-specific pilot-inactivity alerting and installed automation logic were not obtained; this review does not certify a completely silent75-minute interval.

EASA recognizes Cat3 precision approach/autoland capability for the type.[8] That does not grant every airport Cat3 infrastructure, remove aircraft/operator limitations, or make the airplane independently decide which runway is usable. Airbus's2026 public research description explicitly separates conventional ground/satellite-supported systems from future vision-assisted autonomy.[7]

**Useful precedent, bounded:** Hong Kong AAIA08-2023 describes captain incapacitation on an A350-941. The FO used the autopilot and checklists, then chose a familiar manual landing with autothrust over autoland; it was successful.[1] ATC, cabin assistance, company support and a conscious captain were available. Those are not available to Sam. The precedent validates the kind of competence, not interchangeable circumstances or a guaranteed outcome. It also supplies a better basis for Sam choosing to hand-fly than an unverified blanket prohibition.

Sam's existing out-loud briefing, checklist use and refusal to leave the airplane unattended while searching for help are strong workload details. Twenty-five minutes of attempts while continuing away from airports is nevertheless an intentional judgment cost. A pilot review should consider whether the same characterization needs fewer attempts before selecting a field; no authoritative source provides a universal allowable number of minutes here. Do not turn this into a fresh emergency or presume his reasoned persistence is impossible.

## 7. Airport suitability: the highest-leverage choice

Edmonton's published operator facts confirm02/20 at11,000ft,12/30 at10,200ft, major-airport facilities and full front-course ILS on02/12/30.[2] This supports why Sam considers Edmonton rather than a shorter runway. The source's marketing phrase “any size of aircraft” is **not** permission for every weight/weather/runway-state combination.

**The runway-end problem:** using20 for a southbound arrival is understandable geometrically, but the official baseline is not ILS20. Airbus's current xLS documentation says every A350 has FLS and discusses LOC back-course/RNP guidance distinct from ILS.[3] This means “not ILS” does **not** mean “no usable approach” or “no autopilot assistance.” It does mean the current approach briefing cannot stand merely by retaining an ILS label and its alleged decision height/missed-approach details.

Author options, not automatic edits:

- Keep20, but select and verify the actual approach/guidance, supported minima and visual/manual transition for the chosen configuration.
- Select an ILS-equipped runway end, subject to credible wind, routing and landing performance—not automatically02 because it is the opposite end of the same pavement.
- Deliberately establish a2028 airport upgrade; then it is fictional selected infrastructure, not a claim about the2026 airport.

No current complete approach plate or fictional2028 weather/NOTAM set was obtained; this review does not certify any option's final course, altitudes or missed approach. The scene's airfield lights can remain a fictional observation. Lights on do not prove that ILS, weather sensors, rescue vehicles, unobstructed pavement and landing services all work. Conversely, missing tower replies do not mechanically switch every navigation transmitter off.

Sam may arrive to operating lights/transmitters and no staff if the chosen infrastructure remains serviceable. There is no researched fixed unattended-airport shutdown clock here. His observation and instrument indications, rather than an omniscient guarantee, should support each next decision. No new blockage, weather event or outage is required.

## 8. Weight, stopping and postflight consequences

The aircraft-specific load sheet is missing: actual dry operating mass, passengers/baggage/cargo, takeoff fuel, center of gravity and weight variant are unknown. The dossier's secondary-source142.4t empty mass is not a verified Delta dry operating mass. Do not combine it with assumed full loads as if a dispatch document.

**Arithmetic only:**530,000lb ×0.45359237 ≈240,404kg =240.4t. Against an applicable207t MLW, excess is33.4metric tonnes, about73,643lb. Thus prose “more than thirty-three tons” works if **tonnes** are intended; US short tons would differ. The discovery's66tons fuel and later mass are not inherently inconsistent, but cannot prove a burn schedule without zero-fuel mass and units. “Eleven hours” is a condition-dependent endurance estimate, not simply fuel divided by a permanent consumption constant.

MLW is an approved operating/structural limit, not an instant-failure threshold. Airbus explains design margins and event-specific evaluation after abnormal loads, including hard overweight landings.[12] However, the dossier's “certified to land overweight” is too broad. An emergency procedure/possible successful landing is not blanket normal certification at any excess weight. No approved A350 overweight/autoland instruction was obtained; A330 allowances and an online A350-1000 manual mirror were not used as substitutes.

The FAA A350 table's jettison entry is sufficient to challenge a universal “A350 has no way to dump fuel,” but does not identify DAL27's installed system.[9] Resolve **this aircraft's equipment**, not the entire family from recollection. A no-jettison fictional configuration may be an author choice; its validity/Delta applicability remains to verify. If equipped, urgency versus time reducing weight becomes a pilot decision, not an automatic demand to circle or dump. No jettison procedure is supplied.

Runway length alone cannot generate172kt,8,200ft rollout or a brake-temperature ceiling. Airbus's brake-testing explanation includes weight/loading, configuration, lift/drag, deceleration, thrust/reversers, braking model and runway friction; brake condition matters too.[13,14] Surface/wind, touchdown position and actual speed also determine what margin remains. Published length is not automatically landing distance available after declared-distance restrictions. No exact performance model for Yellowknife/Fort McMurray or Edmonton was obtained.

Retain the consequential trade: **more runway and less braking energy versus additional time aloft and finite crew oxygen**. The reader does not need several invented-to-the-unit outputs. If author-generated EFB results remain, their inputs/equipment must be chosen and an A350 specialist should validate them. Hot brakes are a legitimate concern, not an automatic fire prediction or proof500° is universally safe. Monitoring afterward is already a good beat. The A350 is left behind, so no new return-to-service plot is needed; landing success would not by itself certify later airworthiness.[12]
