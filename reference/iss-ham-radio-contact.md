# ISS Contact Post-Collapse: The Ham Radio Path

## Premise
In a scenario where terrestrial infrastructure (power grids, NASA Mission Control, satellite relay ground stations) has failed, amateur ("ham") radio is the most technically plausible surviving link between Earth and a crewed ISS.

## Why It Survives When Everything Else Doesn't
- The ISS carries dedicated ham radio equipment (Kenwood TM-D710E and TM-D710GA transceivers) as part of the ARISS program.
- ARISS's stated purpose explicitly includes serving as a **contingency communications system** for NASA and the crew — it was built to be independent of the primary comms chain.
- The station's radios run on solar power, so they don't depend on any Earth-based electrical grid.
- Four separate antenna systems on the Russian Service Module give built-in redundancy — the link can survive individual antenna failures.
- Unlike Mission Control's link, which routes through NASA's TDRSS satellites and staffed ground terminals at White Sands, NM, the ham link is direct: station to a ground operator, no intermediate relay infrastructure required.

## What Breaks First (the realistic bottleneck)
- **Not the space hardware — the ground side.** A surviving link needs a person on Earth with:
  - A working 2-meter/70cm transceiver
  - A power source independent of the grid (solar + battery, since fuel-dependent generators fail as supply chains collapse)
  - Basic orbital tracking capability
- Without power grids, tools like Heavens-Above or N2YO disappear. A survivor community would need to have pre-collapse downloaded/printed orbital data (TLEs), or fall back to naked-eye tracking — the ISS is bright and moving fast at dawn/dusk, and its ~90-minute orbital period is predictable short-term but degrades in accuracy over time without fresh data.

## Operating Constraints (for realism in a story)
- **Pass windows are short.** A typical overhead pass lasts 8–10 minutes total, with a strong-signal usable window of only 4–6 minutes.
- **Elevation matters more than location.** Passes above 30° elevation give the longest, clearest contact; below 20°, terrain and buildings often block it entirely.
- **No continuous contact.** Once the ISS drops below the horizon, the link is gone until the next pass — anywhere from ~90 minutes to several hours later depending on geometry. "Ongoing comms" is really a series of short check-ins, not a persistent channel.
- **Doppler shift.** The downlink frequency shifts roughly ±9 kHz across a single pass; operators historically use precomputed AOS/TCA/LOS frequency corrections.
- **Basic gear works.** Even a handheld radio with a stock antenna can make contact during a good pass — the barrier is having any operational gear and power, not needing exotic equipment.

## Story-Useful Tension Points
- A single working ham station becomes an extremely high-value asset — arguably one of the last threads to an outside world.
- The "who gets to talk to the crew" question becomes a source of conflict if multiple survivor groups have equipment.
- The crew's dependence on this thin link contrasts with their actual survival bottleneck: consumables (food/water/reboost capability) rather than comms — the radio doesn't feed them, it just tells them how alone they are.
- A pre-collapse character with amateur radio licensing/experience becomes a uniquely important, previously "ordinary" survivor.

## Confidence Note
All hardware/frequency/procedural details above are drawn from documented ARISS/NASA/ARRL sources (high confidence). Anything about how this would unfold in an actual societal collapse is speculative extrapolation for story purposes, not documented contingency planning.
