# Johan Brandt

### 9/5/2028, 06:10 ship's time

Nobody woke him for the watch change.

After twenty-six years Johan rarely needed the knock at his door, but this time he woke late, the light through the porthole already gray instead of black. The *Pacific Tender* was five days out of Yokohama, bound for Seattle, and he'd turned in straight after supper to get his sleep before the midnight watch.

He dressed and went up to the bridge expecting to find Okafor irritated with him, ready with some dry comment about second mates who slept through their own relief, and found him slumped forward against the console, one hand on the autopilot panel. Johan felt for a pulse and found none. The ship was on autopilot, on course, at nineteen knots.

The watch alarm on the bulkhead should have rung every twelve minutes until the man on watch reset it, and if he didn't, rung in the officers' cabins. Its key was turned to off. Okafor kept it off in open water. Johan had spoken to him about that twice.

The ship had changed her date the morning before, ahead of the line, and her clocks ran eleven hours behind Greenwich now. The last entry in the bridge log was just before seven the previous evening. Johan worked it to Greenwich: six in the morning, Tuesday.

He found Second Engineer Alvarez dead in the engine control room, upright in his chair with a logbook open in front of him, the entry stopped mid-sentence. He found the cook dead against the galley counter, a pot on the stove gone dry and scorched black. He went through the accommodation block after that, cabin by cabin, and found them all the same way, no struggle, no mess. It took almost two hours. He was the only man alive aboard the *Pacific Tender*.

He thought of the ship first: a CO2 discharge, or something leaking out of a container and into the ventilation. He took a gas meter from the safety locker and carried it through the accommodation and the control room. Oxygen 20.9, no CO, no H2S. In the CO2 room the bottles were sealed and the release cabinet shut. The dangerous goods list showed nothing stowed near the air intakes. He had eaten the same supper and breathed the same air.

He called on VHF channel 16 and got static. He called on the single-sideband, on 2182 and the coast station frequencies, and got the same. The AIS target list showed five other vessels within thirty miles. He called each one by name on 16, then sent a DSC call to its MMSI, and got no voice and no acknowledgment. He watched the list for fifteen minutes. Course and speed on all five didn't change by more than a degree or a tenth of a knot.

The satellite terminal that carried the crew's internet was the last thing he tried, and for twenty minutes it gave him almost nothing, pages loading in broken pieces. He got Reuters, the *Weser-Kurier*, a Japanese shipping paper. The newest item on each was stamped a few minutes before six that morning, Greenwich, the hour the bridge log stopped, and in Greenwich it was past eight in the evening now. It could have been the terminal, handing him stored pages over a bad link. He got one message off to Lena in Hamburg, *Are you all right. Answer me*, and it showed as sent. Then the connection gave out and didn't come back.

He sat a long time at the GMDSS console at the back of the bridge. He would have to look up the cook's name on the crew manifest, and he was ashamed of that.

The engine was sound, and she had the fuel to reach the Strait of Juan de Fuca. The course on the autopilot would take her there. He was a second mate, a navigator. He knew the bridge and the deck; the engine room he knew from drills and the alarm panel.

He went back down and stood at that panel, with Alvarez in the chair beside him. It showed no alarms. The engine room had run itself all night with nobody in it, which was how she was built to run, as long as an engineer came when the panel called. He switched its calls through to the bridge.

At nineteen knots she would raise the Strait in a little over four days. He took the revolutions down from the bridge a few at a time, as fast as the load program allowed, and it was most of an hour before she settled at eleven and a half. That made it seven days, with the engine working easily and more time to act on anything that came up on the radar.

He went forward to the helm and took her off autopilot and steered by hand for a few minutes.

He put her back on course. In the bridge log, under Okafor's last entry, he wrote the position, course 075, speed 11.5, and *Proceeding Seattle.*
