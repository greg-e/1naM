# John, Pandora, and responsibility for crossing a boundary

## 1. Question and short conclusion

What does John knowingly risk when he disables Pandora and directs searches of restricted records to find survivors?

**“I could go to prison” is credible; “changing this setting automatically sends me to prison” is not.** Separate changing a local control, accessing an outside system, obtaining/using information, and causing harm. Their legal elements differ. Rescue matters, but does not itself create authorization or guarantee a defense. The immediate moral danger is harming records and services belonging to the people he hopes are alive.

Preserve the settled override. Make John accept responsibility for a rescue decision, not declare law or other people's rights extinct.

## 3. Supported legal exposure

### Authorization is not simply obedience

18 U.S.C. §1030(a)(2) covers intentional unauthorized access, or exceeding authorized access, that obtains specified information; (a)(2)(C) reaches information from a “protected computer,” broadly defined through commerce/communications in (e)(2). Profit and damage are not prerequisites. [1]

In *Van Buren v. United States*, the Supreme Court distinguished obtaining information from off-limits computer areas from obtaining information one is entitled to access for an improper purpose. A Georgia officer's forbidden-purpose database lookup did not satisfy this CFAA provision. **That limits this offense; it does not legalize all misuse.** The Court left unresolved whether relevant access limits must be technological or may include contracts/policies: slip opinion pp.13–14 & n.8, 20. [2]

DOJ guidance is narrower still: §B.2 requires computationally established areas, knowing entry into an unconditionally prohibited area, and a sufficient federal interest. §C rejects mere employee-use/terms-of-service violations as the basis for these CFAA prosecutions. It also explains why obtaining a response does not establish permission. This is **charging policy, not enforceable immunity**. [3]

Application: the toggle alone does not establish these elements. Knowingly directing entry into systems/areas outside his permission and obtaining records creates potential CFAA exposure. Merely repurposing information he was entitled to retrieve changes that analysis. Preserve Pandora without treating it as a magic access grant or proof of the eventual legal case.

### Prison is possible, not a predicted sentence

For a first §1030(a)(2) offense, §1030(c)(2)(A) provides a default maximum of one year; (c)(2)(B) raises it to five years in specified circumstances, including furtherance of another criminal/tortious act or information valued above $5,000. Sensitivity or volume alone does not prove those conditions. Government-computer access under (a)(3) has separate elements; a county system is not automatically federal. [1]

Georgia supplies another plausible concern. O.C.G.A. §16-9-93(c) addresses intended examination of another person's medical, employment, financial, or other personal data knowing the examination is without authority. Private emergency-call details could implicate it, depending on facts and jurisdiction. Subsection (h)(1) permits **up to fifteen years and/or $50,000** upon conviction. This is a statutory ceiling, not John's likely sentence. Subsection (b)'s computer trespass requires its own specified intent involving removal, interference, alteration, or malfunction; reading is not automatically trespass. [4]

No sentencing estimate is supportable. Do not multiply maximums by counties/instances or infer recidivism from his youth. Discipline, loss of trust/access, and possible civil claims are additional categories, not established outcomes; CFAA civil claims have conditions in §1030(g).

### Rescue intent does not erase damage or human responsibility

Section 1030(a)(5) distinguishes intentional damage, reckless damage following unauthorized access, and unauthorized access causing damage and loss. “Damage” includes impaired integrity **or availability**, not only destroyed hardware: (e)(8). Medical-care impairment and public-safety threats appear among penalty factors in (c)(4). Read-only intent does not establish zero operational effect; thirty-six searches do not establish damage. [1]

18 U.S.C. §2(b) makes someone who willfully causes an otherwise criminal act punishable as a principal. That is a general reason “the model did it” is not automatic insulation. It is not an AI-specific ruling: attribution, causation and the offense's mental-state requirements still need proof. Unforeseen model behavior is not automatically John's intentional crime. [5]

Do not casually add fraud, espionage, or HIPAA charges; their predicates have not been established.

## 4. Professional and moral obligations

Google's published Code, updated July 16, 2026, expects employee compliance and permits discipline including termination. §I.3 requires authorized collection/use/access of user information; §IV protects confidential information, including partners'; §V.3 requires security-policy compliance; §VII requires legal compliance. Trusted access is not personal entitlement. This does **not** reveal John's internal rules/contract or prove a particular 2028 violation. [6]

FAR 52.239-1(c) illustrates a real public-sector contractual obligation: notify the other party when safeguards cease functioning or new threats arise. **Only if applicable/incorporated**; it binds the contractor under that contract, not every architect personally. No such clause is established for John. [7]

The ACM Code supplies genuine ethical tension. §2.8 allows access when authorized **or compelled by the public good**; public accessibility alone is insufficient. §2.3 says rule-breakers must consider consequences and accept responsibility. §§1.2,1.6,3.7 require avoiding harm, minimizing personal information and special infrastructure stewardship. This is ethical guidance, not legal permission; John's ACM membership is unknown. [8]

His choice is not necessarily mindless compliance versus unlimited access. A compelling rescue purpose can coexist with responsibility, narrow collection, confidentiality, and protection of working services. Having helped create Pandora makes the conflict intimate, not automatically more criminal.

## 5. Emergency and necessity: serious argument, no blanket permission

CFAA §1030(f) protects **lawfully authorized** investigative/protective/intelligence activity of specified agencies. John's employment does not establish that status. DOJ's good-faith-security-research policy concerns access solely for testing/investigating/correcting security flaws, designed to avoid harm and promote relevant security/safety. Survivor searches are not automatically that research merely because he once researched vulnerabilities. [1,3]

Federal necessity is offense- and fact-sensitive. *United States v. Bailey*, an escape case, identifies a reasonable legal alternative as defeating the defense, 444 U.S.394,409–410 (1980). *United States v. Dicks*, 338 F.3d1256,1257–1258 (11th Cir.2003), discusses extraordinary-circumstances justification and reasonable alternatives, while **assuming without deciding** its availability for the immigration offense charged. Its slip p.4 n.1 reproduces a four-part firearms justification test. Neither decides a CFAA rescue case; do not present that firearms test as a settled CFAA rule. [9,10]

Georgia's §16-3-20 includes reason-and-justice justification. In *Tarvestad v. State*, 261 Ga.605–606 (1991), evidence that an unlicensed driver took his pregnant wife for medical care warranted a justification instruction. The reversal concerned that missing instruction, not universal immunity for rescuers. This meaningfully counters “emergencies never matter” without authorizing John's access. [11,12]

For John: unanswered calls, expiring records, failing infrastructure and possibly injured survivors make delay consequential. A reviewer could ask what realistic authorized/public alternatives remained, why a broad search was necessary, what harm it risked, and when it should stop. Establishing an imminent rescue need is harder before a particular injured caller is found. That makes justification uncertain, not his choice irrational.

He cannot know every authorizer is dead while seeking living people. “Nobody has answered” is established; universal absence of anyone able to authorize is not. Future institutional accountability remains a rational fear.

## 6. Scene translation — suggestions, not canon or replacement prose

Keep the pause, personal connection to Pandora, restart and successful control search. Add weight through only a few beats:

- **Recognition:** an authorization warning is not a technical fault. He built the refusal for a reason.
- **Exposure:** briefly acknowledge possible prosecution/prison if authorities return, without a statutory lecture or guaranteed sentence. His earlier near-prosecution can supply emotional memory without invented details.
- **Present harm:** a caller may need the service he risks interrupting; a recorded voice/address belongs to a person, not an abstract dataset. His own returned call is an existing mirror.
- **Responsibility:** consider recording his decision and reason, retaining the fact of missing permission, limiting use/disclosure, and stopping outside rescue needs. These are accountability choices, not legal shields or concealment advice.

**Recommendation:** mention prison briefly, but let the deeper fear be appointing himself to decide whose privacy and safety may be risked. He acts because waiting could abandon someone, not because consequences disappeared. Do not remove the settled override or introduce a delay-heavy procedural subplot.
