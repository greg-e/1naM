# Della's trauma care: assessment, imaging and treatment dependencies

**Research date: November 7, 2026 (EST).** Novel-only preliminary plausibility review, not patient-specific medical advice, an imaging interpretation or a treatment protocol. The evidence can support the intended survival/recovery plot with explicit assumptions; it cannot certify an unseen scan or outcome. No repository edits, messages, handoff or copy. Held flight/ISS choices and the dependable Hook-power premise are untouched.

## 1. Bottom line

Catherine's assessment, stabilization, bedside ultrasound, fracture reduction/immobilization and continuing observations fit an emergency physician's role. A splenic injury and a small traumatic pneumothorax **do not automatically require operations**. Conversely, routine nonoperative-trauma results come from systems with monitoring and rescue capabilities, not one doctor and two untrained helpers. Their absence changes the risk Catherine must accept; it does not prove Della cannot survive.[1,2,10]

The central missing bridge is **how Catherine obtains diagnostically useful CT images**, especially contrast-enhanced abdominal imaging, then combines them with Della's response and subsequent examinations. “Console came up” and “trauma protocol” do not establish those conditions. This is a plausibility gap, not a demonstrated impossibility.

The clearest unsupported medical certainty is “you'll have pneumonia by Sunday.” Painful shallow breathing creates a preventable risk; neither a missed exercise nor a guideline supplies that calendar deadline. It can remain deliberate bedside pressure, but should not become the author's forecast.[10]

## 4. What Catherine can conclude—and should keep checking

The existing mechanism/exam makes prioritizing chest, circulation and abdomen credible. Repeated pulse checks during movement are valuable on-page competence. Oxygen improving a displayed saturation is useful evidence but does not settle the cause of hypoxemia or exclude worsening injury.[10] No new lung injury is inferred.

A single Hb around ten is compatible with blood loss but cannot quantify it without baseline, hydration and clinical context. Abbott's representative IFU explains that hematocrit changes with both red-cell loss and plasma-volume changes; its Hb is calculated, not an independent measurement.[8] Saturday's 10.1 is best read as **not a material downward trend in context**, rather than proof she has regenerated blood or is safe because the decimal rose. Serial blood pressure, examination, urine output, breathing and overall response strengthen the decision. They need not all become dialogue.

“Kidneys are behind, that's two days of not drinking enough” (08:129) is a reasonable working explanation, not an established exclusive cause. NICE identifies hypovolemia, infection and recent NSAID use as relevant renal-risk factors; Della reports repeated ibuprofen and reduced urine.[9] Tablet strength, frequency and total amount are unspecified: do not diagnose overdose from “four at a time.” Retain Catherine's judgment and reassessment rather than adding invented renal values or dialysis.

The belt-shaped abdominal bruise matters independently of the spleen. WSES recommends CT/high suspicion and serial examination for concerning mechanisms or nonspecific findings.[3] A known splenic tear does not explain every abdominal finding automatically. This supports Catherine's existing observation period; it does **not** establish an additional bowel injury or require a complication subplot.

Likewise, “Brain's clean” can mean no acute abnormality she identifies on CT. CDC notes that concussion need not be visible on a scan.[14] There is no contradiction between a reassuring head CT and the canonical concussion/brief blackout. Do not convert the shorthand into “no head injury,” or connect trauma symptoms to the protected event mechanism.

## 5. CT is a chain, not a button

OMC's current public service page confirms CT, ultrasound and digital X-ray capabilities and certified technologists.[4] It does not identify the story's scanner/software, show the relevant emergency circuit, confirm the actual hospital configuration in 2028, or establish a running unattended service.

A representative Siemens **SOMATOM Definition AS / syngo CT VB20** operator manual documents distinct dependencies:[5]

- Whole-system electrical supply versus a UPS that temporarily supports computers; a live console can coexist with an interrupted measuring system.
- Cooling/room conditions and functional equipment checks/calibration, not only a running generator.
- Appropriate patient positioning/coverage, acquisition and reconstruction; the correct examination/protocol for the clinical question.
- Competent operation, radiation protection, patient observation, and diagnostic image quality.
- Usable local images/viewing/export, whether or not the wider hospital record/archive service remains reachable.

These are **representative design requirements**, not proof OMC uses that scanner. No electrical settings, scanner commands, injection parameters or workaround for interlocks/access controls is supplied. Do not invent a generic hospital-fuel countdown or a cloud requirement that forces CT offline.

The source is also useful contrary evidence: it expressly allows **trained physicians** among qualified operators. Catherine need not become a radiographer or have an unseen colleague appear. But “stood behind enough of them” is the weak bridge. A minimal scene can establish model-specific familiarization/local instructions and a successful readiness check, while admitting the scan/read is her own provisional assessment. A manual supports work; it does not confer training, make unsafe trial-and-error acceptable or guarantee interpretation.

**Contrast is the largest omitted clinical dependency.** EAST's diagnostic recommendation is abdominal CT **with IV contrast** in the appropriate stable patient.[1] Contrast helps assess organ perfusion and active/contained vascular injury; noncontrast head imaging answers a different question. The manuscript's single “trauma protocol” cannot establish all acquisition coverage or phases. Omission is not proof Catherine scanned without contrast, but the reader is asked to trust conclusions whose evidentiary basis is currently invisible.

The actual downloaded **2026 ACR Manual on Contrast Media** requires patient-specific risk/benefit assessment and readiness for reactions, with suitable personnel/equipment.[6] Catherine's emergency skills are relevant; scanner operation, contrast delivery and patient monitoring still compete for her attention. No reaction is predicted and no dose or technique is recommended.

Renal concern is **not an automatic contrast veto**. ACR treats relevant renal risk as relative, not absolute, and stresses the cost of missing an important diagnosis; NICE's 2024 update cautions against delaying emergency contrast when delay would be clinically significant.[6,9] Thus do not “fix” the scene by banning contrast for dehydration or declaring it harmless. The defensible author choice is a deliberate clinician decision with actual supplies and diagnostic-quality images.

## 6. Observation without the normal rescue system

EAST's nonoperative splenic-management setting includes monitoring, repeated evaluations and an operating room available for urgent surgery.[1] WSES's later consensus includes serial physiology/laboratory assessment, grade-dependent imaging and access to experienced intervention.[2] Neither source validates a success probability for permanent loss of staff and rescue services.

Here, the CT may provide diagnostic information, but **it cannot stop bleeding**. An empty operating room is not an available operative team. Transfusion capability, qualified assistance, surgical/anesthetic backup and embolization are not established. This is the strongest confirmed **mismatch between ordinary guideline prerequisites and the fictional resource setting**, not proof the conservative choice is wrong or Della dies. Her only clinician may reasonably take the best available path when the preferred backup simply does not exist.

Saturday is not a magic clearance date. WSES's suggested observation and follow-up differ by grade and ongoing status; its higher-grade imaging recommendation concerns contrast-enhanced ultrasound/CT, not any handheld grayscale scan.[2] Do not apply those schedules mechanically to Della, whose grade is unstated and whose first assessment occurs almost two days after injury. Time since impact and time under reliable observation are different.

No apparent increase in bedside fluid is reassuring but not interchangeable with excluding a delayed vascular problem. Conversely, current guidance does not require routine repeat CT in every improving low-grade injury. Catherine can have a sound conditional reason to transfer without proving that repeated scans are always necessary or that one absent follow-up is malpractice.

The existing oral intake, improving observations, protected transport, supplies and read-back support recovery. The next decision needing definition is **what reassuring combination makes Catherine willing to leave John responsible during Huntsville**, including what remains uncertain and which changes would alter her plan. Do not assign John new invasive skills or promise a radio/monitor substitutes for intervention. His monitoring can be useful without becoming a miniature ICU.

## 7. Limb, wound and respiratory recovery

The ankle sequence already contains strong details: initial distal circulation/movement assessment, X-rays, post-reduction imaging, splint rather than immediate circumferential cast, swelling/blisters, and explicit recognition that ideal surgery is unavailable (08:37,137–139). BOA additionally stresses documented circulation/sensation **after** reduction and maintained alignment on follow-up.[12] Those checks can occur off-page; they are not shown, not proven absent.

AAOS supports that trimalleolar/dislocated patterns are often unstable and usually need surgical treatment.[11] Nonoperative salvage is plausible under necessity, but a good first post-reduction image does not prove it will hold. The shin wound's relationship to the fracture and soft-tissue condition needs orthopaedic review; do not silently relabel a closed fracture as open or vice versa.

The existing reference's **10–12 weeks** resembles AAOS's general complete bone-healing range, not a guarantee for this untreated unstable pattern. AAOS's general **3–4 months to normal activities is not a promise of running** after Della's particular injury.[11] Cast removal, union, weight-bearing, endurance and return to heavy mechanical work are different milestones. A lasting limp is a plausible chosen outcome, not something this audit can predict. Preserve the intended disability/recovery arc; do not add an operating team to erase it.

The infected shin wound has a clinically meaningful management thread, not merely an antibiotic bag. CDC supports assessment, cleaning/debridement and treatment of infection, while tetanus vaccination and possible immune globulin depend on wound/immunization history.[13] Her history is not stated; the shot may be appropriate, and missing documentation is not proof a missing treatment. No antibiotic selection, dose, invasive technique or new complication is proposed.

For ribs, pain control enabling breathing/coughing and ongoing respiratory assessment have a clear rationale; excessive opioid effect can itself impair breathing.[10] The incentive device is a reminder/assessment aid within care, not a pneumonia-proof shield or an exact Sunday clock. Keep the repeated effort and John's watchfulness. Their emotional function is stronger than a deterministic threat.

Bleeding risk, immobility and rehabilitation also require an individualized thrombosis-prevention assessment.[2,12] This is a specialist-review gap, not a recommendation to add medication or a clot. Catherine's hidden opioid history is not evidence that she is currently impaired; do not manufacture clinical incompetence to explain missing infrastructure.
