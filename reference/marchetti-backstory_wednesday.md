# Elise Marchetti

### 9/6/2028, 08:40 CEST - Bellinzona

The power had gone off in the night. She had woken at a quarter to two to a silence that took her a while to place: the refrigerator, and under it the hum of the town that she had never heard until it stopped. In the morning the kitchen was gray, and the radio on the windowsill was dead, and Paolo lay under the tablecloth where she had put him.

She took a shower in water that was already going cold, put on trousers and a blouse and her flat shoes, and took her glasses and her car keys.

It took her an hour to drive the three kilometers to the hospital. Every street had stopped cars on it. Most of the engines had run themselves dry overnight, and the horns had gone quiet with the batteries. Twice she got out, released a handbrake, and pushed a small car to the curb to get past. A city bus stood at its stop on the Viale with its doors open and its passengers in their seats. The traffic lights were dark.

The hospital was running on its generators. She could hear them from the car park. The emergency department at San Giovanni had changed shift at seven on Tuesday, and she found the day staff at the nurses' station and in the corridor and in the bays, and the patients who had come in overnight still on their trolleys, and the waiting room full. She went through to the back. She looked for anyone alive and didn't find anyone, and after that she looked for the intensive care unit.

The ICU was quiet except for the alarms. Six beds, four occupied, a nurse on the floor by the second. The patient in bed four was intubated, and the ventilator was still cycling on emergency power, pushing air into a man who had been dead for a day. She found the silence button on the central monitor and pressed it, and then she sat down at the station and opened his trend.

The central station kept three days of trends for every bed. She ran the cursor back to 07:55 on Tuesday. Heart rate 84. Blood pressure 118 over 70. Oxygen saturation 99 percent, on 40 percent oxygen. At 08:00 the heart rate began to climb, then fell, 60, 40, wide ugly complexes, and by 08:06 the trace was flat. The blood pressure went with it. The saturation stayed at 98 and 99 percent until the pulse was too weak for the sensor to read.

She looked at that for a long time. His blood had been full of oxygen when he died. She had seen that trace exactly once before, in a case conference: a lab worker in Milan and a spill of potassium cyanide.

She went back through the other three beds and found the same trace on each, the same minute. She photographed the screens with her phone, and then she took a pen from the station and wrote the numbers on the back of a nursing chart as well, because she didn't trust the phone to last.

**10:15**

From the department's stores she took a box of blood tubes, the plain ones and the purple tops and the gray, needles, gloves, a marker, and a cooler from the blood bank with ice packs from its freezer, which was still cold. She took her samples from the femoral vein, the way it was done after death, from eleven patients who had wristbands with their names and dates of birth, so that there would never be any question of who they were. She wrote each name and *5/9 08:00* on the tubes. She didn't take any from the staff. She didn't know why that mattered to her, and she didn't stop to think about it.

A day was late. Blood changed after death, and some of what she would look for could be made by the body itself in the hours afterward, or lost. She wrote the hours since death on each label too, so that whoever read the results, and it would be her, would know what they were worth.

The lividity had set along the backs of their arms and shoulders, where their weight had lain since Tuesday morning, and it was bright pink-red. She turned the nurse in the ICU onto her side and found the same color along her back. Carbon monoxide would do that. Cyanide would do that. Nothing in a hospital or a river valley would put either of them into everyone in the same minute.

She sat at the nurses' station in the emergency department with a tourniquet around her own left arm, holding the end of it in her teeth, and drew three tubes of her own blood. On the labels she wrote her name, the date, the time, 11:10, and the word *viva*.

**12:30**

The institute was in the new building on Via Chiesa, glass and pale stone, with the parking almost empty. The generator was running behind it. Her badge opened the door. About three hundred people worked there, and at eight o'clock on Tuesday perhaps thirty of them had come in. She found the porter at his desk inside the door and didn't count the rest.

Giulia, her technician, was on the floor of the lab in front of the biosafety cabinet with a pipette still in her hand. The cabinet's fan was running and its light was on. Elise turned it off. She put the cooler on the bench and moved the tubes into a rack and the rack into the minus-eighty freezer. The display read minus 79. The freezer was on the building's emergency circuit, and she checked the label on the plug to be sure.

Then she went down to the animal facility.

She put on a gown and shoe covers at the door, out of habit, and went through. Ivan, who had looked after the mice for as long as she had been at the institute, lay in the corridor between the rooms with a cage card in his hand. The lights were on in the holding room and the air handlers were running, the same filtered air he had been breathing at eight o'clock on Tuesday. She pulled a rack out and looked in the cages, and then another rack, and another.

The mice were moving. They were at their water bottles, and in their bedding, and climbing on the bars of the lids, two hundred cages of them, the way they always were. Not one of them was down.

She stood in the holding room for a while. Then she went back upstairs, sat at her desk, opened a new laboratory notebook, and wrote Tuesday's date at the top of the first page. Under it she wrote what she had seen, in order, with the times: the river, the bell, the cyclist's pulse stopping under her fingers, the school, Paolo at 08:21. Under Wednesday she wrote the monitor trends, the lividity, the eleven samples and her own, the mice. She wrote no conclusions. At the bottom she wrote *Animals unaffected?*, and looked at the question mark, and left it there.
