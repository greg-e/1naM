# CLAUDE.md — 1 in a Million: Canon & Continuity Rules

This file is the working rulebook for revising this book, and the full source of truth for canon and retcon history — later entries in a retcon log supersede earlier ones, since decisions were made one-by-one with the author over time. Each narrative scene lives in its own file under [sections/](sections/) (named `NN-dayX-slug.md`, e.g. [sections/01-day0-john.md](sections/01-day0-john.md)) — that's the source of truth for prose.

Before writing or editing any scene, skim the relevant character/plot entries below. Before a large structural change, propose it and get author confirmation the same way past retcons were logged — don't silently overwrite settled canon. When you write a new scene, create a new file in `sections/` — don't write narrative prose inline in this file.

## Timeline — `time.md` is the canon of time (author-maintained, do not edit)

[time.md](time.md) is the author's manual timeline and the **single source of truth for chronology** — every dated/timed beat (clock times, day order, who is where when). It is maintained by hand by the author. **Never edit, rewrite, "sync," or reformat `time.md`** — read from it, and when a scene's timing disagrees with it, `time.md` wins and the scene gets fixed. If you believe `time.md` has an error or a gap, flag it to the author; don't change it yourself. The older `timeline.md` and `timeline-simple.md` are being removed and are superseded by `time.md` — don't consult or update them.

## Core premise (fixed)

- The event happens at 2:00 AM Eastern, September 5, 2028. Fast-acting, airborne, near-total in lethality.
- Survival rate is ~1 in a million — roughly 350 survivors in the continental US, no concentration in any one place. Higher survivor density in Canada, South America, Europe, and Asia (exact ratios not yet pinned down).
- Most survivors don't know they're immune until they wake into the silence. Death was instantaneous and symptom-free for almost everyone — no illness, no warning, nothing to distinguish one death from another.
- **Why some people survive is a deliberate mystery.** This is the emotional and thematic core of the title. Do not resolve it. No genetic/hereditary theory, no antibody theory, no exposure-survivor case study.
- September 5, 2028 is a **Tuesday, the day after Labor Day** (Monday the 4th) — deliberate, already used in Section 1 (John rules out "must be a holiday" because he'd just had Monday off). Keep this in mind in later sections: it's the first normal workday/school day after a long weekend, so there's no plausible "everyone's off" explanation available to any character.

## The grid-shutdown timeline — pivotal subplot

Full technical detail lives in `power-grid-shutdown-timelines.md` (repo root) — researched-but-speculative, its own confidence caveats apply, and the author substantially revised it (Draft 4, 2026-09-14) in a way that **overturns a rule the prose had already leaned on** — see below. This is a **dedicated subplot**, not just background realism. Key mechanics, current version:

- **North America is four separate, independently-synchronized interconnections (Eastern, Western, ERCOT, Quebec), not one grid** — each fails on its own clock. Within each zone: an automated "silent window" for the first ~2 hours (streetlights, card-reading gas pumps, municipal water pressure all still work); individual thermal plants (coal, then gas) start tripping from hour ~2 as fuel/fault limits catch up with them one at a time; then, zone by zone, frequency sags until it crosses the relay threshold and that whole interconnection cascades down hard and fast — a matter of minutes once it starts. **Realistic full "lights out" for a given region is hours 8–24 post-event, not days** — this is faster than the subplot's earlier drafts assumed.
- **Hydro is NOT exempt from this — this reverses the rule used through 2026-09-09.** Utility-scale hydro is grid-tied through the same protective relaying as everything else and goes down with the rest of its interconnection, unless a specific plant happens to already be electrically islanded (rare, not something to plan around). **Don't write "the Pacific Northwest / TVA / Quebec still has power because hydro" as a reliable mechanism anymore.** The only things that can plausibly still show power more than a day out: (a) a rare, small, accidental surviving pocket where generation and load happened to balance (real 2003 Northeast-blackout precedent — a few blocks of western New York rode it out unexplained for days; use this for small, temporary, don't-count-on-it patches, as Section 6 now does for Huntsville); (b) an individual building's own backup generator with days of fuel and nothing to refuel it but reserve — hospitals and certified airports are required to have exactly this, which is why Section 8 attributes Providence Seaside's power and PDX's runway lighting to their own plant, not the grid; (c) an individual property's own islanding-capable solar+battery system (used for Grace's house in Section 8 — a detail that also characterizes her, since she's the one who installed it).
- Nuclear plants SCRAM safely within hours (same as before), then run on backup diesel/battery for ~3 days. The danger window has **tightened to roughly 10–21 days post-trip** (not weeks 3–8, the earlier draft's figure) — when an uncooled spent fuel pool can boil dry and expose fuel rods — a regional (not global), Fukushima-scale contamination event with a years-to-decades exclusion zone.
- **Location still matters, but the "safest" ranking is now specifically about nuclear exposure, not about whose grid stays up longest** (nobody's does, for more than about a day, with rare exceptions per above). John's home region (Alpharetta, GA / Plant Vogtle) has the highest nuclear share and second-closest reactor proximity of the locations profiled — worst on the list. Reno, NV (TruckHouse's location) has zero nuclear generation, so zero contamination risk regardless of how its own grid fails — this, not "better grid durability," is what gives the eventual TruckHouse relocation its ticking-clock safety reason.
- **No in-house power scare, with one deliberate exception (2026-09-19, author-added, Section 4).** The general rule still holds — power is not John's personal problem as an ongoing danger, and the pressure the subplot puts on him is mainly a **date on a calendar** (his region's ~10–21-day nuclear danger window), which feeds the eventual Reno relocation. But Section 4 now shows the grid actually going dark at John's own house around 12:50 a.m. that first night — consistent with the grid-shutdown doc's own "hours 8–24 post-event" full-blackout window, not a contradiction of it — and it is explicitly *not* a scare: his emergency generator picks up the load without missing a beat, paying off a line he says earlier that same section ("I'm going to go double-check the generator before I turn in"). Use this as the model for any future in-house power beat: preparedness confirmed working, not jeopardy. Don't write a version where the backup fails or the outage itself creates danger/tension without the author asking.
- Look for actual scenes built on this — characters reasoning about which regions to avoid, a supply run routed around an approaching danger window, a character with direct plant knowledge — not just narration referencing it in passing.

## Craft rules (how to write this book, not just what happens in it)

- **Don't launch with known holes.** When the group plans a real-world logistics operation, show them catching and solving the gap *before* departure, not glossing over it.
- **Verify before committing, on the ground and in the air.** A low pass / visual check before landing anywhere without a working tower fits Sam's established competence.
- **Keep the full cast engaged.** As the survivor roster grows, look for real tasks and scenes for supporting characters rather than letting the same few carry every beat.
- **Day-numbering is relative, not exact** — *but `time.md` is authoritative where it does pin a time.* Internal chapter order matters more than every "Day N" label being perfectly synced. Keep relative order correct; don't force exact day-count consistency. When [time.md](time.md) gives a specific clock time or date for a beat, treat it as canon (see the Timeline section near the top of this file) and don't edit `time.md`.
- **Check who'd actually be present at 2:00 AM before placing a body somewhere.** The event hits at 2:00 AM sharp — a specific hour, not "sometime during the day." Workplaces, schools, offices are realistically empty at that hour except for actual night shifts (security, hospital staff, 24-hour operations); don't populate a building with casualties just because it's normally staffed during the day. **Cuts both ways:** a genuinely 24-hour location (a Waffle House, a hospital, a gas station) *should* have staff present at 2:00 AM — Section 6 handled this correctly (Waffle House employees confirmed dead, but outside on a break, which is why the interior itself is bodiless).
- **Ground vehicles are not a source of friction, this early in the timeline.** It's only been a few days since the event — batteries haven't died, gas hasn't gone stale, tires haven't gone flat. The reliable, repeatable answer whenever the group needs a car is a **rental car lot**: keys already in the consoles, tanks topped off by routine pre-event servicing, starts clean on the first try. Established in Section 6 (Huntsville) specifically to replace an earlier draft that had invented unneeded battery/ignition trouble. Use this same beat anywhere the group needs a car for the foreseeable future — don't manufacture car trouble as a plot obstacle. (This doesn't apply to aircraft, which keep their own real fuel/keys/maintenance friction, or to vehicles many weeks/months further into the timeline, if the story gets there.)
- **Log retcons instead of silently overwriting.** When a change contradicts existing material, identify every affected section explicitly, confirm the decision with the author, and note what still needs cleanup.
- Treat the deleted post-Section-2 plot as gone, not as a well of names/beats to casually reuse — if something from it turns out to still be wanted, that's the author's call to bring back explicitly.
