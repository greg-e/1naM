**Writing with Operational Realism.**
While both **C.S. Forester** (*The Good Shepherd*) and **James Clavell** (*Whirlwind*) write historical/thriller fiction grounded in high-stress, real-world operational environments, their writing styles are **fundamentally different in scale, focus, and narrative pacing.**

Here is how their styles compare across key dimensions:

### Key Differences

* **Scope and Narrative Scale:**
* **Forester (*The Good Shepherd*):** Hyper-focused, intimate, and laser-targeted. The entire novel takes place over a grueling 48-hour period, restricted almost entirely to the interior world, tactical decisions, and immediate perceptions of a single protagonist (Commander George Krause) as he defends an Allied convoy.
* **Clavell (*Whirlwind*):** Massive, sprawling, and multi-threaded. Set against the backdrop of the 1979 Iranian Revolution, *Whirlwind* features dozens of viewpoints, sprawling subplot lines, complex political factions, and a panoramic ensemble cast spanning various locations across Iran.


* **Prose and Internal Monologue:**
* **Forester:** Sparse, rhythmic, and deeply atmospheric. Forester excels at capturing the relentless physical exhaustion, quiet self-doubt, and procedural duty of a military professional. The prose mirrors the cadence of naval commands and sonar pings.
* **Clavell:** Conversational, exposition-heavy, and dramatic. Clavell prioritizes fast-paced dialogue, cultural friction, intrigue, and external action over deep single-character psychological interiority.


* **Technical Detail vs. Cultural Worldbuilding:**
* **Forester:** Obsessively technical about *mechanics and tactics*—radar, depth charges, course corrections, vector geometry, and seamanship.
* **Clavell:** Obsessively detailed about *culture, logistics, and political dynamics*—corporate maneuvers, regional customs, aviation logistics, and cross-cultural conflict.

### Core Similarity

The main overlap in their appeal is **operational realism**. Both authors write for readers who enjoy seeing skilled professionals perform complex, high-stakes tasks—whether that is maneuvering a World War II destroyer against U-boats or piloting helicopters through a revolutionary uprising.

If you enjoyed *The Good Shepherd* for its tight focus and relentless tactical tension, *Whirlwind* offers a much wider, epic tapestry rather than a direct stylistic match.

## Core premise

- The event happens at 2:00:00 a.m. Eastern (06:00:00 UTC), September 5, 2028: a single light-speed pulse of exotic particles from a magnetar starquake, passing through the whole planet in under a second. It knocks out cytochrome c oxidase, the enzyme cells use to consume oxygen, in humans only: the pulse matches the human enzyme, period. Death is histotoxic hypoxia: the blood stays oxygenated and the cells can't use it. Infrastructure is untouched, **every animal is unaffected (Dan lives)**, and nothing lingers. This is not an evolution book: nothing on the page or in reference explains why the human enzyme differs from an animal's or where a survivor's difference came from. Full mechanism, evidence chain, and the hidden-canon tier: `reference/cause_and_effect_2026-10-10.md`. The characters never use the word "magnetar" until the evidence gets them there.
- About 1 in a million survive: roughly 350 in the continental US, with no concentration anywhere. Survivor density is higher in Canada, South America, Europe, and Asia (exact ratios not yet set).
- **Survivors have no lasting symptoms.** They wake into the silence feeling like themselves, and nothing in their bodies sets them apart from the dead or tells them what happened. Survivors awake at the instant felt it as a few seconds of not being able to get a breath, and it passed: Della in the US (Section 8), and in Europe, where it struck at 08:00, nearly every survivor, Marchetti among them. The clues to the killing mechanism come from the dead (lividity), from Della's account, from the timestamps, and later from lab work, never from survivors' ongoing complaints. Ordinary exertion, grief, and shock stay on the page (out of breath on a hill, breathing hard after a near miss); don't write a lingering cough, headache, or air hunger.
- Most survivors don't know they've survived anything until they wake into the silence. Unconsciousness came in seconds, death within minutes. Most people were asleep and died without waking. Those who were awake show it: eyes open, mouths open as if gasping for air, some with dried blood under the nose from injuries in the collapse. **All the dead show cherry-red or bright pink lividity**, the classic cyanide and carbon-monoxide sign, which any ER doctor reads at once. These signs are clues to the killing mechanism.
- **Why ground survivors lived is a deliberate mystery on the page**, and it's the emotional and thematic core of the title. The author holds a hidden-canon answer (`reference/cause_and_effect_2026-10-10.md` §4) so the science stays consistent, but the page never confirms it: no character proves a genetic, hereditary, or antibody cause. Marchetti may suspect; she never says. **What killed everyone else is a separate, answerable question**, and Marchetti's work at the Hook can reach real findings on it.
- **Why the ISS crew lived is answerable on the page**: the station's orbital speed shifted the pulse's energy just outside the lethal band (Doppler detuning). The effect on the crew is zero: nobody aboard feels anything. This, and the fact that nothing lingers, is how the group establishes it's safe for the crew to come home. What nobody can rule out is the magnetar flaring again.
- September 5, 2028 is a **Tuesday, the day after Labor Day**. Section 0 uses this (on the walk John expects the short week to have everyone out early, and the driveways are still full). It's the first normal work and school day after a long weekend, so no character has an "everyone's off" explanation.

### John Hartman

#### Demographics:
 Age 61 | Alpharetta, GA (subdivision off Old Milton Pkwy near GA-400) | Second-generation German American | Widower (wife Naomi died 16 years ago) | Brother Mark is his only close family | Principal Applied AI Architect, Public Sector at Google (D.C.-based, hybrid metro Atlanta).

#### Physical Appearance & Presence:
 6'1", 195 lbs ("tall and lanky"). Broad-boned, rawboned frame. Thick brown beard peppered with white patches, thick brown hair streaked with white/gray. Deep tan, ruddy complexion, dark brown eyes behind black Wayfarer glasses. (Looks like Grizzly Adams/Jeremiah Johnson). Active and outdoors: long walks and trails with Dan, and the tan is earned. **But underneath the mountain man he's a real nerd**, and it shows the moment a system interests him: he talks in architectures and data flows, builds a model before he trusts a hunch, and will happily go too deep on telemetry, radios, or avionics for anyone still listening. Play the contrast; people who judge him by the beard are surprised.

#### Attire:
 T-shirts, worn olive drab BDU cargo pants, flip-flops, baseball cap outside. Boots for long walks and work (the Day 0 walk; Sam won't let him on a wing in flip-flops).

#### Background:
 His German immigrant parents settled in the South and gave him a deliberately American name so he'd fit in and not carry the stigma of WWII; they also dropped the second n from the family name (Hartmann on their own papers, Hartman on his). Georgia Tech (BS ECE) / Johns Hopkins (MS Systems Engineering). Put himself through school working four years of late nights at a Waffle House; ten years ago he redid his kitchen around a Waffle House-style flattop and gas burners, cooking settles him, and he takes his hash browns scattered, smothered, covered, and diced (so does Sam). Early youth vulnerability-research episode led to a federal consulting offer instead of prosecution. 30-year systems architect leading Gemini deployments for federal civilian agencies (DOT/FAA, DHS, USCG, NOAA, FEMA). Works off a Google-issued **dev box**, not a tower workstation: an aluminum block about the size of a hardcover book, ~128 GB of unified memory, ~100 W off a laptop-style power brick `[verify: 2028 spec]`, running a cut-down build of his team's model locally (a terse, no-slop Gemini build tuned for fast operational answers), with the eval toolchain and authenticated agency connectors: FAA ERAM and STARS, the new SMART system (Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes, and Trajectories) `[fictional]`, Coast Guard AIS, an MCP server for Aireon's satellite ADS-B, and station-by-station grid data. His laptop docks at his desk and remotes into it; on the road his phone is the front end, talking to it by voice. It holds the capture archive, travels in his flight bag, and runs off his storm battery when there's no mains: a **Jackery Solar Generator 5000 Plus** (5.04 kWh LiFePO4, 7,200 W, 120/240 V, wheels and pull handle, ~135 lbs `[verify weight]`, with folding solar panels `[verify kit contents]`). Full, it runs the dev box ~2 days alone, and the panels recharge it anywhere with sun. It's the heaviest single item in the PC-12's cabin and counts in every weight and balance. Instrument-rated private pilot as a weekend hobby: a G1000 Cessna 182, callsign "Cessna Four Six Five Hotel" `[decide: full N-number; verify it isn't a real airplane]`, in his own hangar at PDK near the Quality Aviation hangar `[verify]`, reached through a keypad gate to the back lot; most weekends he's there maintaining or flying it. He keeps a small pocket notebook on him to get thoughts out of his head and onto paper where he can see them. Keeps Naomi's study untouched. His instinct when a system fails is an engineer's habit: capture the state of a live system before you analyze it, keep the raw data, and never trust a live system to stay up. Collect first while the pipes are open; analyze later.

#### Friction & Flaws:
- The Safety Architect's Agony: Deeply respects protocols and safety layers because he helped build them. Overriding or stripping a safety filter causes him real internal agony, a moral dilemma of sorts: what is right, what is wrong, what he got caught doing, knowing the implications.

- Systems Realism: Knows how fragile public telemetry really is; feels an exhausting obligation to bypass slow bureaucratic consensus to fix anomalies himself.

- Quiet Withdrawal: Conserves his mental and emotional bandwidth for his work, his brother, and sitting in his late wife's untouched study.

- Not Corporate: John wears his everyday attire to meetings in DC, most comfortable in the background solving problems.


- Borrowed Faith: Naomi had a deep faith in God, and John thought he did too until she died and he found it was just her faith; he had none of his own. This is a spiritual journey for John toward the light of life he never thought he would ever need or want.

#### Physical Appearance & Presence:

- Circadian Rhythm: After years of long-haul flying, he has a hard time sleeping and adapting to regular days.

#### Physical Appearance & Presence:

#### Attire:
 NASA, rocket, or constellation T-shirts (one NASA shirt gone soft from washing), athletic shorts or jeans, and worn sneakers.

#### Background:
 His mother, Kendra Vance (see her entry), has been aboard the ISS since March. His grandmother lived across town. He knew the way, and couldn't make himself walk out past Carl and through a neighborhood where nothing moved, so he stayed in his room. Carl was due to stay through the fall. Growing up in Rocket City with a parent in orbit, he follows the ISS on tracking apps and keeps one eye on the sky. He talked to his mother only on scheduled family calls, which meant trips to Mission Control in Houston `[verify how ISS family conferences work]`; he has never used a ham radio and knows nothing about one. He doesn't remember his father; it's old, settled grief, part of his story rather than a raw wound ("it's always kind of just been me and Mom"). He knows space history cold (Rick Husband commanded *Columbia*). Huntsville runs the gamma-ray burst instrument on NASA's Fermi satellite (its operations center is at UAH and Marshall), so to Tyler "the satellite that watches for explosions in space" is a hometown thing, not trivia; that's why he asks John whether Fermi saw it (see The winter at the Hook). **He's smart, not compulsive.** The Huntsville ration log was survival: he counted the water because his mother taught him to count it before he's thirsty. At the Hook there's water and the situation has changed, so after the first few days (Sections 11 and 12, where Johan's "a log is signed" gets it one signature) he lets the log go and turns to things that matter more: the sky, the station, his mother. On Day 0 he found Carl dead on the living room couch, locked himself in his upstairs bedroom, and stayed there until Sam and Catherine reached him on Sunday Sept 10 (Section 10). Give him real presence, not cargo status, and be deliberate about how much danger he faces on-page.

#### Friction & Flaws:

- Half-Asked Questions: He starts sentences and stops when they head somewhere he isn't sure the adults can handle, so the question that matters most often goes unasked until someone draws it out.

#### Friction & Flaws:

#### Friction & Flaws:
- Pain Pills: An active, hidden opioid habit since the high-school back injury. - Nowhere to Transfer: Six years of sending the hard cases out. Now she is the end of the line, and out of her depth on what she used to hand off: surgery, ICU care, anything chronic. Della's ankle, which needed an operating room, is the first proof.

- Plainspoken to a Fault: Slow to anger and patient with stubborn machinery, but she says what's wrong with a plan in the fewest possible words, whoever made it.

### Johan Brandt

#### Demographics:
 Age 48 | Bremerhaven, Germany | Divorced (ex-wife Katrin) | Daughter Lena (22), a university student in Hamburg | Second Mate (the navigating officer), container ship *Pacific Tender*.

#### Physical Appearance & Presence:
 About 6'0", thick-set and muscular, with wide shoulders and a solid core. He moves with the low, automatic balance of a man who has spent more of his adult life on moving decks than on land. Gray eyes set in deep crow's feet from decades of sea glare, short-cropped blond hair going salt-and-pepper, and a well-kept beard. Skin weathered by wind and salt; large, calloused hands scarred from mooring lines and deck machinery; two fingers missing from his left hand, taken by a mooring winch. He speaks low, spare, German-accented English and uses a nod or a direct action where words aren't needed.

#### Attire:
 On the bridge: dark work trousers, a company polo or sweater, safety boots. On deck: coveralls, gloves, hard hat, a handheld radio, and an insulated jacket. Off watch: plain dark wool sweaters, heavy denim or canvas pants, and a thick watch cap. Always a heavy-duty pocket knife.

#### Background:
 His name is spelled Johan and pronounced "YO-hahn"; the English speakers around him hear it as Yohan. When the event hits, the *Pacific Tender* is bound Yokohama to Seattle, and all ~22 of her other crew die. Twenty-six years of sea watches and shipboard maintenance cycles, including two typhoons and a cargo-hold fire off Vladivostok. Off watch he's usually in the chart room correcting charts and the passage plan, or walking the deck checking lashings. Celestial navigation is in his training and he still keeps it up; his GMDSS certificate makes ship's radio familiar, though amateur radio isn't. The engine room is the engineers' world, which he knows from drills and the alarm panel; alone on the voyage, it's the part of the ship he can't fully answer. His coffee is black and very strong. He brings the ship to Port Angeles Harbor alone and anchors her ≈Day 9–10. Once ashore he lives with the group ("he would not go back and forth to the ship... no thanks") and works the ham link to the ISS from the Hook with John. He meets the group in Section 12. **Not a POV character.** Before Section 12 he reaches the page only through his deck, radio, and engine logs; after it, from outside, through John's and Sam's eyes. He arrives a competent stranger: the flaws below are canon for the author, and the group and the reader learn them slowly over the winter, never from inside his head. He keeps his logs in his cabin aboard *Active* and nobody opens them.

#### Friction & Flaws:
- No Cut Corners: He carries a deck officer's authority and a navigator's precision and has no tolerance for sloppy safety practice. In a group that improvises to survive, he'll stop work to do it right.

- Ships Before People: He knows the ship's charts, cargo plan, and lashings better than the people who worked them, and he had to check the manifest for the cook's name. The shame of that stays with him.

- A loner who has a hard time empathizing with others. He holds a Chief Mate's license and keeps signing on as Second because it keeps him on the navigation, away from cargo, crew management, and paperwork: the job with the fewest people in it.

- Lost two fingers on his left hand to a mooring winch early in his career.

### The night-lights receiver

### The grid-shutdown subplot

Technical detail: `reference/power-grid-shutdown-timelines.md` (researched but speculative, with its own confidence caveats). This is a subplot, not background texture.

- **North America runs on four separately synchronized interconnections** (Eastern, Western, ERCOT, Quebec), and each fails on its own clock. The first ~2 hours are an automated silent window: streetlights, card-reading gas pumps, and municipal water pressure all still work. From hour ~2, thermal plants (coal, then gas) trip one at a time as fuel and fault limits catch up. Then frequency sags past the relay threshold and the whole interconnection cascades down within minutes. **Full lights-out for a region comes at hours 8–24.**
- **Hydro goes down with its interconnection** like everything else, through the same protective relaying. Power more than a day out comes only from:
  - **a small accidental pocket** where generation and load happen to balance, such as a few blocks of a city; small and temporary;
  - **a building's own backup generator** with days of fuel, like Olympic Medical Center's; hospitals and certified airports are required to have one;
  - **a property's own islanding-capable solar+battery system;**
  - **the Hook's microreactor** (see Places), the one source in the book that doesn't depend on fuel deliveries.
- **Nuclear plants SCRAM safely within hours**, then run on backup diesel and batteries for ~3 days. The danger window is **~10–21 days after the trip**, when an uncooled spent fuel pool can boil dry and expose fuel rods: a regional, Fukushima-scale contamination event with a years-to-decades exclusion zone.
- **Location matters for nuclear exposure**, not for whose grid lasts longest. John's region (Alpharetta / Plant Vogtle) has the highest nuclear share and second-closest reactor of the locations profiled, the worst on the list. Port Angeles is very low risk `[derived; general meteorology]`: the only Pacific Northwest plant, Columbia Generating Station, is ~220 mi east across the Cascades, and prevailing winds carry any release away. Verify Naval Base Kitsap's naval reactors (passive decay-heat removal, not the spent-fuel-pool scenario) and Hanford's legacy waste tanks before putting this on the page.
- **Power at the group's own base is never jeopardy.** The subplot's pressure is a date on a calendar. Any in-house power beat follows the Section 5 model: preparedness confirmed working. Don't write a backup failure or outage-driven tension unless the author asks.

### Unattended-world hazards

Danger from what nobody is tending, never from antagonists. Source notes: `reference/more_to_think_about_unattended_world_hazards.md`; where it differs from this entry (its "ghost lineage" opening, its scale, its GPS mechanism), this entry wins.

- **Dogs.** Animals are unaffected, so every pet outlives its owner. Day 0: dogs bark behind closed doors and fences all over a suburb at dawn; a neighborhood is never silent. ## Writing rules

### Point of view

- **The book is multiple POV, in close third person.** Three characters carry POV: **John, Sam,** and **Kendra Vance** (Tyler's mother, aboard the ISS). Each scene file has one POV character, except the document pages, which have none.
- Everyone else (Johan, Marchetti, Catherine, Della, Tyler, Dan) is seen only from outside, through a POV character's eyes. Their inner lives come through dialogue, action, and what the POV character notices. Della's backstory stays her own account, told at dinner.
- **Kendra's scenes put the ISS on the page directly**, not only as the far end of the ham link. Her POV is where the station's side of the event, the consumables clock, and the safe-return question play out. "No Mission Control scene" still holds: the ground she can reach is the ham link.
- The POV-observes-only rule applies to every POV character: narration reports what that character perceives and knows, never what the reader knows from other threads.

### Document pages

Johan's and Marchetti's threads reach the page as documents they made, not as POV scenes, the way Catherine came in through her 911 calls. A document page carries no narration and no interiority: only what its maker would write down.

- **Marchetti's transcripts.** She records every pass off the transceiver and types it up afterward. The pages start at her first ISS contact (~early October); nothing of hers appears in September. Each page opens with a ham-log header (her callsign and the station's, date, AOS and LOS in UTC, maximum elevation, TX/RX frequencies, mode), then speaker lines, with her own gap markers (*[noise, 3 s]*, *[unreadable, 4 s]*), cut off at loss of signal mid-word. Passes are in English, the station's working language; personal lines switch to Italian, which she types in Italian with her own English gloss in square brackets. Luca works her passes; Kendra's voice doesn't appear until after Section 16. She reports what she saw and measured, compressed, with no conclusions (No Premature Conclusions), so the survival mystery stays closed. Pages are short, each placed at its date as `NNa-Bellinzona.md` between the winter's Hook sections. When she reaches the Hook she hands the file to Catherine and John, and from then on she is seen from outside, like Johan.
- **Johan's logs.** Deck log and radio log merged by time, with engine-log lines wherever he signs under Alvarez's entries; in English, the ship's working language, in his spare style. The remarks column carries the story. Three pages (5a, 9a, 11a) plus Friday's inside Section 12, named `NNa-Pacific Tender.md`. Nobody in the story ever reads them.

### Scarcity

With ~350 survivors nationwide, every search for survivors comes back sparse, never a flood of hits. Characters never know the true survivor count and don't act as if they do. Don't invent discovery mechanisms that make finding people easy; every survivor found costs real effort.

### Craft rules

- **Don't launch with known holes.** When the group plans a real-world logistics operation, show them catching and solving the gap before departure.
- **Verify before committing.** A low pass or visual check before landing anywhere without a working tower fits Sam's competence.
- **Keep the full cast engaged.** As the roster grows, find real tasks and scenes for supporting characters instead of letting the same few carry every beat.
- **Day numbering is relative.** Chapter order matters more than exact "Day N" labels. Use the Timeline for sequence and clock times.
- **Check who is actually present at 2:00 a.m. before placing a body.** Offices and schools are empty at that hour except for night shifts (security, hospital staff, 24-hour operations). A 24-hour place (a Waffle House, a hospital, a gas station) should have staff there.
- **Ground vehicles aren't a source of friction this early.** Batteries haven't died, gas hasn't gone stale, tires haven't gone flat. When the group needs a car, the answer is a **rental car lot**: keys in the consoles, tanks topped off by routine servicing, starts on the first try. Aircraft keep their real fuel, key, and maintenance friction, and vehicles many weeks or months on may not be so easy.
- **The "machine keeps going after the operator dies" motif** (Sam's A350, Johan's ship, Della's crash) is used deliberately; any further use must be a conscious echo.
- **No teaser lines.** Don't announce that something matters before showing it. Three patterns, usually found together, and all of them read as AI slop:
  - *Teaser fragments:* a short verbless beat that flags significance ("An airplane doing something different." "Routine." "Fly first." "Dry air, eleven hours of it ahead.").
  - *Placeholder nouns:* "something different," "something wrong," or "something off" standing in for the concrete thing the POV character actually sees (a track that turns south while every other one holds its heading).
  - *Pre-stating the payoff:* a line that says up front what the following sentences show. Let the evidence come first; if the character draws a conclusion, it comes after, in plain words ("Every other airplane on the map was holding a heading. This one had turned and landed.").

### Line editing

Draft, tighten, and audit prose with the Zinsser skill ([.claude/skills/zinsser/SKILL.md](.claude/skills/zinsser/SKILL.md), `/zinsser`): cut clutter, use strong verbs, keep unity, and stop when the scene is done, always in the author's own plain voice. This file wins wherever the two differ.

### Operational realism

Write every scene, new or revised, with procedural accuracy, correct domain terminology, and believable friction instead of dramatic abstraction.

**1. Concrete specificity over abstract drama.** Don't carry an outcome or feeling with generic emotional adjectives ("he was panicked," "it was intense"). Show the mechanical steps, equipment, control inputs, and conditions that produce the situation or answer it. Name the hardware, switches, sub-assemblies, and readings with their correct designations.

| Abstract (avoid) | Operationally realistic (target) |
| --- | --- |
| "He checked his gauges as the engine sputtered." | "He scanned the fuel flow indicator; port main was fluctuating below 120 lbs/hr despite the auxiliary pump remaining energized." |
| "The ship turned hard to dodge the submarine." | "He ordered hard right rudder, ringing down emergency full on the engine order telegraph to bite into the turn before the wake straightened." |
| "The helicopter flew through bad weather over the mountains." | "The aircraft held 4,500 ft MSL in light continuous turbulence, tracking the 040° radial to clear the ridgeline under visual flight rules." |

**2. Time, geometry, and friction.**
- *Procedural lag:* account for radio latency, distance, mechanical response (spool-up, hydraulic pressure build), checklist length, and fatigue errors.
- *Environmental limits:* line of sight, fuel endurance, weather minimums, thermal limits, weight and balance, communication dead zones.
- *Mental load:* filter decisions through exhaustion, sensory overload, and tunnel vision, never perfect omniscience.

**3. Professional cadence.** Professionals under stress use brevity codes, acronyms, and short direct syntax, not expository dialogue.

**Jargon: explain only what the reader needs.** A blend of letting action carry the jargon and explaining it outright:
- **Texture stays unexplained.** If the reader can follow the scene without knowing what a term means (a checklist item, a frequency, a system name one professional says to another), leave it bare and let context carry it.
- **Plot-bearing terms get one clause, once.** If the story turns on what a thing *does* (AIS showing a ship under human control, the 911 location record that yields an address, a spent fuel pool boiling dry), give its function in a single clause at first use. Function before name; spell out the full acronym only when the name itself tells the reader something.
- **Explanation needs a reason in the scene.** It comes from a character who knows telling one who doesn't (John to Sam on grids and reactors, Sam to John on turbines, Della on diesels), or from the POV character's own working thought. A character never explains a term to someone who would already know it.
- **Never a glossary aside.** No parenthetical definitions, no narrator stepping out to lecture. Everyday terms (FAA, GPS, 911, AI) are never expanded.

**Technical ruleset:**
1. **Equipment:** precise makes, models, variants, and subsystems; correct military, maritime, aviation, and medical nomenclature.
2. **Procedure:** actions in the right order (checklist, directive, feedback, acknowledgment), with mechanical and biological delay.
3. **Logistics:** track finite resources (fuel in lbs/gal/endurance, battery capacity, thermal limits, supplies) and respect performance envelopes (density altitude, sea state, visibility).
4. **Perspective:** a character knows only what instruments, line of sight, or incoming comms tell them.

**Audit pass:** before finishing any prose, search for "quickly," "suddenly," "dangerous," "massive," "intense," and similar words, and replace them with measurements, instrument readings, or procedural steps.

**How this fits the rest of this file:**
- **Specifics must be real.** The PC-12, A350-900, MH-65E, Argus-class OPC, container ship, expedition yacht, Crew Dragon, and ER and orthopedic procedures all have real specs. Check them against `reference/` or research, and flag anything unverified with `[verify]` rather than inventing a number. A confident wrong figure is worse than a vague one.
- **Canon still wins.** Added precision never overrides a settled beat or the order of events. It can move a clock time in the Timeline when the real-world math says it's wrong.
- **Not every scene needs friction.** Deliberately easy beats (the Minneapolis fuel stop, the Amarillo stop, the Phoenix→KCLM leg, rental cars) get the same concrete detail without manufactured trouble.
- **Grief beats stay.** The ban on emotional adjectives applies to them too: carry John's, Sam's, Catherine's, and Della's grief through physical action and observed detail, but keep the emotional core.
- **Character descriptions are reference, not copy.** Use the appearance and habit details in the Characters entries through observed action on the page; don't paste them in as descriptive blocks.
- Write units as plain text in prose ("120 lbs/hr," "FL280," "121.5"), not LaTeX.
- **Radio phraseology follows the FAA AIM** (Ch. 6 §3 for distress and urgency; Ch. 4 §2 for general phraseology). 121.5 is "121.5" or "the emergency frequency," **never "Guard."** MAYDAY (or PAN-PAN) is said three times, then the message elements in AIM order: station addressed, aircraft ID and type, nature of distress, weather, intentions, position and heading, altitude, fuel remaining (the AIM says minutes; on this book's radios, hours read more naturally and are fine), people on board. Try the frequency in use or the controlling facility first, then any station and 121.5; squawk 7700 when unable to establish contact. Airline callsigns are spoken in group form, with "Heavy" for heavy aircraft in US airspace ("Delta Twenty-Seven Heavy"). With nobody on the ground, calls are "transmitting in the blind."
- **Airline terminology.** On airliners and the C-17 it's always the **flight deck**, never the cockpit; the PC-12 and the MH-65Es can be a cockpit. Pilots on an augmented crew take **breaks**, not rest blocks or rest periods. Airline pilots in cruise wear lap belts, not shoulder harnesses. Crew hierarchy holds: a first officer doesn't call out to or wake a captain on his break.
