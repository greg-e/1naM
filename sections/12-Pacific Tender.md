# Pacific Tender

### 9/14/2028, 05:30 ship's time - Johan

He had kept her outside all night at dead slow, west until midnight and then back, and at half past five there was enough gray in the east to pick out the top of Vancouver Island. Tatoosh Island light bore 088, eleven miles. He took the revolutions up a few at a time from the bridge, and by twenty past six she was making eleven and a half.

Cape Flattery came abeam at a quarter to seven, black rock with the swell breaking white along the foot of it. Neah Bay opened up behind the cape, a breakwater and a line of boats on their moorings all lying the same way. He looked at it through the glasses until it closed. He made a pot of coffee in the pantry behind the bridge and drank it standing at the radar.

At eight o'clock the DSC alarm went off on the VHF at the GMDSS console.

He had sent that set more than forty calls since the fifth, and nothing had ever come back on its screen. He crossed the bridge and read it. ALL SHIPS. SAFETY. CH 16. The sender was a nine-digit number starting with 00366, a United States coast station. He silenced the alarm, and the speaker on 16 opened up.

"All stations, all stations, all stations. This is John Hartman at the Coast Guard station on Ediz Hook, Port Angeles, Washington. Position four eight degrees zero eight decimal four minutes north, one two three degrees two four decimal six minutes west. There are people alive here. We have power, food, and a doctor. Answer on channel one six. Over."

It was an American voice, with some static on it. The speaker hissed. Above the handset, taped to the console, were the card with the Georgia number on it and the strip of printer paper with the same name. He read the time off the GPS and wrote it in the log, 0800, and his pencil went through the paper on the last zero. He picked up the handset.

"Ediz Hook, Ediz Hook, Ediz Hook, this is motor vessel *Pacific Tender*, *Pacific Tender*, *Pacific Tender*, on channel one six. Over."

His voice came out rough. He hadn't used it since he called Tofino the evening before. The speaker hissed, and he stood there and watched the second hand on the console clock go around, and then around again.

"Ediz Hook, this is *Pacific Tender*. I hear you. Position four eight degrees two four minutes north, one two four degrees two two minutes west, course zero nine six, speed eleven and a half, inbound. One person on board. Over."

Nothing came back. He looked at the radar, which showed him the cape and an empty lane. The GPS position had changed by half a mile, so he corrected it and keyed the handset again.

**08:01 local Pacific Time, Thursday**

The phone went off on the shelf over John's bunk. It had gone off fourteen times since the tower's antenna came up on Wednesday afternoon, each one a new contact the receiver had never been able to hear from the roof: a log barge off Clallam Bay, a seiner adrift off Pillar Point, something small going three-tenths of a knot sideways past Sekiu. He had told it not to filter, so it hadn't. He had gotten to sleep a little before five.

He got one eye open and found the screen.

**Channel 16, tower. Voice, not scheduled. 08:01:09.**

**"Ediz Hook, Ediz Hook, Ediz Hook, this is motor vessel Pacific Tender, Pacific Tender, Pacific Tender, on channel one six. Over."**

He read it twice. Then he was in the passageway in his T-shirt and socks with the phone in his hand, and he could hear a man's voice coming from the wardroom before he got there, low and hoarse and foreign, reading a latitude. "...one person on board. Over."

He caught the back of a chair and leaned over the laptop. "Tower, channel 16, live. Now."

**Local override. Live.**

"*Pacific Tender*, *Pacific Tender*, this is John Hartman on Ediz Hook. I hear you. Go ahead. Over."

The speaker hissed long enough for him to look at the cable to make sure it was plugged in.

"Ediz Hook, *Pacific Tender*. Good morning. This is Second Officer Brandt." The man stopped, and when he came back he was slower. "John Hartman. You sent a message to all ships on the fifth, from Georgia. I received it. Over."

John sat down. He had asked the model about that message on Sunday and it had given him a zero. "Say again. You received the SafetyNET message?"

"Received. It came every six hours. The last one was Friday, zero seven four one Greenwich. Over."

He reached for the notebook, and it was in his shirt, and his shirt was in the cabin. He wrote *Fri 0741Z* on the back of a printout. Somebody's generator in Portsmouth, Virginia had kept going three days longer than he'd known about.

"How many on board?"

"One. Twenty-two dead. All of the crew, on the fifth. How many are you?"

"Five. And a dog."

Sam had come in behind him in a Coast Guard T-shirt, with Tyler at his elbow and Dan shoving past both of them. It was a while before the speaker opened again.

"Five," the man said. "Understood. Request you shift to channel two two alpha. This is the calling channel."

John looked up at Sam. He had twenty-three ships on the board and not one other voice between here and Japan. Sam had started to grin. "I'd shift," he said.

"Shifting two two alpha," John said, and told the model. While it was changing over he asked it for the AIS.

**PACIFIC TENDER. Container ship. 48°24'N, 124°22'W. Course 096. Speed 11.4 kt. Tower receiver, first report 07:52.**

**New contact alert sent 07:52. Acknowledged 07:52.**

He had no memory of it. He looked at the 11.4. Every other ship he had up there had stopped, or was going back and forth with the tide.

Catherine came in with her glasses in her hand. Sam told her in about six words, and she looked at the speaker. "Is he hurt? Ask him about his breathing."

"Our doctor wants to know if you're hurt, and if you've had any trouble breathing."

"No. I am tired. I will anchor in your harbor about twelve thirty, four eight zero seven decimal eight north, one two three two five decimal zero west, south of the pilot station."

"What do you need from us?"

"What do you have?"

John looked at Sam, and Sam reached over and pulled the laptop toward him. "This is Sam Reyes. I fly airplanes. We don't have a tug or a pilot. We have a forty-one-foot boat, and nobody here has driven it."

"Then I need nothing, thank you."

Tyler had gone down the passageway at a run and come back. "Della says what does he draw," he said, out of breath, "and tell him there's deadheads inside the Hook."

"Forty-one feet," the voice said, when John had passed it on. "The deadheads are in the Coast Pilot. Who is Della?"

"She's our mechanic. There's a bulk carrier adrift about nine miles northwest of us, *Golden Kestrel*. She goes up and down with the tide."

"I have her on AIS."

John had started to say they'd bring the boat out as soon as he was down, and he got most of it said.

"Tomorrow," the man said. "She has not lain to this anchor before. I will stay on watch through the night and see how she takes the tides. Come after zero eight hundred. I will rig a ladder on the side away from the wind. *Pacific Tender* standing by on one six and two two alpha."

Catherine put her glasses on. "He's been by himself on that thing for nine days. I could be out there this afternoon."

"It's his ship," Sam said. He was looking at the bulkhead clock. "Twelve thirty. I want to see that."

### 11:15 ship's time - Johan

He had begun taking the way off her at half past ten. By a quarter past eleven she was down to six knots with *Golden Kestrel* a mile and a quarter on his port beam, rust-streaked and riding high, her wheelhouse windows reflecting the sky. He took the autopilot off and steered from the helm, walking out to the wing and back.

The Hook came up as a low line of driftwood and sand with the tower standing on the end of it, then a lighthouse, and a hangar. He put the wheel over and brought her around the end of the spit, south and then west, and at that speed the turn took ten minutes. The harbor opened up with the mill at the far end and the town behind it. No smoke came from the stacks. A white ferry lay at her dock.

The cutter lay along the pier on his starboard hand, half a mile off, with the sun on her, and he took the glasses out on the wing. People were standing at the rail at her stern, four of them. A tall man with a heavy beard had a cap in his hand. A shorter one stood beside him with his arms folded. A small woman had a hand up to her eyes. In front of them a child was up on the bottom rail with something held against his chest, and a dog stood beside him with its front feet on the rail.

The child put an arm straight up.

Johan lowered the glasses and looked at them without. He raised his arm. Then he went back in, because she needed five more degrees.

The wind was west at about eight knots, so she would lie the way she was going. He had the bearings for the spot on the chart: the light on 046, the hangar on 021. At half a mile he stopped the engine. At three cables he put her astern, heard the air go in, and felt it take hold through the deck. Her bow began to walk to starboard, as he had known it would. He watched the speed on the GPS come down: 2.1, 1.3, 0.6, 0.0, and then it read 0.3 with the arrow pointing behind her. He stopped the engine. The sounder showed eighteen fathoms.

It was 12:22. He clipped the handheld to his jacket and left the bridge empty.

He took the elevator down and went out the main deck door and forward along the alley between the stacks on the port side, with the lashing rods going by and his boots loud on the plates. It took seven minutes, the same as yesterday, and he went up the forecastle ladder with his hand on the rail.

Letting go on the brake would have been faster, and in shallower water he would have done it. But eighteen fathoms was a long way for eleven tonnes to fall with one man on the wheel and nobody to shout if it ran away. He put the windlass in gear, took off the brake, knocked out the pin, and threw back the stopper bar. Then he walked her out on the motor. The chain came up out of the locker one link at a time, with the rust dropping off it and the deck shaking under his feet. It took six minutes to get the first shackle out and into the water, and then the second, and halfway through the second the chain went slack in the hawse. She was on the bottom.

He screwed the brake down, took the windlass out of gear, and looked over the side. The water was sliding forward along the hull, very slowly. He eased the wheel. The chain ran ten feet with a roar and a cloud of red dust, and he screwed it down again. He let her fall back on the wind and gave her chain as she took it, a few fathoms at a time, watching the painted links go by. Three shackles, four, five. At six on deck he screwed the brake down hard, dropped the stopper bar across, and drove the pin.

The chain lay out ahead of her in a long curve. He stood with his hand on it. It rose as she came to the end of it, straight and dripping, and he felt the weight come on and the hum in the links. Then it sagged back slowly into the water. She had brought up.

He got the anchor ball out of the forecastle store and ran it up the forestay. It took him two tries to clear the halyard.

Walking back, he looked across at the cutter. They were still at the rail.

He took his bearings from the bridge wing, the light, the hangar, and the mill stack, and wrote them down. He set the GPS anchor alarm at a tenth of a mile. Then he rang FINISHED WITH ENGINES on the telegraph, and it rang in the control room with nobody to answer it, so he took the elevator down, answered it, and wrote it in Alvarez's book with the time. When he got back up he took the handset.

"Ediz Hook, *Pacific Tender*. Anchored at one three zero one. Six shackles on deck, starboard anchor. Over."

"*Pacific Tender*, Ediz Hook." It was the one who flew airplanes. "We watched it. Nice work. See you in the morning."

He stayed on the wing as it got dark, with the bearings on a card, and took them again on the turn of the tide at seven. She swung a little to the north and settled. Nothing came on along the town. He had come past this harbor more times than he could count, at night, with the pilot boat coming out from under the lights and the whole foot of the mountain lit up behind it. Now the only lights he could see stood in a row out on the spit, and three of the windows in the cutter were yellow.

He heated water and washed all over for the first time since Sunday, and trimmed his beard in the mirror with the scissors from the first aid locker, and cut himself under the jaw. He laid out a clean shirt.

Then he went up to the console. The tape had dried out, and the card came away easily. He looked at the number on it. He put it in the back of the chart table drawer, under the old corrections. The strip of printer paper he folded and put in the pocket of the clean shirt. He lay down on the mattress across the bridge from the panel, with the anchor alarm on, and when he looked at the clock again it had been five hours.

**07:30 local Pacific Time, Friday**

The forty-one-footer lay at the float below the pier. Della had given them the start-up off the top of her head the night before, and John had it on the back of a sheet: battery switches, blowers, the raw water, one engine and then the other. "It's got two throttles, and you steer it with those more than the wheel," she'd said. "Go practice before you put it against a ship. And wear the vests. They're on the hooks in the boat shed, and Catherine's not fishing you out."

"What do you want to know about him?" John had asked.

"Ship that size, one guy," Della said. "Ask him who's been doing his fuel."

Sam took it out into the middle of the harbor and spent an hour on it, backing and turning around a crab pot buoy with his jaw set, while John stood in the door with a line in his hands and a bumper he kept moving. Twice it went somewhere neither of them had asked it to. Around nine they went back for Catherine and her bag. Tyler stood at the end of the float with Dan on the leash. He had asked if he could go, and Catherine had told him they'd see about it.

From the Hook the ship had looked big. From the boat she filled up everything on the left side, a black wall with her draft marks climbing out of the water and rust running down from the hawse, and over it the containers stacked up seven and eight high, blue and orange and gray. It took a long time to go down her side. The chain went into the water ahead of her at a long slant and didn't move. The gangway was on her port side, an aluminum stair let down from the main deck to about a yard off the water, with a rope ladder hanging at the bottom of it and a lifebuoy on the rail. A man stood at the top.

Sam brought the boat in at about four knots. He got one throttle back and the other one the wrong way, and the bow swung in toward the plates, and John's handheld said, "Too fast. Keep off. Go around."

Sam said something in Spanish and went around.

The second time he came in along her side at a walk, and the voice said, "Stop there. Come to me. Slowly. Hold." John got the line on the post at the foot of the stair on the second throw. "Not to your hand," the radio said. "Make it fast to the boat." John made it fast to the boat.

He went up the rope ladder first, the six rungs of it, then the stair with Catherine behind him taking it one tread at a time with a hand on each rail. The smell reached him about halfway up, and it was the same as Cutter's and the hospital's, thick and sweet, on a light wind. He got the N95 out of his pocket and put it on.

The man at the top was as tall as he was, and half again as wide through the shoulders, in a clean dark polo shirt with a shipping company's name on it and the creases still in it from the drawer. He had a short gray-blond beard with a fresh nick under the jaw. His eyes were red around the rims, and they went over John, the boots and the vest and the mask, and then down past him to how the boat lay against the stair.

"Hartman," he said.

"John." John held out his hand, and it was taken and let go, a big hard hand. "It's good to see you."

"Brandt. Johan." It came out *Yo-hahn*. His eyes moved. "You are the doctor."

Catherine had reached the top with her bag and she already had two fingers on his wrist. He looked down at them and let them stay. She was watching his fingernails. "When did you last sleep?"

"Last night. Five hours."

"Before that?"

"Half an hour. Since Yokohama, half an hour."

"Any trouble breathing? Any time, since the fifth? Headache? Nausea?"

"No."

She went on counting. His other arm hung at his side, and John saw that the hand on it stopped short, two fingers gone at the root, the scars white and old. Sam came up the stair and said, "Sam Reyes," and got the same handshake.

Johan unbuttoned his shirt pocket with his free hand and took out a strip of paper folded in four, and held it out to John.

It had gone soft along the folds. He opened it. *ALL SHIPS ALL SHIPS ALL SHIPS. THIS IS JOHN HARTMAN, ALPHARETTA, GEORGIA, USA.* He had never seen it on paper. He'd sent it from his office with the door shut, and he had taken out one word and put it back. *TWO SURVIVORS HERE.* The telephone number was the phone in his pocket, which hadn't had service in nine days.

"I couldn't have been much use to you with that number," John said.

"No." Johan looked at the paper. "On the first day I thought it was the ship. A gas, or something in the cargo. I tested for it. Then this came." He got his wrist back from Catherine and buttoned the pocket. "So I knew it was not only the ship."

John folded it on the same folds and held it out. "You can have it," Johan said. "It is yours." John put it in his shirt.

They went up in the elevator, with Johan holding the door and not saying anything about the mask. The passage outside it had cabin doors down both sides, all of them shut. The bridge was as wide as the ship, with windows all the way across, and it smelled of coffee and old sweat. Sam walked straight to the front and stood at the center console with his hands behind him, reading it the way he had read the Pilatus. Then he turned around and looked at the mattress. It lay on the deck against the after bulkhead, with a blanket folded on it, as far from the alarm panel as it could get. Next to the radar there was a clean rectangle on the deck, the same size.

"That's a long way from your panel," Sam said.

"Yes," Johan said. "So I must stand up."

Sam nodded. He looked at the panel, then the mattress. "I was on the A350 out of Atlanta," he said. "Delta. Four of us up front, and three hundred nineteen behind. I woke up on my break over Saskatchewan. I put it down at Edmonton."

"With whom?"

"Nobody."

Johan looked at him for a while. Then he went to the pantry, came back with the pot and three cups, and poured. He didn't ask anyone. John took the mask off to drink it. It was the strongest coffee he had ever had in his mouth, and his eyes watered. Sam drank his and held out the cup for more.

John told it after that, the way he'd told it to Catherine on the leg north: the aircraft, the ships, the grid, the reactors, Phoenix, the bridge, Huntsville, the reactor behind the hangar. Johan stood with his cup and listened and asked two questions, both about ships. When John said there was a cabin for him on *Active*, he looked out the window at her.

"If you'd rather keep your own things out here, we can run you back and forth," John said.

"No, thanks." He set the cup down. "But I do not leave her like this. You said east winds in the winter?"

"Della says so. So does the book."

He looked at their boots again, and at Sam's hands. "Then you will want gloves," he said.

### 9/15/2028, 11:40 ship's time - Johan

The pilot had the brake. A pilot was not a seaman, but this one did what he was told and repeated it back, which was more than Johan could say for most cadets. The tall one he put on the stopper bar on the port side, and he had to take him by the arm once and move him two steps to the left, out from behind the chain. "If it jumps, it goes there," he said, and showed him. Hartman looked at the place where he had been standing and stayed where he was put.

They let go the port anchor at 11:52 and gave her four shackles, and veered the starboard to eight. The doctor had stayed in the ship's office with the official log and the crew list, copying names into a book of her own. She asked him how to spell Okafor, and he told her.

He shut the quick-closing valves on the main engine's fuel, and then the bunker tanks one at a time, and tagged them. He had the two of them walk the main deck and the engine flat with the watertight door list, closing and calling, with him at the panel watching each light go green. No. 2 generator he left running on its day tank. Unattended it had perhaps forty hours in it, and when it stopped the lights and the fans would stop. They rigged two battery lanterns from the lifeboat stores on the forestay and the stern rail and put in new cells.

He went down by himself to the chill room off the galley. The compressor was running, and the vegetables he'd stacked in the passage on the fifth had gone to black water and flies. He put his hand on the door. Okafor, Alvarez, Bautista. It would hold its cold a while after the generator stopped, not long. He checked that the dog was down.

In the control room Alvarez's logbook lay open on the desk. He wrote the shutdown under his own entries, time and valves, and signed it, and then he closed the book. It was the first time it had been closed since the fourth. He left it square on the desk.

In his cabin he packed one bag: clothes, the picture of Lena at her school in Hamburg, his knife. He took the sextant in its box. From the chart table he took the *Nautical Almanac*, the sight reduction tables, and the Strait and Puget Sound charts, and he left the drawer shut. On the bridge he opened the deck log and wrote it out.

*1635. At anchor Port Angeles Harbor 48-07.8N 123-25.0W. Stbd anchor 8 shackles, port 4. M/E shut down, F.O. isolated, W/T doors closed. No. 2 D/G on day tank. Anchor lights on battery fore and aft. 22 crew remain aboard. Left vessel. Brandt, Second Officer.*

He put the deck log and the official log in the bag. He looked at the barometer and tapped it. Then he switched off the radar, because nobody would be looking at it.

"You want a minute?" the pilot asked at the head of the stair.

"No." He went down first and took the bag from the doctor, and cast off the line himself, and showed the pilot how to back away from a ship's side, which was with the port engine.

The cutter's brow was long and level at this state of the tide. Just inside it was a small stand with a logbook on it, a pen in the gutter. He stopped, and the tall man almost walked into him. The last entries were in careful round writing, a date and five names with a place beside each one, and they went to the bottom of the page. *Whitlock, quarterdeck. Tran, control room. Haas, galley.* After that came a signature nobody could read, and under it in capitals JOHN HARTMAN.

Johan picked up the pen. He turned to the next page and wrote the date, *1722, Brandt, J., 2nd Off., M/V Pacific Tender, aboard*, and he put the pen back in the gutter.

**18:00 local Pacific Time**

They ate in the sick bay. There were six of them now and two stools, and Johan took the deck by the door with his back against the bulkhead and his plate on his knees. The potatoes were fried in a thin flat cake with cheese on them, and he ate them. He had not sat down with anyone to eat since the night the cook had made adobo. A brown and black dog came and stood in front of him and pushed its nose into his left hand, into the place where the fingers stopped, and breathed there. He didn't know what to do about that, so he left the hand where it was.

The mechanic lay propped up in the lower bunk with one arm in a sling and a cast on her leg to the knee, a broad short woman with yellow fading around her eyes. She had been looking at him since he came in. She finished what was on her fork.

"Who's been doing your fuel?"

"I have."

"You know how to do that?"

"No." He told it from the beginning, because she was looking at him as if she would know. The settling tank, Alvarez's book, the transfer by hand. The purifier tripped on Thursday night and he accepted the alarm in his sleep. Main engine shutdown on the service tank at 04:00 on the Saturday, thirty-four hours not under command, beam-on, with the bowl swinging on the hoist. A hundred and twenty discs.

She had stopped chewing. "You pulled the bowl on a purifier by yourself. In a seaway."

"Both of them. From the manual."

"With that hand."

"I carried the discs against my chest."

"What'd you get wrong?"

"The first start, I left the operating water valve shut."

The corner of her mouth went up. "Everybody does that," she said. She held out her fork to the doctor, who took the plate. "I've got a pump on the pier I can't get down to. Tomorrow."

The boy sat on the stool with a spiral notebook on his knees. He was very thin and his shirt had once been dark. He had not said anything to Johan, but he had watched his hands, and now he slid off the stool and came over and stood with his pencil. "How much water is on your ship?" he asked.

"Fresh water? About three hundred tonnes, when we left Yokohama. Perhaps two hundred fifty now."

"How many liters is that?"

"A tonne is a thousand liters."

The boy wrote it down, and Johan could see the page from where he sat. It was ruled in columns with a pencil and a straightedge. *Active*, a figure in liters, a date beside it, a line for each day. At the top it said *5 people*, with a line through the 5, and over that he had written *6*. Under it he divided.

"You keep this every day?" Johan asked.

"Yes sir."

"It is a log, then. Who has signed it?"

The boy looked at the page.

"A log is signed," Johan said, "or it is only notes."

The boy thought about that. He put the notebook on the bunk rail and wrote at the bottom of the column, slowly, his name and the date, and turned it around to show him. Johan read it, and nodded, and went back to his potatoes.

**21:15 local Pacific Time**

John was at the corner of the wardroom table when Johan came in with a mug. He had found the galley's big urn and done something to it; John could smell it from across the room. He stood looking at the screen and the gray box and the laptop, and at the Jackery on the deck.

"This is all from before it stopped," he said.

"Everything through Wednesday morning, the sixth. After that, only what the station can hear."

"Germany?"

"Not much. News until a few minutes before it happened, some power data, ship traffic in the North Sea." John pulled out the other chair, and Johan stayed standing. "What do you want me to look for?"

Johan took a phone out of his pocket and laid it on the table and turned it around. It had no signal and eleven percent. It was open to one message, with the time under it. *Are you all right. Answer me.* "My daughter," he said. "Lena Brandt. She is twenty-two. She studies in Hamburg."

John typed it, with the university and the phone number Johan read off to him, and the message, and the time.

**Lena Brandt, Hamburg, after Sept 5 06:00 UTC: 0 records.**

**Message delivery status: no source in archive.**

**Social posts in archive after 06:00 UTC, worldwide: 11. Matches: 0. Coverage of German sources: partial. Status: Unknown.**

He turned the screen. Johan read it, and read it again with his head a little forward. John had found eleven posts in the whole world that Tuesday, and he knew how little of a city in another country the archive could see.

"It says sent," Johan said.

"Sent means it left your ship. I can't tell you more than that. I'm sorry."

Johan nodded. He picked up the phone and turned it off, to save what was left, and put it in his pocket. "Thank you," he said, and he took the mug and went off along the passageway toward the cabin Sam had shown him.

John sat for a while. Then he pulled the keyboard over. He wanted to see where she'd been.

"Pacific Tender. Every AIS report in the archive."

**412 reports, Sept 5 06:00 UTC to Sept 6 12:00 UTC. Last position 51°01'N, 170°13'W.**

It drew the track, a straight line south of the Aleutians. On Tuesday night he'd asked the model for AIS course changes by command after 02:06, it had told him zero, and he had gone on to other things. Under the track it gave him a column of speeds, and they were all nineteen. He scrolled down, and they stopped being nineteen.

| Time (EDT) | Time (UTC) | Position | Course | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sept 5, 17:30 | 21:30 | 50°17'N, 174°33'W | 075 | 19.0 |
| Sept 5, 17:50 | 21:50 | 50°19'N, 174°24'W | 075 | 16.2 |
| Sept 5, 18:10 | 22:10 | 50°20'N, 174°17'W | 075 | 13.4 |
| Sept 5, 18:28 | 22:28 | 50°21'N, 174°11'W | 075 | 11.5 |
| Sept 6, 08:00 | Sept 6, 12:00 | 51°01'N, 170°13'W | 075 | 11.5 |

He read the first line and the fourth. She had come down seven and a half knots in a little under an hour, in even steps, fifteen and a half hours after 06:00 UTC, in the middle of the North Pacific. It took a hand on a lever to slow a ship like that. It had been in the archive since the 19:00 pass on Tuesday. He'd been pulling into his garage when it started, and he'd been making up the guest room when it finished.

"Every vessel in the archive," he said. "Speed change of more than ten percent in under two hours, not ending in a stop, after 06:30 UTC Sept 5. Drop anything in a current or on a tide."

It took eleven minutes. He got up and looked at the Jackery and came back.

**11,240 vessels. Matches: 1.**

**PACIFIC TENDER.**

He got out the pocket notebook and turned back to the page with the six questions. Next to *3*, under *ISS, 7?*, he wrote *Johan Brandt*. He looked at *SafetyNET 2141*, a little way up the line, and he took the strip of paper out of his shirt pocket and unfolded it, and read it over, and put it in the notebook at that page. Next to *SafetyNET 2141* he wrote *1 ship*.
