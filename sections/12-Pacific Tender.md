# Pacific Tender

### 9/14/2028, 05:30 ship's time - Johan

He kept her outside all night at dead slow, west until midnight and then back. At half past five, enough gray showed in the east to pick out the top of Vancouver Island. Tatoosh Island light bore 088, eleven miles. He brought the revolutions up from the bridge console. By twenty past six she was making eleven and a half knots.

Cape Flattery came abeam at a quarter to seven—black rock with the swell breaking white along its foot. Neah Bay opened behind the cape, a breakwater and a line of moored boats all lying the same way. He watched through the glasses until the point closed it off. He made coffee in the pantry behind the bridge and drank it standing at the radar.

At eight o'clock, the DSC alarm went off on the VHF at the GMDSS console.

He had sent that set more than forty calls since the fifth. Nothing had ever come back. He crossed the bridge and read the screen. *ALL SHIPS. SAFETY. CH 16.* The sender was a nine-digit number starting with 00366, a United States coast station. He silenced the alarm. The speaker on 16 opened up.

"All stations, all stations, all stations. This is John Hartman at the Coast Guard station on Ediz Hook, Port Angeles, Washington. Position four eight degrees zero eight decimal four minutes north, one two three degrees two four decimal six minutes west. There are people alive here. We have power, food, and a doctor. Answer on channel one six. Over."

An American voice, carrying static. Taped to the console above the handset were the strip of printer paper with that name on it and the card where Johan had written its number. He read the time off the GPS and logged it: *0800*. The pencil point broke through the paper on the last zero. He picked up the handset.

"Ediz Hook, Ediz Hook, Ediz Hook. This is motor vessel *Pacific Tender*, *Pacific Tender*, *Pacific Tender*, on channel one six. Over."

His voice came out rough. He hadn't used it since calling Tofino the evening before. The speaker hissed. He watched the second hand on the console clock sweep around twice.

"Ediz Hook, this is *Pacific Tender*. I hear you. Position four eight degrees two four minutes north, one two four degrees two two minutes west. Course zero nine six, speed eleven and a half, inbound. One person on board. Over."

Nothing came back. The radar showed the cape and an empty shipping lane. The GPS position had moved half a mile; he corrected the numbers and called a third time.

### 08:01 local Pacific Time, Thursday

The phone chimed on the shelf over John's bunk. It had gone off fourteen times since the tower antenna came online Wednesday afternoon—a log barge off Clallam Bay, a seiner adrift off Pillar Point, a small target drifting sideways past Sekiu at three-tenths of a knot. He had told the model not to filter. He had gotten to sleep just before five.

He opened one eye and read the screen.

**Channel 16, tower. Voice, not scheduled. 08:01:09.**

**"Ediz Hook, Ediz Hook, Ediz Hook, this is motor vessel Pacific Tender, Pacific Tender, Pacific Tender, on channel one six. Over."**

He read it twice. Seconds later he was in the passageway in his T-shirt and socks, phone in hand. A man's voice echoed from the wardroom—low, hoarse, foreign, reading coordinates. "...one person on board. Over."

John caught the back of a chair at the wardroom table. "Tower, channel 16, live."

**Local override. Live.**

"*Pacific Tender*, *Pacific Tender*, this is John Hartman on Ediz Hook. I hear you. Go ahead. Over."

The speaker hissed long enough that John checked the patch cable.

"Ediz Hook, *Pacific Tender*. Good morning. This is Second Officer Brandt." A pause. "John Hartman. You sent a message to all ships on the fifth, from Georgia. I received it. Over."

John sat down. On Sunday the model had told him nobody answered that broadcast. "Say again. You received the SafetyNET message?"

"Received. Every six hours. The last one was the ninth, zero seven four one Greenwich. Over."

John reached for his shirt pocket, remembered the notebook was in his cabin, and grabbed a printout, writing *Sept 9 0741Z* on the blank side. A backup generator in Portsmouth, Virginia, had run three days longer than he knew.

"How many on board?"

"One. The other twenty-two are dead. How many are you?"

"Five. And a dog."

Sam stepped into the wardroom in a Coast Guard T-shirt, Tyler at his elbow, Dan pushing past their legs.

"Five," Brandt said over the speaker. "Understood. Request you shift to channel two two alpha. Sixteen is for calling."

John glanced up at Sam. Twenty-three contacts sat on the AIS board; this was the first with a pulse.

Sam's mouth twitched. "I'd shift."

"Shifting two two alpha," John said. While the model switched frequencies, he pulled the AIS target.

**PACIFIC TENDER. Container ship. 48°24'N, 124°22'W. Course 096. Speed 11.4 kt. Tower receiver, first report 07:52.**
**New contact alert sent 07:52. Acknowledged 07:52.**

John had dismissed the alert in his sleep. He stared at the speed: 11.4 knots. Every other target on the screen was dead in the water or washing back and forth with the tide.

Catherine walked in holding her glasses. Sam briefed her in six words. She looked at the speaker. "Is he hurt? Ask about his breathing."

"Are you injured? Any trouble breathing?"

"No. Tired. I will anchor in your harbor about twelve thirty, four eight zero seven decimal eight north, one two three two five decimal zero west. South of the pilot station."

"What do you need?"

"What do you have?"

Sam pulled the laptop toward him. "This is Sam Reyes. I fly airplanes. We don't have a tug or a pilot. We have a forty-one-foot utility boat, and nobody here has driven it."

"Leave it at the pier. I need nothing, thank you."

Tyler sprinted down the passageway and came back winded. "Della says ask what he draws, and tell him there's deadheads inside the Hook."

John relayed it.

"Twelve point eight meters. Forty-two feet," Brandt replied. "The deadheads are in the Coast Pilot. Who is Della?"

"Our mechanic. There's a bulk carrier adrift nine miles northwest of us, *Golden Kestrel*. Moves with the tide."

"I have her on AIS."

"What do you need from us?"

"Nothing. When the ship is secure, I will come in. Tomorrow. I want a night on watch to see how the anchor holds on the tide. *Pacific Tender* standing by on one six and two two alpha."

The carrier clicked off. Sam checked the bulkhead clock—four hours until she rounded the spit.

### 11:15 ship's time - Johan

He began taking the way off her at half past ten. By a quarter past eleven she was down to six knots. *Golden Kestrel* lay a mile and a quarter off the port beam, rust-streaked and riding high, wheelhouse windows reflecting the sky. He clicked the autopilot off and steered from the helm, walking out to the bridge wing and back.

The Hook came up as a low line of driftwood and sand, then the tower near the spit's end, a lighthouse, and a hangar. He put the wheel over, bringing her around the spit, south and then west. At six knots the turn took ten minutes. The harbor opened up with the paper mill at the far end and the town climbing the hill behind it. No smoke rose from the stacks. A white ferry sat dark at her slip.

The cutter lay moored along the pier on his starboard hand, half a mile off in the sun. He raised his binoculars on the wing. Three adults and a child stood at the stern rail: a tall man with a heavy beard holding a cap, a shorter man with his arms folded, a small woman shading her eyes. The child stood on the bottom rail holding something to his chest, a dog propped beside him with its front paws on the steel.

The child raised one arm straight up.

Johan lowered the glasses and raised his hand. Then he stepped back inside; the bow needed five more degrees.

The wind was west at eight knots. She would lie the way she was heading. He checked the chart bearings: the light on 046, the hangar on 021. At half a mile he stopped the engine. At three cables he pulled the telegraph astern, heard the starting air rush in, and felt the propeller bite through the deck plates. The bow walked to starboard. On the GPS, the speed bled off: 2.1, 1.3, 0.6, 0.0, then 0.3 astern. He stopped the engine. The sounder read eighteen fathoms.

12:22. He clipped the handheld VHF to his jacket and left the bridge.

He rode the elevator down to the main deck and walked forward along the port alley between the container stacks, boots ringing on the steel plates past the lashing rods. Seven minutes to the bow. He climbed the forecastle ladder.

Dropping the hook on the brake alone in eighteen fathoms risked eleven tonnes of steel running away with the chain. He engaged the windlass motor, released the brake, knocked out the pin, and threw the stopper bar clear. He walked the anchor out under power. The chain climbed from the locker link by link, shedding rust, shaking the deck under his boots. Six minutes for the first shackle. Halfway through the second, the chain went slack in the hawse pipe. Bottom.

He screwed the brake down, disengaged the windlass gear, and looked over the bulwark. Water slipped forward along the hull plates. He slacked the brake. Ten feet of chain roared out in a cloud of red dust before he snubbed it. As the wind pushed her astern, he fed her chain a few fathoms at a time, counting the painted links. Three shackles, four, five. At six shackles on deck, he cranked the brake tight, dropped the stopper bar across the links, and drove the pin home.

Ahead of the bow, the chain rose out of the water in a long, dripping curve, humming under his palm as the ship's weight came onto it. Slowly, the links sagged back beneath the surface. She had brought up.

He pulled the black anchor ball from the forepeak locker and hoisted it up the foremast, clearing a fouled halyard on the second try.

Walking aft, he looked across the water at the cutter. The four figures still stood at the stern rail.

On the bridge wing, he shot bearings on the light, the hangar, and the mill stack, logged them, and set the GPS anchor alarm to a tenth of a mile. He rang *FINISHED WITH ENGINES* on the telegraph. Down in the engine control room, the bell rang to an empty desk. He took the elevator down, answered the telegraph himself, and logged the time in Alvarez's book. Back on the bridge, he keyed the VHF.

"Ediz Hook, *Pacific Tender*. Anchored at one three zero one. Six shackles on deck, starboard anchor. Over."

"*Pacific Tender*, Ediz Hook." Reyes's voice. "We watched it. Nice work. Call when you're ready."

He stayed on the wing into dusk, checking the bearing card at the seven o'clock tide change. The hull swung north and settled. Ashore, the town stayed black. He had steamed past Port Angeles dozens of times at night with the pilot boat running out under the harbor lights and the mountain lit up behind the mill. Tonight, only a row of lights along the spit and three yellow portholes on the cutter broke the dark.

Down in his cabin, he took a hot shower, trimmed his beard with scissors from the first aid kit—nicking the skin under his jaw—and laid out a fresh company polo.

Back on the bridge, he peeled the dried tape off the GMDSS console. He filed the index card in the back of the chart drawer under the old notices to mariners, folded the strip of printer paper into his clean shirt pocket, and lay down on the mattress opposite the alarm panel. He slept five hours straight.

### 9/15/2028, 06:10 ship's time - Johan

First light showed the bearings unchanged. The night before, he had listed the shutdown tasks on the back of a chart correction form. He worked down the list.

Port anchor first. One hook would hold her in September, but nobody would be aboard in January when the winter gales blew down the Strait. He walked the port anchor clear of the hawse on the windlass motor, knocked it out of gear, and braced himself aft of the brake wheel. At 07:12 he let go. Chain thundered up the pipe in a fog of rust; he snubbed it at two shackles, eased it to four, and locked the brake. Crossing to starboard, he veered the first chain out to eight shackles. Once both stoppers were pinned, he noted the times on his sheet.

Below decks, he tripped the quick-closing fuel valves on the main engine and isolated the bunker tanks one by one, hanging a tag on each handle. Carrying the ship's watertight door checklist, he walked the main deck and engine flat, dogging every hatch. Back on the bridge panel, two indicator lamps still burned red. He went back down. The second door had a mop handle jammed across the coaming; he kicked it clear and dogged the steel shut.

He left the No. 2 diesel generator running off its day tank—forty hours of fuel, enough to keep the ventilation fans and freshwater pumps running until Sunday. From the lifeboat locker he pulled two battery lanterns, loaded fresh cells, and hung one from the foremast and one on the poop rail.

On the provision flat outside the galley, the vegetables he had stacked in the passageway on the fifth had rotted into black water. The chill room compressor still hummed. He rested his palm against the insulated door. *Okafor. Alvarez. Bautista.* He checked the latch and walked down to the engine control room.

Alvarez's logbook lay open on the console. Johan recorded the valve closures and shutdown times beneath his previous entries, signed his name, and shut the cover for the first time since September fourth.

In his cabin, he packed a canvas bag: spare clothes, the framed photo of Lena at her school in Hamburg, his rigging knife. He cased his sextant, pulled the *Nautical Almanac*, sight reduction tables, and Puget Sound charts from the bridge drawer, and topped off his steel thermos from the galley urn. At the chart table, he made his final entry in the deck log:

*1635. At anchor Port Angeles Harbor 48-07.8N 123-25.0W. Stbd anchor 8 shackles, port 4. M/E shut down, F.O. isolated, W/T doors closed. No. 2 D/G on day tank. Anchor lights on battery fore and aft. 22 crew remain aboard. Left vessel by free-fall lifeboat. Brandt, Second Officer.*

He slid the deck log and official log into his bag, tapped the barometer glass, and switched off the radar.

Aft on the poop deck, the enclosed orange lifeboat sat nose-down on its thirty-five-degree launch skid, sixty feet above the water. He had inspected it every week for two years and never launched it. He walked the platform: lashings clear, hatch gasket clean, release hook set. Climbing through the stern hatch, he strapped his bag, chart roll, and sextant box beneath the seats and bumped the starter. The diesel fired on the first revolution. He checked the raw-water exhaust discharge over the side, ran it for two minutes, and shut it down.

Stepping back onto the platform, he pulled the heavy steel safety pin and hung it on its bulkhead hook. Inside the boat, he dogged the stern hatch and keyed his handheld.

"Ediz Hook, *Pacific Tender*. I leave her now. Free-fall lifeboat, one person. To you in fifteen minutes. Over."

"*Pacific Tender*, Ediz Hook," Hartman answered. "We're at the float below the pier. We'll take your line."

Johan strapped himself into the aft-facing helmsman's seat, cinching the four-point harness tight and pressing his head back against the padded rest. Through the forward polycarbonate port, the cutter was a gray sliver across the flat harbor. He gripped the hydraulic release pump handle and stroked it fourteen times. The hook tripped, and the skid dropped away beneath him.

### 16:52 local Pacific Time

John saw the drop before the sound reached the pier. The orange hull slid off the container ship's stern ramp, fell nose-first, and punched into the harbor in a geyser of white water as tall as the boat. It vanished beneath the surface. A second later the crack of the impact echoed across the anchorage. Catherine gripped John's forearm. A hundred yards astern of the ship, the lifeboat breached, rolled upright, and settled level. The diesel clattered to life.

"Well," Sam said. "That's one way."

Eleven minutes later the craft idled up to the float—the size of a short school bus, salt water streaming down its viewports. The engine shifted to neutral a yard off the timber rub-rail. The stern hatch swung open, and a broad-shouldered man stepped onto the aft grating with a coiled bow line.

"Take it," he called, and tossed the coil. John caught it against his chest. "Not in your hand. Make it fast." John took two turns around a cleat while Sam caught the stern line.

The man handed across a canvas kit bag, a fitted mahogany box, a roll of paper charts, and a dented steel thermos, then stepped onto the float. He stood nearly as tall as John and half again as wide, wearing a creased dark polo with a shipping line's crest on the breast. A fresh razor nick marked the underside of his jaw below a short gray-blond beard. Red-rimmed eyes scanned John's boots and cargo pants, then checked the hitches on the cleats.

"Hartman."

"John." They shook—a heavy, calloused grip. "Glad you're here."

"Brandt. Johan." He pronounced it *Yo-hahn*. He looked past John's shoulder at Catherine. "You are the doctor."

Catherine already had two fingers pressed to his radial artery, her eyes on his nail beds. "When did you last sleep?"

"Last night. Five hours."

"Before that?"

"Half an hour at a time. Since the fifth."

"Any shortness of breath since the fifth? Headache? Nausea?"

"No."

She counted for fifteen seconds and released his wrist. Sam introduced himself and got the same firm shake. As Johan's left arm dropped back to his side, John noticed the middle and ring fingers were missing at the knuckle—old, white-scarred stumps.

Johan unbuttoned his breast pocket, drew out a strip of thermal paper folded in quarters, and handed it to John.

The creases were worn soft. John unfolded it: *ALL SHIPS ALL SHIPS ALL SHIPS. THIS IS JOHN HARTMAN, ALPHARETTA, GEORGIA, USA. TWO SURVIVORS HERE.* It listed his cell number. He had typed that prompt behind the closed door of his home office eleven days ago; he had never seen the Inmarsat printout from a ship's terminal.

"That phone number wasn't much help after Wednesday," John said.

"On the first day, I thought it was the ship," Johan said. "Fumigant gas from a leaking container. I wore a breathing pack and tested the air. Then the printer spat this out. So I knew it was not the ship."

John refolded the slip along its creases and held it out.

"Keep it," Johan said. "It is yours." John tucked it into his shirt pocket.

Sam looked across the water at the battery lantern glowing on the freighter's stern. "Where'd you sleep out there?"

"On the bridge deck. Across from the alarm panel."

"Why across from it?"

"So I must stand up to silence it."

Sam nodded once. "I was on a Delta A350 out of Atlanta. Four pilots up front, three hundred nineteen in the back. Woke up on my bunk break over Saskatchewan. Put it down in Edmonton."

"With whom?"

"By myself."

Johan studied him. Without a word, he crouched, unscrewed the cup from his steel thermos, filled it with black coffee, and handed it to Sam. Sam knocked it back; his eyes watered, and he held the cup out for a second pour. Johan refilled it and passed it to John. It tasted like hot tar.

"We have an officer's stateroom open on *Active*," John said. "Or the forty-one-footer is yours if you prefer sleeping on your ship."

"No. She is secure. Two anchors down, watertight doors dogged, fuel isolated. The generator will run until Sunday." Johan glanced back at the orange lifeboat tied to the float. "That does not go back up the davit. It is yours now."

He shouldered his kit bag, tucked the sextant case under his left arm, and let Sam carry the charts up the brow. Stepping onto the cutter's quarterdeck, Johan halted in front of the watch stand. The ship's log lay open with a pen resting in the gutter. He read down the page—the five crew names John had logged on the seventh beside the compartments where they had died: *Whitlock, quarterdeck. Tran, control room. Haas, galley.*

Johan flipped to a clean page, picked up the pen, and wrote: *14 Sept 2028, 1722. Brandt, J., 2nd Off., M/V Pacific Tender, aboard.* He laid the pen back in the crease.

### 18:00 local Pacific Time - Johan

All six of them ate in the sick bay. With only two stools, Johan sat on the deck plates inside the doorway, spine against the bulkhead, balancing his tin plate on his knees. Fried potato cakes with melted cheddar. He hadn't eaten a meal in the same room with another human being since the ship's Filipino cook made pork adobo on the third. The brown-and-black dog padded over, pressed its wet nose into the scarred gap on his left hand, and exhaled. Johan held his hand still.

In the lower rack, the Coast Guard mechanic lay propped on pillows, left arm in a sling, right leg casted to the knee, yellow bruising fading around her cheekbones. She had watched him since he sat down. She swallowed her bite of potato.

"Who tended your fuel purifiers?"

"I did."

"You a deck officer or an engineer?"

"Deck." He gave her the short version, because she had the eyes of a chief who knew the machinery: pumping the settling tank by hand with Alvarez's notebook propped on the rail; sleeping through the purifier alarm Thursday night; losing the main engine on a fouled service tank at 0400 Saturday; rolling beam-on in the trough for thirty-four hours while he rigged a chain fall to pull the purifier bowl. One hundred and twenty conical steel discs, scraped clean with a putty knife.

She stopped chewing. "You tore down an Alfa Laval bowl solo in a seaway?"

"Both units. With the manual open on the workbench."

"One-handed."

"I hugged the disc stack against my chest."

"What'd you screw up?"

"First restart, I forgot to crack the operating water valve."

The corner of her mouth lifted. "Everybody does that once." She passed her empty plate to the doctor. "I've got a bilge pump on the pier I can't reach with this cast. Tomorrow."

On the stool beside the medicine cabinet, the thin boy sat with a ruled spiral notebook across his knees. He slid off the stool, walked over with a stubby pencil, and looked down at Johan. "How much fresh water is on your ship?"

"Three hundred tonnes leaving Yokohama. Call it two hundred fifty now."

"How many liters in a tonne?"

"One thousand."

The boy bent over his knee and wrote the figure. The page was ruled into neat columns with a straightedge: *USCGC Active*, daily liter counts, dates. At the top, *5 people* had been crossed out and replaced with *6*, followed by long division in the margin.

"You update that every day?" Johan asked.

"Yes, sir."

"Then it is an official log. Who signs it?"

The boy looked down at the paper.

"A log must be signed at the close of the watch," Johan said, "or it is only scrap paper."

The boy set the notebook against the bunk rail, printed his name and the date carefully at the foot of the column, and turned it for inspection. Johan checked the entry, nodded once, and finished his potatoes.

### 21:15 local Pacific Time - John

John was working at the wardroom table when Johan walked in carrying two mugs. He had scrubbed out the galley's thirty-cup percolator; the wardroom smelled like roasted chicory. Johan stood behind the chair, studying the aluminum dev box, the laptop, and the portable battery pack on the deck.

"All of this is from before?" Johan asked.

"Through 0800 Eastern on Wednesday the sixth. After that, only local radio and AIS off the spit."

"And Germany?"

"Wire reports up to two minutes before the event, European grid frequency drops, North Sea AIS. Not much else." John pushed out the spare chair; Johan remained standing. "Who are we looking for?"

Johan pulled a dead smartphone from his pocket and laid it on the table. Nine percent battery when he'd powered it down. Before turning it off, he had left it open to a single WhatsApp message sent at 06:14 UTC on the fifth: *Are you all right. Answer me.*

"My daughter," Johan said. "Lena Brandt. Twenty-two. University of Hamburg."

John entered her name, the university, her mobile number, and the message timestamp.

**Lena Brandt, Hamburg, after Sept 5 06:00 UTC: 0 records.**
**Message delivery status: no source in archive.**
**Social posts in archive after 06:00 UTC, worldwide: 11. Matches: 0. Coverage of German sources: partial. Status: Unknown.**

John angled the monitor toward him. Johan leaned forward and read the four lines twice. Out of eight billion people, the global scrape had caught eleven social media posts after 06:00 UTC on Tuesday.

"My phone showed one check mark," Johan said. "Sent."

"That just means the packet cleared your ship's satellite dome. I don't have the German cell tower logs. I'm sorry."

Johan nodded, slipped the dark phone back into his pocket, and picked up his mug. "Thank you." He walked aft toward his stateroom.

John sat alone with the hum of the server fans. After a minute, he pulled the keyboard closer.

"Pacific Tender. Pull every AIS report in the archive."

**412 reports, Sept 5 06:00 UTC to Sept 6 12:00 UTC. Last position 51°01'N, 170°13'W.**

The map plotted a straight track south of the Aleutian chain. On Tuesday night in Alpharetta, John had queried the database for any vessel making a helm course change after 02:06 EDT; the model had returned zero, because Johan hadn't touched the autopilot heading. John looked at the speed column instead. For fifteen hours after the event, the ship had steamed east at 19.0 knots. Then the numbers stepped down:

| Time (EDT) | Time (UTC) | Position | Course | Speed | 
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | 
| Sept 5, 17:30 | 21:30 | 50°17'N, 174°33'W | 075 | 19.0 | 
| Sept 5, 17:50 | 21:50 | 50°19'N, 174°24'W | 075 | 16.2 | 
| Sept 5, 18:10 | 22:10 | 50°20'N, 174°17'W | 075 | 13.4 | 
| Sept 5, 18:28 | 22:28 | 50°21'N, 174°11'W | 075 | 11.5 | 
| Sept 6, 08:00 | 12:00 | 51°01'N, 170°13'W | 075 | 11.5 | 

Seven and a half knots bled off in fifty-eight minutes, stepped down in three smooth increments in the middle of the North Pacific. An engine didn't fail in neat three-knot steps; a human hand on a bridge telegraph had pulled that throttle back. The proof had been sitting on his hard drive since Tuesday evening in Georgia.

"Query every vessel in the archive," he said. "Filter for speed changes greater than ten percent under two hours, not ending in a dead stop, after 06:30 UTC September fifth. Exclude tidal and current drift."

The local model crunched the table for fifteen minutes.

**11,240 vessels evaluated. Matches: 1.**
**PACIFIC TENDER.**

John opened his pocket notebook to the page with his original questions. Under *Question 3: Who else is alive?*, beneath *ISS (7?)*, he wrote *Johan Brandt*. He pulled the worn slip of Inmarsat thermal paper from his shirt pocket, pressed it flat between the pages, and next to his note for *SafetyNET 2141Z*, wrote: *1 ship*.
