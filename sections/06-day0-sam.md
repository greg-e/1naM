# 6 — Sam, Day 0

The call had barely gone dead in his hand before it hit him, all of it, all at once, in a way the morning's shock had somehow kept at arm's length until now. Elena. Maya. Gone the same way as everyone else on this earth had apparently gone, quietly, in their own bed, on an ordinary Monday night that had no business turning into the last one. He didn't remember sliding down the wall of the gate area until he was already sitting on the floor with his back against it, the phone still clutched in one hand like it might ring again if he just held onto it hard enough. He didn't try to be quiet about any of it. There was no one left in the entire building to hear him, and some distant, still-functioning part of his brain noted that fact with a kind of flat, useless clarity even while the rest of him came apart completely.

He didn't know how long he stayed like that. Long enough that the light through the terminal windows had shifted several degrees toward evening by the time he became aware of it again. Call it twenty minutes. Could have been twice that. It didn't matter, in the end, because it always ended the same way it had ended twenty-five years of training into him: a pilot's job was to act, not to sit with it, and sitting with it could come later, except later kept arriving and he kept discovering there wasn't going to be a version of later where that was actually true. So he did what he'd always done instead. He got up.

Four o'clock, Wednesday, PDK. He had a promise on the table now, a real one, made to a man he'd never met who had gone and stood in his own house and looked at his own dead family so Sam wouldn't have to hear it secondhand from a stranger. That was worth something. That was worth getting up off a terminal floor for, even today, even like this.

He didn't leave right away. Some practical, unglamorous part of him — the same part that had run checklists out loud to nobody all morning — pointed out that he hadn't had water or food since the flight, and that a phone worth anything at four percent wasn't worth anything at all. He raided the dead coffee kiosk near the gate first, grabbed two bottles of water and a fistful of the granola bars and bagged snacks stacked behind the register, and ate one of them standing right there, mechanically, tasting almost nothing. A charging cable turned up in the gate agent's abandoned podium, still plugged into the wall and still, astonishingly, live — one more small mercy from whatever was keeping this province's lights on — and he let his phone pull what it could while he finished the water, watching the battery icon climb a few grudging percentage points at a time. It wasn't much. It would have to be enough.

He walked back out through the same service door he'd come in through, pockets heavier with everything he'd taken, and the A330 was still sitting exactly where he'd left it, huge and dark and silent on the main ramp, wingtip lights long since drained of whatever battery power they'd had left. He didn't look at it long. There was nothing left to do for that aircraft, and he wasn't going to be the kind of man who stood around grieving a machine when there was actual work in front of him.

The general aviation apron sat off to one side of the terminal, a scatter of smaller aircraft tied down for a night that had never properly ended — a handful of PC-12s, a couple of King Airs, and further along, a row of the province's water bombers parked wingtip to wingtip, big amphibious tankers waiting on a fire season nobody was going to call this year. It came down to the King Airs or the PC-12s. The King Airs he considered longer — twin engines, real redundancy, more airplane than he strictly needed — but something about the idea of managing two engines and a heavier systems load on no sleep and a day like today didn't sit right. The PC-12s were simpler. Single engine, single lever for power, forgiving handling, and five of them sitting right there in a neat row like the world had been saving them for exactly this. Simple was what he had left in him tonight.

Getting into one turned out to be the easy part. Getting one running was not. Cabin doors were unlocked on two of the five — small mercies, FBO staff who hadn't bothered locking up rental aircraft on a slow Tuesday morning — but neither of those had keys in the ignition, and a PC-12 wasn't a car he could hotwire out of pure stubbornness. He tried the FBO building next, a low prefab structure with PAL AVIATION stencilled across the front in faded blue, and found the front door locked but a side window someone had left cracked for ventilation, wide enough for a determined man to force the rest of the way. Inside, past a dark reception desk and a coffee maker still holding a half-pot gone cold, he found what he was actually looking for: a pegboard behind the counter, key fobs hanging in neat rows, each one tagged with a tail number in a dispatcher's careful handwriting. It took him longer than he wanted to admit, cross-referencing tags against the registrations painted on the tails outside through the window, before he found a match — a well-kept PC-12 near the end of the row, tie-downs still cinched tight, prop covers on, the kind of aircraft somebody had clearly loved.

The lights were still on in the FBO office. That stopped him for a second on his way back out, longer than it probably should have — the overhead fluorescents humming steady and ordinary, the little digital clock on the wall blinking the correct time, a space heater under the desk still ticking warm. Whatever had taken out the power on the mainland hadn't touched this province yet, or maybe never would; half of Newfoundland and Labrador ran on hydro out of dams so enormous they barely needed anyone minding them, and it wouldn't surprise him at all if those kept turning long after everything else in the world had gone quiet. He didn't examine the thought much further than that. It was one small, strange mercy in a day that hadn't offered him many, and he decided to just take it.

He did a full walk-around before he touched anything else, the same discipline that had kept two hundred and forty passengers alive through worse mornings than this one — tires, control surfaces, fuel caps, the belly for anything leaking that shouldn't be. The aircraft was clean. Whoever owned it took care of it. That was worth something too.

Fuel was the part he'd expected to dread, and it wasn't the fueling itself that gave him trouble. A Pilatus sat low enough to the ramp that reaching the wing tanks meant nothing more than an ordinary step stool from the FBO's tool room — no ladder, no harness, none of the whole small ground crew it took to fuel a widebody. He'd flown a few hundred thousand pounds of Jet-A around the world over the years and never once, not a single time, actually held the nozzle himself. Somebody else's job, always, on every airplane he'd ever flown professionally. It struck him as strange, working that out for the first time at forty-three years old, standing alone on an empty ramp with a stool under one arm.

Finding something to fuel it *with* was the actual fight. No line crew left to run a truck, no one to call for a top-off, and the single Jet-A bowser parked near the fuel farm sat locked up tight — cab keyless, pump panel keyless, a card-lock system wanting an account number he didn't have and a network authorization that, powered province or not, wasn't coming back online to approve anything for him tonight. He went back through the FBO's pegboard a second time, more carefully, and found a smaller ring behind the first one tagged GROUND EQUIPMENT in the same careful dispatcher's hand — the first thing all day that had simply gone right without a fight. The bowser turned over rough and cold on the third try, and once the engine caught, the pump panel woke up with it. No bypass. No hotwiring. Just a truck somebody had thought to leave the keys for.

The fueling itself, once he had a working truck, was almost anticlimactic after that — a short hose, the stool to get his eyeline over the wing, and the slow, patient work of watching fuel climb by sight against the tank markings because the bowser's own gauge had quit working years before tonight. It took the better part of an hour, unglamorous and slow, and left his hands smelling like Jet-A for the rest of the night. He didn't mind. It didn't leave much room in his head for anything else while he did it.

He spent a little of the charge he'd fought for on one last piece of business before he committed to anything. The signal here was better than it had any right to be — another small gift from whatever grid quirk was keeping this whole province lit — and he pulled real weather off an aviation app that, against every reasonable expectation, still loaded: clear skies most of the way down the eastern seaboard, a weak front well south of any route he'd actually be flying, nothing that should trouble a PC-12 at altitude. He sat in the cockpit with a chart open on his knee and did the math properly, the way training had drilled into him to do it even when no controller on earth was ever going to check his work. Gander to Atlanta direct was more distance than he wanted to fly alone, exhausted, on a day like today. Split into two legs, it looked a great deal more sane. Bangor sat almost exactly where he needed a fuel stop to be — a real field, long runway, the kind of place built to handle exactly this sort of transatlantic diversion, same as Gander itself had been built for. Atlanta could wait until morning. He had until four o'clock Wednesday, not four o'clock today, and he wasn't going to throw away the only advantage he had left — a full night's rest before the second leg — just to prove to himself he could push through on fumes.

What the math didn't have a line item for was everything that had already happened since the call dropped — the twenty minutes on the floor that might have been forty, the food, the keys, the better part of an hour spent watching fuel climb by eye against a wing tank marking. He'd budgeted the flight itself carefully and hadn't once stopped to add up the ground time eating into the daylight around it, and when he finally checked the actual clock against the plan instead of just the plan against itself, it read a good deal later than the version of tonight he'd been carrying in his head.

He filed no flight plan, because there was no one left anywhere to receive one, but he wrote the numbers down anyway on a scrap of paper from the FBO's own notepad and tucked it into his shirt pocket, headings and fuel burn and a planned arrival time at Bangor, the same disciplined habit he'd have followed on any ordinary day with two hundred souls behind him instead of an empty cabin and a promise to keep. Don't launch with holes you already know about. He'd learned that lesson from men who hadn't survived learning it any other way, and today of all days felt like exactly the wrong day to unlearn it.

He climbed in, ran the checklist out loud to no one the way he had that morning in a different, larger aircraft, and taxied out past the row of tied-down King Airs and the silent water bombers and his own dead widebody sitting huge and abandoned on the main ramp behind him. The runway lights came up clean when he keyed the mic out of pure habit, one more small mercy from a grid that hadn't failed here yet.

He held short a moment longer than he needed to, looking west down the runway at what was left of the day — a thin band of orange burning low over the water, going out fast, nothing like the margin he'd promised himself back at the fuel truck. It wasn't nothing. It also wasn't what he'd planned. He ran the numbers one more time anyway, the same stubborn habit, and let the answer talk him into moving instead of stopping: Bangor was a real, lit, purpose-built field, same as this one, and it would have runway lights waiting for him whatever time he actually got there. It had to. He wasn't going to sit on a taxiway all night arguing with a clock he couldn't get back.

Four o'clock, Wednesday, PDK.

He pushed the power lever forward and lifted off into a sky that was running out of light faster than his flight plan said it should, chasing what was left of it west, and went to keep the only promise he had left.
