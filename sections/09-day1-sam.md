# 9 — Sam, Day 1

He slept in four-hour-old fits in the left seat with his flight jacket bunched up against the window, and woke somewhere past first light to a cold cockpit and a runway stretching out gray and enormous in front of him, the same length that had saved his life a few hours earlier now just looking like what it actually was in daylight — a relic, built for bombers that had stopped flying this route before he was born, kept alive by whatever hardened Cold War circuitry nobody had bothered to decommission along with everything else. He sat with that a minute, working the stiffness out of his neck, before he let himself think about the day ahead.

The fuel gauge was the first real problem, and it didn't take him long to confirm what he'd already half-known circling in last night. Between the planned leg, the failed approach, and two more diversions before Loring had finally answered him, he'd burned through more of the tank than any sane flight plan accounted for. What was left wasn't nothing, but it wasn't Atlanta either — not from here, not over eleven hundred nautical miles with a proper reserve on the other end, not in an aircraft he needed to still trust completely by the time he got there.

He went looking anyway, because hope was cheap and the alternative was worse. The old fuel farm sat a quarter mile off the main ramp, tanks the size of houses behind a chain-link fence with a rusted padlock that gave up faster than the one in St. John's had, and it told him within thirty seconds of getting inside that this particular hope wasn't paying off. No bowser. No pump truck. Whatever fuel infrastructure a working Air Force base needed, decommissioning had apparently taken all of it away decades ago and left the concrete pads and the empty tank farm standing here like a movie set, a base that could still turn its own lights on for a stranger in the dark but had nothing left in it that actually burned. He checked two hangars on the way back for good measure, found nothing but empty tie-down chains and a single dead forklift, and made his peace with it standing on the ramp in the cold morning air.

Bangor was still the answer. It had always been the answer — the dark last night had been the only thing wrong with it, and daylight didn't care about pilot-controlled lighting. A real field, a real FBO, the kind of place business jets refueled at on their way to somewhere more interesting, and close enough that even his diminished tank could make the hop without touching the reserve he wasn't willing to touch again today. He ran the numbers twice or he wouldn't have trusted himself, climbed in, and flew the short leg south with the sun properly up now, the whole stretch of Maine underneath him quiet and green and giving away nothing about what had or hadn't survived down there.

Bangor in daylight looked almost ordinary from altitude — hangars, a terminal building, a scatter of parked aircraft that made a lot more sense now than they had as absences in the dark. He did the low pass anyway, out of the same discipline that had kept him alive twice already on this trip, and found the runway clean, the ramp clear, nothing waiting for him that hadn't been waiting the night before when he just hadn't been able to see it. The FBO here turned out kinder than St. John's had — a self-serve card lot that didn't need power at all, just gravity and a hand crank on the pump, meant for exactly this kind of situation by a company that had apparently planned for outages better than most. It cost him forty minutes and a sore shoulder instead of the better part of an hour and a broken lock. He topped off both tanks properly this time, no guessing by eye against a tank marking, and allowed himself, briefly, something close to satisfaction.

He tried John before he did anything else, standing on the ramp with his phone held up at an empty sky the same way he had outside the FBO in St. John's two nights and a lifetime ago. No bars. Not one, not flickering, not almost — just the small, final icon a phone made when there was nothing left anywhere nearby for it to talk to. He hadn't really expected different. Maine's grid had already told him everything it had to say about itself. He put the phone away and didn't waste any more time on it.

What was left was just the plan, and he made himself walk through it the same deliberate way he'd have briefed a real crew if he'd had one. Bangor to Atlanta direct — a real distance, but an honest one this time, well inside what a full tank actually offered him, with fuel to spare on the other end instead of fumes. Call it four hours in the air if the winds behaved, less if he got lucky. He'd lost the whole overnight buffer he'd built into yesterday's plan, but he'd never actually needed all of it — just enough to land rested instead of reckless, and he'd gotten that much, even if it hadn't come from the airport he'd meant to get it from. Wheels up by mid-morning put him into PDK with hours to spare before four o'clock, not the razor's edge he'd been quietly bracing for since Limestone's lights had first come up out of the dark.

He climbed back in, ran the checklist out loud one more time to no one, and pointed the aircraft south for good — Georgia at the end of it now instead of just an idea, a runway he'd never actually seen with his own eyes waiting on the other end of it, and a man he still hadn't met standing somewhere underneath all that distance, keeping the same promise Sam was.
