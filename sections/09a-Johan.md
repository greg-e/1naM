# Johan Brandt

### 9/9/2028, 04:00 ship's time

The deck had gone quiet under him, and that woke him.

The panel was red from end to end with the buzzer going. M/E SHUTDOWN. F.O. INLET LOW PRESS. He silenced it and looked at the GPS: 9.4 knots and dropping, 51°54' north, 147°20' west. The radar was empty out to twenty-four miles. He wrote the position down and switched on the two red all-round lights that meant not under command, and logged those.

By the time he reached the control room she had fallen off beam to the swell out of the southwest and was rolling, slow and deep, with all those containers stacked up high.

The alarm list showed No. 1 PURIFIER TRIP, Thursday 23:12, and F.O. SERVICE TANK LOW LEVEL, Friday 23:40. After each one, in the same column, ACCEPTED BRIDGE. He had no memory of either. He would have reached up off the mattress, pressed the button, and gone back to sleep. He had filled the settling tank on Thursday and thought that was the job. With both purifiers down, nothing had gone from the settling tank to the service tank in twenty-nine hours, and the engine had used what was there.

He copied the two lines into the deck log as they stood.

The purifier manual was on the shelf over Alvarez's desk. He took it down to the flat with the tool board and a chain hoist and opened No. 1. The hood came off with eight bolts. The lock ring took a special spanner, clipped to the board with a label on it, and it wouldn't move until he hit the spanner with a four-pound hammer. The bowl came up on the hoist and swung with every roll, so he waited for the top of each one to bring it another few inches. The disc stack came out in one lump, the sludge baked between the discs like tar. The manual said a hundred and twenty discs. His left hand was two fingers short and couldn't span them, so he carried them to the tray a few at a time against his chest and cleaned them one by one in diesel on the deck plates, with a boot wedged against the tray to stop it sliding.

He slept on the plates for an hour on Saturday night. He had it back together by two on Sunday morning, and it tripped on the first start because he'd left the operating water valve shut. It ran on the second. After that he stood on the flat with the ship rolling and watched the discharge gauge hold steady while the service tank came up a little at a time. He opened No. 2 as well, because Alvarez had meant to. Its disc stack was baked solid too.

By one o'clock on Sunday the service tank read sixty percent, and he vented the fuel pumps. He stood at the control room console and gave the main engine starting air. She turned over and didn't fire. The first try took the start air down from thirty bar to twenty-six. He went back and vented the pumps again and tried, and she didn't fire. He let the compressors bring it back up to thirty and tried a third time at 14:02, and the cylinders caught, one and then all of them, and it came up through the plates into his boots.

He took her up to eleven and a half knots from the bridge, a few revolutions at a time, and brought her back to 096. He switched off the two red lights and logged it: not under command thirty-four hours two minutes, drifted thirty-one miles.

The printer's last strip lay in the tray, Friday, 21:41. Nothing had come since.

He ate cold rice out of the pot, standing at the radar. Then he dragged the mattress across the bridge to the far bulkhead, where he'd have to get up and walk to reach the panel, and lay down.
