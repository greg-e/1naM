# The Relay

### 10/12/2028, 20:30 local Pacific Time - John

John had the frequency sheet out of its sleeve on the wardroom table, under the lamp, and he was reading the pencil.

He had read the typed part a dozen times since Arthur Keene's house. The voice frequencies, the packet frequency, the modes. He had never read the margins. Arthur's notes were small and slanted and mostly abbreviations, and some had been written over older ones that had been rubbed out. Beside the voice entries, boxed, there were three lines:

*ISS: mem 12*
*TONE OFF!!*
*HI pwr*

John turned the smaller radio around so he could see its face, and opened the manual to the menu pages, and went through memory 12 one setting at a time with his finger on the page. The frequencies were right. They had always been right; he had checked them before every pass. Two lines down, the radio was sending a tone under his voice at sixty-seven hertz. On the line under that, the power was set to low.

"Johan," he said.

Johan was at the other end of the table with the HF manual. He came and looked over John's shoulder.

"It's been sending a tone with every call," John said. "For the repeater in town, I'd guess. That's what Arthur used this channel for when he wasn't working the station. And the power's turned down to five watts." He put his finger on the margin. "He wrote it down. Tone off. He gave it two exclamation points."

"And we checked the frequencies."

"I checked the frequencies. That's all I checked."

Johan looked at the radio for a while. "On a ship there is a list for this," he said. "Before a set is used, every line. Not only the ones you know about."

"I know."

"Then make the list."

John turned the tone off and put the power on high and wrote both changes on the back of the sheet with the date. Then he took a clean page and wrote down every line in the menu for memory 12, what it said and what it should say, and what the manual said each one did. It filled the page. He put it in the sleeve with the frequency sheet.

Della had him bring the power supply to the sick bay in the morning so she could read the label on the back. "Thirty amps," she said. "You won't hurt it at fifty watts. Don't key it for ten minutes straight."

### 10/16/2028, 12:10 local Pacific Time

They had worked three daytime passes since the change, Saturday, Sunday, and this one. Nobody had been calling on any of them. John had called on each pass from the time the station came over the mountains until it went down into the Strait, at full power with no tone, and the speaker had given him nothing back but noise.

"They're not listening," Sam said. He was in the doorway with a mug. "They were on the thirtieth. Somebody was sitting at it."

"Somebody's got to be at the radio when it goes over us," John said. "And they don't know there's anybody here to be at it for."

He opened the frequency sheet again. Below the voice entries was the packet line, 145.825, and in Arthur's pencil beside it, *APRS digi — msgs relayed, no op needed*. The manual for the smaller set had a chapter on it. The radio had its own modem and could send a short line of text that the station would repeat back down to anyone listening, with nobody aboard touching anything. If someone looked at the station's radio screen, the last messages would be on it.

Arthur's laptop had the program for it. It wanted a callsign for the sender. John didn't have one, and he wasn't going to use a dead man's. He typed *EDIZHK*, and the program took it.

The message could be sixty-seven characters long. He wrote it on paper first and counted, with Tyler reading it back to him.

*PORT ANGELES WA. 6 ALIVE. LISTENING EVERY PASS 145.800.*

"Fifty-five," Tyler said. "You've got room."

"Room for what?"

Tyler thought about it. "Nothing," he said. "That's good."

### 10/17/2028, 11:42 local Pacific Time

The pass came up out of the southwest at 11:38, and John sent the line every two minutes, the way the manual said, so the station's relay wouldn't get it stepped on. Each time the radio made a short braying burst on the speaker and went quiet. Johan held the antenna outside. Tyler sat at the laptop with the tracking program open in one window and the packet window in the other.

At 11:46 the speaker brayed when John hadn't touched anything.

Tyler leaned in to the screen. A line had come up in the packet window, and he read it out with his finger under it.

"EDIZHK, then a bunch of letters, then R-S-zero-I-S-S with a star. Then Port Angeles W-A, six alive, listening every pass." He looked up. "It came back. It says ISS."

John read it over his shoulder. The star after the station's name meant it had gone up and been sent back down by the relay on the station. Their message had been aboard, for a second, and had come back down to anyone in two thousand kilometers who was listening.

"Again," he said.

They got it back twice more on that pass. Johan came in with the cable over his arm, and John turned the laptop so he could see.

"It heard us," Johan said.

"The relay heard us. It doesn't take a person." John looked at the line on the screen. "But it's on their radio now. If anybody looks."

Johan sat down with the log and wrote it, the times and the three returns, and under it, *Text relayed by station. Voice reply: none.*

They sent it on every daytime pass for two days.

### 10/19/2028, 10:51 local Pacific Time

The pass was from the northwest, high, out over the water. At 10:53 the noise on 145.800 changed, and a man's voice came out of the speaker, accented, not the commander.

"Port Angeles, Port Angeles, this is NA1SS. NA1SS calling Port Angeles. We have your message. Over."

Tyler was standing. John was already keying.

"NA1SS, this is Ediz Hook, Port Angeles. We read you. Six alive. Over."

He let go.

"Port Angeles, NA1SS. I hear something. A voice under the noise. Unreadable. Say again, slowly. Over."

John said it again, slowly, with the position. Outside, Johan swung the antenna and the voice on the speaker went thin and came back.

"Port Angeles, NA1SS. Still under the noise. I have a voice but no words. We will call you every pass over you. Every pass. Over."

He called back twice more. The second time the voice on the speaker said something they couldn't make out, and then the noise came up over it, and at 11:01 the pass was gone.

Johan came in. He didn't have the cable over his arm this time; he'd left it on the pavement.

"They heard you," he said.

"Under the noise. Not words." John looked at the power on the display. Full. No tone. "It's not the radio this time."

"No," Johan said. "It is me."

"You can't know that."

"When he spoke and I moved, he went away. And came back." Johan held up his hands a little apart, the way he'd been holding the antenna. "The station goes over in nine minutes. I point where I think it is. I am late. Or I am low. Or I am turned wrong."

Tyler had sat down at the tracking laptop again. He had the pass on the screen, a curved line across a circle, with the numbers for where the station had been each second.

"It tells you," he said. "Where it is. Up and around. The whole time."

"I can't read a screen and hold the antenna."

"I can read it to you," Tyler said.

### 10/20/2028, 12:22 local Pacific Time

They tried it on the next pass with nobody on the other end, for practice. Tyler sat in the doorway of the operations building with the laptop on his knees and the tracking window open, and every ten seconds he called the two numbers to Johan on the pavement: the compass bearing, and how high.

"Two-two-zero, fourteen. Two-two-five, nineteen. Two-three-two, twenty-five."

Johan turned on his heel with the antenna up and his elbows in, and tipped it as the numbers went up. He had found a way to rest the boom against his shoulder. On the speaker the station's own noise rose and fell, and John, at the table, wrote down where it fell away.

At the top of the pass the numbers came faster than Tyler could say them and he started skipping. Johan went past where he should have been and came back.

"Again," Johan said, when it was gone. "Next one."

Tyler wrote the pass in his notebook, below a column that had nothing to do with water anymore: the time Arthur's program said the station would come up, the time the noise had actually changed, and the difference. Fourteen seconds early, today. Eleven on Tuesday.

"It's getting earlier," he said.

"It's coming down," John said. "A little lower every day, and nobody to push it back up. So it goes around a little faster."

Tyler wrote that down too, in fewer words, and turned the notebook around so Johan could see it.
