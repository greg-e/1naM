# 7 — John, Day 1-2

Huntsville International came up out of the haze looking like any airport John had flown into, real runways, a real tower, a terminal building catching the last low sun without a single light on inside it. Sam did the low pass anyway, one slow circuit over the runway to check it clear, before he lined up for real. "Old habits," he said, when John didn't ask.

They found the rental car lot at the edge of the airport, rows of cars where the agency had left them, keys in the consoles, tanks topped off by routine servicing. They picked one that started on the first turn. It had been barely two days; batteries hadn't had time to die, and gas hadn't had time to go bad. John read the address off his notebook while Sam drove, through streets as empty as every street had been for two days. Three turns from the house, John caught himself staring at a working traffic signal cycling green to yellow to red for nobody, porch lights on down half a block. "That's not the grid holding," he said. "This whole region's on the same interconnection, and by now that zone's supposed to be dark end to end. This is a pocket. Whatever's feeding this block happened to end up balanced, generation and load close enough that nothing ever tripped. It happened for real once, back in '03. A few blocks of western New York rode out a blackout nobody could explain for days while everything around them went black. Accidental. Won't last."

The house was a single-story brick ranch with a basketball hoop bolted over the garage, and John thought of the bike against Sam's garage door. The front door gave when Sam tried it. They found a man in the living room, stretched out on a couch in front of a television that had gone dark, one arm hanging off the cushion. John checked him anyway and got the same answer he always got.

Past the living room the house was silent. They found a closed door at the end of the hall with a wooden name plaque on it, block letters spelling out TYLER over a hand-painted rocket ship.

He knocked soft, twice. "Tyler? My name's John. This is Sam. We're not going to hurt you. We just want to make sure you're okay."

Nothing, for long enough that John started working out what he'd say next. Then, muffled through the door, small and cracked: "Are you real?"

"We're real, buddy," Sam said, crouching down so his voice came from lower. "I'm a pilot. I flew a plane here. John found you on a computer, from all the way in Atlanta. We've been looking for people since Tuesday, same as I bet you've been hoping somebody would."

A long pause. Then the lock turned.

The boy who opened the door was smaller than John had pictured, ten years old, eyes red-rimmed and wary, a blanket wrapped around his shoulders despite the warm evening. He looked at both of them for a long moment. "I called 911 like eleven times," he said. "The voice kept telling me help was coming. It never came."

"It sent us instead," John said. "Just took us longer than either of us wanted."

Tyler's eyes went past them, down the hall toward the living room. "I already know about Uncle Carl," he said, flat, like he'd practiced it. "I found him. That's why I locked the door. I didn't know what else — " He stopped. Started over, smaller. "I didn't want to see him again."

"You don't have to," Sam said. "You never have to again, if you don't want to. That's not your job anymore. That's ours."

None of them had eaten a real meal since John couldn't remember, and it was Sam who noticed the boy swaying on his feet. They found a Waffle House four blocks over, the sign still lit off the same patch of grid. Around back, two cars sat in the employee spots with a person slumped in each, two people who'd stepped out for a break sometime after two in the morning and never gone back in. Neither John nor Sam mentioned it to Tyler. Inside, through the propped front door, the restaurant was as its staff had left it mid-shift: register open, a rag draped over the counter edge, the grill cold, nobody inside.

"Sit," Sam told Tyler, steering him into a booth. John was already around the counter, tying on an apron off a peg before he'd decided to. "What sounds good?" he called, sliding a spatula off its hook. "I can make anything on this menu, and about half of what isn't." Tyler looked at him like nobody had asked him what he wanted in two days. "Waffle," he said finally. "And hash browns. Scattered." John had the grill lit inside a minute. He hadn't touched a flat-top since he was twenty-two, paying his way through Georgia Tech one overnight shift at a time, and his hands remembered it anyway.

Sam slid into the booth across from Tyler and started with an easy question. "You do all that on your own? The last two days?"

Tyler nodded, working a loose thread on the edge of his blanket instead of looking up. "Nobody came," he said. "I kept thinking somebody would."

"Somebody did," Sam said. He waited a moment, then tried again. "That was your uncle, back there. Carl, you said at the door. Was that your dad's brother, or your mom's?"

Tyler picked at the syrup ring on the table. "My dad died when I was four. Car accident. I don't really remember him. It's always kind of just been me and Mom." He said it flat, an old fact. "Carl's Mom's brother. He came to stay with us in March, when she launched."

John's spatula stopped over the eggs. "Launched," he repeated.

"She's not here because she's not *here*," Tyler said, like he'd had to explain this before to people who didn't believe him. "She's an astronaut. NASA. She's been up on the space station since March. Carl was only supposed to stay till she got back down in the fall."

Sam went still. John kept working the eggs, because stopping would scare the boy. Somewhere overhead was a woman, alive as far as either of them knew, with no way of knowing whether anyone on the planet under her was still breathing. "She's on the ISS," Sam said.

"Right now," Tyler said. "Two hundred and fifty miles up. I used to watch her go over at night. I still do."

"She know?" John asked, looking up from the grill. "About any of this?"

"I don't know how she'd find out," Tyler said, and his voice wasn't steady. "I don't know if she can even see down here well enough to tell anything's different." He looked past John at Sam. "Can they even land it? If they wanted to?"

Sam didn't answer right away. "I don't know," he said finally. "But somebody built that thing to come home. That much I know."

"We'll figure it out," John said, plating eggs. "Not tonight. But we will."

Tyler didn't ask the question underneath that one, whether anyone would ever get word to her, or her to him.

John came around the counter a few minutes later with a plate in each hand: hash browns scattered the way he used to plate them at three in the morning for truckers and drunks, and a waffle with the edges gone dark the way he'd always liked his own. "Rosa used to order hers just like that," Sam said, nodding at the hash browns. Tyler didn't ask who Rosa was.

"Where'd you learn to do that?" Sam asked John.

"Told you. Worked the grill line through school." John didn't say the rest of it, that everyone he'd worked the line with thirty-some years back was probably gone now, and here he was doing it again for one scared kid instead of a full counter.

Tyler ate like someone who hadn't in a while, careful and then not careful at all, and the three of them let the rest of the meal go quiet.

They drove back to the airport in the last of the light, Tyler asleep before they'd cleared the parking lot. Sam carried him from the car to the airplane without waking him, laid him across the back row of seats with a jacket for a pillow, and came back up to the cockpit to find John sitting with both hands pressed flat against his knees, not breathing right.

It came out of him all at once, over a ten-year-old with a rocket ship on his door who'd sat behind it alone for two days waiting for somebody to come. John hadn't cried in front of anyone yet, not over a stranger in a car on his own road, not at Harry's bedside, not in Sam's hallway while Sam found his wife and daughter. It was the blanket around Tyler's shoulders that did it. He put his face in his hands in the front seat of a stranger's airplane and let it happen.

Sam didn't say anything. He sat down in the other seat and stayed there, one hand on John's shoulder for a moment and then just beside him, the two of them keeping watch over a sleeping kid.

It didn't last long. When John sat back up, wiped his face with the heel of his hand, and let out a breath that shook a little on the way out, Sam still hadn't said a word. They sat up front a while longer, not talking much, listening to Tyler breathe in his sleep.

They slept in shifts in the cabin, on a ramp gone dark once the patch of grid that had lit the Waffle House gave out around midnight, and woke to a gray sky. Breakfast was what they could scrounge from a vending machine. Tyler was quiet. He didn't cry. He asked once, very small, whether they were leaving Carl here, and John told him the truth, that they were, that there wasn't a better answer for any of them today, and that it didn't mean anyone had stopped caring about what was left in that house. Tyler nodded like he'd expected that answer.

The GA ramp had the same luck that had gotten them the rental car: a low cinderblock FBO with the lights dead and the door unlocked, and behind the counter a pegboard of tagged keys, one marked FUEL TRUCK in block handwriting. Sam had the truck started and rolling onto the ramp before John had finished checking the office for a working phone; the engine caught on the second try. "Jet-A," Sam said, reading the placard on the truck's tank. "PC-12 drinks the same thing as everything else out here. That's the whole reason Pilatus built it that way. You can gas it up anywhere a jet lands, not just wherever the avgas pump still works." He worked the hose and the ladder himself, up on the wing feeding fuel into the tanks, while John chocked the wheels and watched the quantity gauges climb.

Then he came down, wiped his hands on a rag off the truck's mirror, and nodded John and Tyler toward the nose. "Preflight," he said. "Every time, no exceptions." He looked at Tyler, hanging back with his hands in his jacket pockets. "You too. Come look."

They walked it together, Sam narrating the way John guessed he'd narrated it to a hundred new first officers, slower now and plainer, pitched for a ten-year-old and a pilot with eleven hundred hours in a Skylane and none in anything with a turbine. Fuel caps seated and safetied. Sumps drained into a little cup Sam handed to Tyler to hold ("checking for water, see how it comes out clean, that's what we want"). The prop pulled through by hand while Sam explained why one nick in any of the five blades mattered, the pitot tube uncovered and clear, the gear legs checked for strut extension. At the tail Tyler asked about the rubber strip along the leading edge. "Deice boot," Sam told him. "Inflates when we need it and breaks the ice off before it can build up and change how the wing flies. Your mother's station runs the same idea backwards. Everything up there's built to keep something *out*. Down here it's mostly built to get something *off*." Tyler didn't answer, but John caught him glancing up once, and Sam saw it too and let it go.

By the time they'd worked all the way around, John had the start of a checklist in his head he hadn't carried the morning before. "Good enough," Sam said, and gave the last inspection panel a pat. He held the door for Tyler, then for John, and climbed in last.

"Phoenix next," Sam said. He glanced back once at Tyler, already half-asleep again against the window, then at John. "What are we going to do about Tyler and his mom?"
