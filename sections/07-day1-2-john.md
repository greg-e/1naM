# 7 — John, Day 1-2

The house was dark when he got back from Marietta, every window of it, and for a second on the walk up from the car he had the disorienting thought that his own power had finally gone too — before he remembered he'd left in daylight and never turned a light on. Inside, it was all still there: the low hum of the workstation down the hall, the refrigerator, the small ordinary sounds of a house that still had a grid under it. Dan went straight to his bowl and looked back at him, and John realized he couldn't remember the last time he'd eaten. Sometime yesterday.

He made himself fix that first. Not a meal — he didn't have the patience for a meal — but eggs, four of them, and toast, and the rest of a sleeve of crackers, eating fast at the counter while Dan worked through his own dinner on the floor. He'd caught himself once already since all this started getting so far into a problem that his hands began shaking and he didn't know why, and he wasn't going to do that twice. Twenty minutes later he felt steady in a way that embarrassed him, how simple the fix had been.

Then he went back down the hall, sat at the desk, and tried to lay out what he actually had.

Not much, was the honest answer. He had Sam — a voice, a promise to meet, a man flying south out of Newfoundland in something he hadn't picked yet. He had a notebook with two crossed-out numbers in it and one that mattered. He had a machine that would still do very nearly anything he asked it, and a line to the rest of the world that was thinner every time he checked it. What he did not have was any reason to believe that Sam and a dog were not the only other living things he was ever going to find.

The thing that broke that open came to him sideways, the way the useful ones usually did — a memory of the first morning, phone against his ear on the shoulder of Old Milton, a calm synthetic voice talking about call volume and estimated response times while a dead man sat slumped in the car ahead of him. He'd written it off in the moment. A dispatch system on autopilot, a dead end, nothing. But that voice had answered him with no human anywhere in the loop, on a morning when every dispatcher in every center in the country had to be as dead as everyone else — which meant it hadn't answered *him*. It had answered everyone. Every person anywhere in the country frightened enough to dial those three digits that morning had gotten the same patient, useless voice, and every one of those calls was still sitting in a log on a server nobody was watching.

He sat up straighter without deciding to. *I wasn't the only one who tried.*

He didn't spend any time deciding whether to go after it. That decision had already been made, a few hours ago, at this same desk — the model was still running with its refusals stripped out and Claude still answering alongside it, and pointing that at emergency dispatch instead of at Delta's systems was a difference of target, not of kind. He had done the arithmetic once. It still came out the same. He started.

The work ate the whole night and looked nothing like the clean line that had found Sam. Emergency dispatch in this country was a patchwork — hundreds of county and regional systems, decades of underfunded infrastructure held together with tape long before anyone bolted an AI layer onto some fraction of it — and reaching into any of it meant treating every jurisdiction as its own separate, ugly little problem. Past midnight he stopped doing it himself and turned the model loose: not one instance on one thread but a dozen in parallel, each working a different state's dispatch infrastructure at once, filtering everything they could reach the way he'd have filtered it by hand. Not who called. Who pushed back. Who argued with the reassurance, who refused the script, who hung up angry and scared and *alive*.

Most of it was noise. Systems that had never gotten the AI layer and held nothing but silent logs of rings nobody answered. Systems that had it and had gone dark themselves in the last day, taking their data down with them. Call after call that turned out to be exactly what it looked like — someone panicking about a wreck, a break-in, a chest pain, going quiet mid-sentence the same way the whole world had gone quiet, an ordinary emergency indistinguishable from a hundred thousand others. He fell asleep sitting up twice and woke both times to Dan's nose against his hand, and kept going, because the connection he was doing it over was a little worse every hour and he could not make himself stop while it still worked at all.

By the gray edge of Thursday morning, out of what the model put — with heavy caveats — at somewhere over four thousand calls nationwide that fit the pattern, he had three that survived everything he threw at them.

A repeat caller in Huntsville, Alabama, who had argued with the triage voice for almost six minutes straight before the line cut out mid-word. A woman in Phoenix, Arizona, who had called back four separate times across two hours, each call shorter and more desperate than the one before. And a single call from Portland, Oregon — oddly calm, giving map coordinates instead of a street address. *Coordinates.* Someone who knew exactly where she was and exactly how much trouble that was.

He didn't have names. A name on an account was not the same thing as knowing a person, and he made himself keep that straight. What he had was three addresses and three mobile numbers that, as far as the model could tell before its own reach started to fray, still belonged to live accounts.

He dialed Huntsville first, on a connection his own instruments told him was down to its last few hours, maybe less.

Nothing. Not a ring, not a busy tone, not even the dead-air hum of an open line going nowhere — a flat failure notice, the sound a call makes when there is no tower left anywhere on the far end for it to reach. Phoenix gave him the identical failure, word for word.

He dialed Portland with the map he'd been half-remembering all night sitting right at the front of his mind: the Pacific Northwest running mostly on hydro the way Newfoundland did, dams that turned whether or not anyone was there to mind them, a grid that might outlast the others by days. If any of the three numbers was going to carry his voice, it was that one.

It gave him the same flat failure as the other two.

He set the phone down and sat with it a while, because it was telling him the thing he'd spent the whole night working not to hear. Nobody answering was a stranger asleep, or a stranger who'd learned not to trust an unknown number, or a stranger already dead in a room with the phone ringing beside them. This was worse and simpler than any of that: nobody's phone had anywhere left to answer *from*. Three regions, three calls, and not one of them still had a network standing to carry him into it. The collapse he'd watched crawl across his instruments all night had already been to every one of those places and moved on.

So he did not, in the end, get to say any of it. He'd spent the whole search half-rehearsing it without letting himself admit that was what he was doing — *you're not the only one, I'm real, I'm coming* — and there was no one on any of the three lines to say it to. What he had instead was a page with three addresses on it and three dead numbers, and under each one a few lines of transcript. A man in Huntsville who had argued with the machine for six minutes. A woman in Phoenix who had called back and back and back. Someone in Portland who had stayed calm enough to read out map coordinates, slowly, like she was talking to a search team that was actually coming. Three people who had been alive and fighting less than two days ago, three specific points on a map, and no way on earth to know whether any of them still was.

It changed the shape of everything that came after. This had never really been a phone call — that was a hope he'd carried because carrying it was easier than the alternative. It was three points on a map, hundreds and then thousands of miles apart, that somebody was going to have to physically go and stand on before anyone learned anything at all. And there was no clock he could read on it. The man in Huntsville, the woman in Phoenix, whoever had given those coordinates in Portland — any of them might last a week, or might have been gone before he ever found the call, and the only way to know was to fly there and see, one at a time, however long that took.

He wasn't going to be home for a while — he understood that now the way you understand weather coming in. So before he left he made his one machine into a kit he could carry: the model's weights copied onto a stack of portable drives, the most capable laptop in the house, every charger and cable and adapter he could dig out of a drawer, all of it into a hard case that had held camera gear in some other life. It would not run the model the way the workstation had. It would run enough of it.

Then he put Dan in the car, and the case on the seat, and the notebook — three addresses, three dead lines, a dead man's crew list crossed out at the top of the page — into his jacket, and pointed north toward PDK. The four o'clock still had daylight in front of it. For the first time since the morning before last, he had somewhere he actually had to be — and this time it came with the understanding that after it, there would be three more.
