# 7 — John, Day 0-1

The Anthropic floor in Midtown took up half of a mid-rise that had gone as dark and still as everything else by the time John got there, lobby doors locked, elevator banks dead, a security desk with nobody behind it. He found the stairwell instead, four flights with Dan trotting patiently ahead of him the whole way, and let himself into the office itself with the badge that, against all odds, still worked — a small, specific relief, the first confirmation that something about tonight might actually go according to plan.

The space was empty in exactly the way he'd expected, a scatter of desks nobody would ever sit at again, a kitchen island with a bowl of fruit gone soft and a cold-brew tap nobody would tap again either — and warm, gently humming, entirely alive in the one corner that mattered: a rack of inference hardware behind a glass-walled server closet, its status lights blinking their ordinary green, fed by a building generator he could hear cycling somewhere below the floor. He stood in the doorway of that closet for a second just breathing it in, the low mechanical hum of it, before he let himself feel anything like hope.

He ate before he touched anything else. Some part of him had learned that lesson already today, watching himself get too deep into a problem to notice his own hands shaking from low blood sugar, and he wasn't going to make that mistake twice. The office kitchen still had what offices always had — a cabinet of granola bars nobody had gotten to yet, a case of water under the sink, a jar of peanut butter he ate three spoonfuls of straight, standing at the counter with Dan's food bowl already down on the floor beside him. It wasn't a meal. It was fuel, and he treated it exactly that plainly, and felt steadier for it within twenty minutes in a way that embarrassed him a little, how simple the fix had turned out to be.

The building's own internet was dead — whatever fiber ran into Midtown had gone the way of everything else sometime in the last few hours — but his phone still showed two bars, thin and flickering, enough to tether if he was patient with it. He plugged the four SSDs into the office rack, watched the model come back up on hardware that could actually run it properly instead of laptop scraps, and sat there for a moment with a tethered connection he already knew wasn't going to last. Cell towers ran on backup power same as everything else, batteries with a number of hours on them and nobody left to swap them out before they hit zero. Whatever window this was, it was a window, not a door. He wasn't going to waste it deciding what to do with it.

That decision came to him almost embarrassingly late, sitting there with a live connection and no immediate plan for it — a memory surfacing out of the noise of the day, small and specific: his own call, that morning, phone pressed to his ear on the shoulder of a dead road, a calm synthetic voice running through a script about call volume and estimated response times. He'd assumed, in the moment, that it meant nothing — a dispatch system on autopilot, a dead end. But if that system had answered him, unprompted, automatically, on a morning when every human dispatcher in the building had to be dead along with everyone else — then it hadn't answered him specifically. It answered everyone. Every single person in the country desperate enough to call 911 that morning had gotten the same calm, useless, endlessly patient voice, and somewhere behind that voice, on some server nobody was watching anymore, every one of those calls was still sitting in a log.

He sat up straighter without deciding to. *Maybe I wasn't the only one who tried.*

The work that followed ate the rest of the night and most of the next day, and it looked nothing like the clean, purposeful search that had found Sam. Emergency dispatch ran on a patchwork of vendors and county-by-county systems, decades of underfunded infrastructure duct-taped together long before anyone had built an AI layer on top of it, and getting into any of it meant treating each jurisdiction as its own separate, ugly little problem. He stopped trying to do it himself somewhere around midnight and started directing the model to do it instead — not one instance working one thread, but a dozen running in parallel, each one hammering at a different state's dispatch infrastructure at once, cross-referencing whatever it could reach against the same filter John and Catherine had built that very first morning: not who called, but who pushed back. Who argued with the reassurance. Who refused to accept the script and hung up angry, scared, alive.

Most of what came back was noise. Systems that had never gotten the AI overlay at all and just held silent logs of unanswered rings. Systems that had it but had gone dark themselves sometime in the last twenty hours, taking their data with them. Call after call that turned out, on closer inspection, to be exactly what the transcript made it look like — someone panicking about a car accident, a break-in, a heart attack, gone quiet mid-call the same way everyone went quiet, nothing left behind but an ordinary tragedy indistinguishable from a hundred thousand others that morning. He fell asleep sitting upright twice, jerked awake both times by Dan's cold nose against his hand, and kept going anyway, because stopping felt like the one thing he actually couldn't afford.

By the gray edge of Wednesday morning, out of what the model estimated — with real caveats, real uncertainty — as somewhere over four thousand calls that fit the initial pattern nationwide, he had three that held up under everything he could throw at them. A repeat caller in Huntsville, Alabama, arguing with the triage voice for nearly six minutes before the line went dead mid-sentence. A woman in Phoenix, Arizona, who'd called back four separate times over two hours, each call shorter and more desperate than the last. And a caller in Portland, Oregon, a single call, oddly calm, giving coordinates instead of an address — *coordinates*, which struck him immediately as the detail of someone who knew exactly where she was and precisely how much trouble that was. He didn't know any of their names, not really — a name attached to an account on file wasn't the same thing as knowing a person, and he was careful with himself not to pretend otherwise. All he actually had was three addresses and three callback numbers that, as far as the model could tell before its own connection started failing, still belonged to active lines.

He was double-checking the third number against the model's confidence score, still not quite ready to trust his own hands with the dial, when the tether dropped out from under him without any warning at all — not the thin, flickering two bars he'd been nursing all night, just gone, one bar to none between one glance at the screen and the next. He tried toggling it back a dozen ways he already knew wouldn't work. It didn't come back. Whatever window Atlanta's cell network had been running on, it had just run out, right on schedule, exactly the way he'd known it eventually would and hoped it wouldn't, not yet, not before he'd made a single call.

The office phones were no better — VoIP handsets that needed the same dead internet to do anything at all, decorative now, plastic and glass with nothing behind them. He stood in the empty kitchen for a full minute doing the kind of rapid, ugly math he'd gotten good at over the last day, and landed on the only answer that made any sense: he needed a connection that didn't care whether Atlanta had a network left to offer it. He needed something that talked to a satellite instead.

He also made himself admit, standing there, that he wasn't coming back here. Whatever this office had given him — power, a rack, one good night's head start — it had given it to him already, and the next time this building's generator ran dry there'd be nobody left to notice or care. So he stopped thinking about the problem in front of him for five minutes and packed like a man who meant that. Three more laptops off three dead colleagues' desks, the beefiest ones he could find by feel, docking stations and every USB-C brick and cable in the supply closet, a soft-sided case usually reserved for demo equipment that fit two of the laptops and half the SSDs with room to spare. It was the mobile kit he'd originally come here to build, just assembled in more of a hurry and with a great deal less certainty than he'd planned on. He didn't know yet whether any of it would run the model half as well as the office rack had. He knew he'd rather have it and not need it.

REI was the idea that actually got him moving — the store out at Atlantic Station, close enough to be barely a detour on the way to PDK rather than a real one. He'd bought a tent there once, years ago, and remembered enough of the layout to picture exactly where the satellite gear lived: a little display alcove near the checkout, handheld communicators for backcountry hikers, and — newer than he expected, restocked sometime in the last year — a compact satellite hotspot unit, the kind meant to give a campsite a thin shared signal for a weekend. It wasn't a phone. It didn't need to be. If he could get it talking to a satellite at all, he had a laptop and a voice-calling app that could do the rest.

The store was locked and dark, exactly as empty as everywhere else, and the display unit came off its mount easier than the glass case protecting it. Getting it working was the actual problem. The hotspot wanted an account — a real one, tied to a data plan, activated the ordinary way through an app that needed the very internet it was supposed to provide, the exact kind of closed loop the whole last two days had been full of. He sat on the floor of the store with the hotspot, a fresh laptop, and the model, and worked the problem the way he'd worked every other problem since yesterday morning: not elegantly, just thoroughly. Retail demo units almost never shipped blank. Somewhere in the device's own firmware, provisioned by the manufacturer specifically so a bored customer could test it on a showroom floor, there was usually a dormant service credential nobody had bothered to revoke. It took the model most of an hour of patient, methodical probing — the same unglamorous, faintly illegal instinct that had cracked open a crew-scheduling leak two nights ago — to find it, wake it, and talk the unit into believing it was still sitting in a demo case with a corporate account behind it. A status light went from red to a slow, hopeful amber, and then, twenty minutes later, to green.

It was slow. It was going to be slow for as long as it lasted. It was also, as far as he could tell sitting on the floor of an empty outdoor store with his laptop balanced on his knees, an actual working connection to the actual rest of the world, and he didn't waste it feeling amazed for longer than a few seconds before he opened a calling app he hadn't touched in years and dialed Huntsville first.

Nothing answered. Not a ring, not a busy signal, not even the dead-air hum of an open line going nowhere — just a flat failure notice, the kind of thing a call said when there was no tower left anywhere on the other end for it to land on. Phoenix gave him the exact same failure, word for word, and he sat with that for a second, because it told him something he hadn't fully let himself consider yet: this wasn't nobody answering. This was nobody's phone having anywhere left to answer *from*. Two whole regions had gone the way of his own, sometime in the last day, taking whoever was on the other end of those numbers further out of reach than a simple unanswered call ever could.

He made himself dial the third number anyway, because two dead grids didn't tell him anything about a third — and some old, half-formed memory of a map surfaced right as he did it, something about the Pacific Northwest running on hydro the way Newfoundland did, dams that didn't need anyone minding them to keep turning. If that was true, Portland's lights might still be on. Portland's *towers* might still be on. It was worth one more try before he let himself believe the whole country was going dark at the same rate his own corner of it had.

It picked up on the second ring.

"H-hello?" A woman's voice, young, strained, careful in a way that told him pain before it told him anything else.

"My name's John," he said, fast, before she could hang up on a stranger's voice out of pure exhaustion. "I'm not with the dispatch line — I'm a person. I'm real. I found your number through the 911 system, but I'm not police, I'm not anyone official, I'm just another person who's still here."

The silence on the other end went on just long enough that he thought he'd lost her. Then, quieter, wrecked at the edges: "Oh God. Oh, thank God. Somebody's — you're actually—" She stopped, breathed, started again. "My name's Grace. I'm in Portland. I'm hurt — my leg, I think it's broken, I can't really walk on it, I've been out here since yesterday and I didn't think —" Her voice caught hard on that last word and held there.

"I hear you," John said, and meant it with his whole chest. "You're not alone anymore. Tell me exactly where you are."

She did, coordinates and a rough description of a coastal overlook off a road whose name she wasn't fully sure of, her voice shaking less the longer she talked, like just being heard was doing some of the work pain medication should have been doing instead. John wrote every word of it down on the same notepad that still had Sam's numbers crossed out on it, felt something in his chest that he didn't have a clean name for — not quite hope, not quite relief, something closer to the specific, staggering weight of a stranger's life suddenly, undeniably his responsibility.

"I don't have a way to get to you myself, not today," he told her honestly, because he'd promised Sam the truth and he wasn't going to start lying to people now. "But I'm not the only one out here anymore either. I have someone I'm about to meet who can help me figure out how. I will call this number again. I promise you that."

"Okay," Grace said, small and steady, like a woman deciding to believe him because the alternative was worse. "Okay. I'll be here. I'm not exactly going anywhere."

He held onto that sentence longer than he probably should have after the call ended, some fragile, terrible attempt at humor from a woman with a broken leg alone on a cliff, and it did more to keep him upright through the rest of that afternoon than anything else had.

But it was the two silences, not the one voice, that he couldn't put down sitting there on the store's floor afterward. A ring with no answer was a stranger not picking up. A dead network was a whole region gone quiet at once — towers down, or power down, or both, the same slow collapse he'd watched eat his own house right on schedule now apparently well underway in Alabama and Arizona both. Somewhere in Huntsville and somewhere in Phoenix, two people who'd been desperate enough to argue with a machine less than a day ago were now sitting in places nobody could reach by any ordinary means at all — not by phone, maybe not for a while by anything. That wasn't nothing. If anything it was worse than nothing, and it changed the shape of what came next from a phone call into something that was going to require an actual airplane and an actual road, sooner rather than later.

He packed the hotspot in with everything else — the laptops, the SSDs, the cables and cases and connectors he'd stripped out of Midtown on his way out the door — three addresses and one very real, very injured woman now sitting in his notebook alongside a dead man's crew list from a day and a half ago. The four o'clock deadline still had daylight in front of it. He gathered Dan and the whole improvised mobile kit that used to be a home office and then an actual office and was now just whatever fit in his car, and pointed north toward PDK, faster than the speed limit strictly allowed, because for the first time since yesterday morning he had somewhere he actually needed to be.
