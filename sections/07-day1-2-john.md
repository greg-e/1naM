# 7 — John, Day 1-2

He saw it come in a long way out — a speck low over the trees off the approach end, resolving as it got closer into a single high-wing airplane that settled onto the numbers, rolled most of the runway out, and turned off toward him. John stood by the car with his hand on Dan's collar and watched it come up the taxiway the way you watch something you've spent two days trying to reach finally show up in front of you. A PC-12, a tail number he didn't know and didn't need to. Nobody else on earth was going to land a Pilatus at this specific airport at this specific hour.

It swung around and stopped a respectful distance off, the propeller wound down, and the door came open and folded down into its own steps, and a man climbed out into the last of the light. He looked worse than John had pictured — hollowed around the eyes, three days of stubble, a borrowed fleece a size large on him — and also exactly like the voice from the phone. Dan got to him first, tail going, entirely unbothered that this was a stranger, and Sam went down on one knee on the taxiway and put both hands into the dog's fur for a long moment before he stood back up and looked at John properly.

Neither of them said anything for a second. There didn't seem to be a version of *hello* that fit two men who had already said the worst things they'd ever have to say to each other over a dying phone line.

"John," Sam said finally, like he was confirming it to himself.

"Sam." He put his hand out, and Sam looked at it for half a second and then ignored it and pulled him into something closer to a hug, hard and brief and over before John's arms had caught up. Neither of them mentioned it. There wasn't a version of the day that had room for being awkward about it.

"You made it," John said.

"You went to the house." Not quite a question. Sam's jaw worked. "Thank you. For that. For all of it."

"I'd do it again." John meant it, and let the silence sit there a moment before he moved them both off it. "You eaten anything real today?"

Sam huffed something that wasn't a laugh. "Define real."

There was a restaurant on the field — a low sandbagged building done up like a forward airbase from a war eighty years gone, revetment walls and a Jeep out front, dark now like everything else. The door was unlocked. Inside, the kitchen's walk-in was still cold, still running off whatever kept the terminal's emergency lights on, and John found steaks in it and a crate of vegetables that hadn't turned, and a range that lit off a pilot flame when he tried it. He cooked while Sam sat on a stool at the pass and talked, and then didn't talk, and then talked again. They ate at a table by a window that looked out on the ramp and the airplane and the empty sky over it, and they went back over the two days — not the hard parts, they'd done those on the phone, but the shape of it, the how, the what-comes-next. By the time the plates were empty it was full dark and neither of them had said the word *tired* out loud, but it was in the room.

"I've got work to do tonight," John said. "The thing in the back of my car. It's how this all started — it's how I found you. I want to point it at finding other people."

Sam nodded slowly. "You think there are others."

"I know there's at least a chance. I've had a piece of it running since this afternoon." He turned his coffee cup a quarter turn on the table. "You should sleep. There's nothing you can do on this part, and one of us being sharp tomorrow is worth more than both of us being half-gone."

Sam didn't argue it, which told John more about how far down he was than anything he'd said. There were crew rooms off the back of the terminal with couches in them. Sam took one; John set up in the restaurant, at the table by the window, with the workstation humming on a chair beside him off the storm battery and a cable run to an outlet that still had life in it, and the dog asleep across his feet.

The work looked nothing like the clean line that had found Sam. Emergency dispatch in this country was a patchwork — hundreds of county and regional systems, decades of underfunded infrastructure held together with tape long before anyone bolted an AI layer onto some fraction of it — and reaching into any of it meant treating every jurisdiction as its own separate, ugly little problem. He'd stopped doing it by hand that afternoon and turned the model loose: not one instance on one thread but a dozen in parallel, each working a different state's dispatch infrastructure at once, filtering everything they could reach the way he'd have filtered it himself. Not who called. Who pushed back. Who argued with the reassurance, who refused the script, who hung up angry and scared and *alive*.

Most of it was noise. Systems that had never gotten the AI layer and held nothing but silent logs of rings nobody answered. Systems that had it and had gone dark themselves in the last two days, taking their data down with them. Call after call that turned out to be exactly what it looked like — someone panicking about a wreck, a break-in, a chest pain, going quiet mid-sentence the same way the whole world had gone quiet, an ordinary emergency indistinguishable from a hundred thousand others. He fell asleep at the table twice and woke both times to Dan's nose against his hand, and kept going, because the connection he was doing it over was a little worse every hour and he could not make himself stop while it still worked at all.

By the gray edge of Thursday morning, out of what the model put — with heavy caveats — at somewhere over four thousand calls nationwide that fit the pattern, he had three that survived everything he threw at them.

A repeat caller in Huntsville, Alabama, who had argued with the triage voice for almost six minutes straight before the line cut out mid-word. A woman in Phoenix, Arizona, who had called back four separate times across two hours, each call shorter and more desperate than the one before. And a single call from Portland, Oregon — oddly calm, giving map coordinates instead of a street address. *Coordinates.* Someone who knew exactly where she was and exactly how much trouble that was.

He didn't have names. A name on an account was not the same thing as knowing a person, and he made himself keep that straight. What he had was three addresses and three mobile numbers that, as far as the model could tell before its own reach started to fray, still belonged to live accounts.

He dialed Huntsville first, on a connection his own instruments told him was down to its last few hours, maybe less.

Nothing. Not a ring, not a busy tone, not even the dead-air hum of an open line going nowhere — a flat failure notice, the sound a call makes when there is no tower left anywhere on the far end for it to reach. Phoenix gave him the identical failure, word for word.

He dialed Portland with the map he'd been half-building all night at the front of his mind — the Pacific Northwest running mostly on hydro, big dams that kept turning whether or not anyone was there to mind them, the same class of machine that had kept a corner of Atlantic Canada lit long enough for Sam to fly an airplane out of it. A grid that might outlast the others by days. If any of the three numbers was going to carry his voice, it was that one.

It gave him the same flat failure as the other two.

He set the phone down and sat with it a while, because it was telling him the thing he'd spent the whole night working not to hear. Nobody answering was a stranger asleep, or a stranger who'd learned not to trust an unknown number, or a stranger already dead in a room with the phone ringing beside them. This was worse and simpler: nobody's phone had anywhere left to answer *from*. Three regions, three calls, and not one of them still had a network standing to carry him into it. The collapse he'd watched crawl across his instruments had already been to every one of those places and moved on.

So he did not, in the end, get to say any of it — *you're not the only one, I'm real, I'm coming.* What he had instead was a page with three addresses on it and three dead numbers, and under each one a few lines of transcript. A man in Huntsville who had argued with the machine for six minutes. A woman in Phoenix who had called back and back and back. Someone in Portland who had stayed calm enough to read out map coordinates, slowly, like she was talking to a search team that was actually coming. Three people who had been alive and fighting less than two days ago, three specific points on a map, and no way on earth to know whether any of them still was.

It changed the shape of everything that came after. This had never really been a phone call — that was a hope he'd carried because carrying it was easier than the alternative. It was three points on a map, hundreds and then thousands of miles apart, that somebody was going to have to physically go and stand on before anyone learned anything at all. And there was no clock he could read on it. Any of the three might last a week, or might have been gone before he ever found the call, and the only way to know was to fly there and see, one at a time, however long that took.

He wasn't going to be home for a while. He understood that now the way you understand weather coming in.

The light came up gray and then gold through the restaurant window, and out on the ramp the airplane sat waiting, and somewhere behind the terminal Sam was asleep on a crew-room couch not knowing yet that the day had three destinations in it. John made a fresh pot of coffee in the dead restaurant's kitchen, set a cup by the couch without waking him, and stood at the window with his own, watching the field come up out of the dark.
