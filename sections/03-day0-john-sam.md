# 3 — John, Day 0

Forty-five minutes after pulling out of his own driveway, one thing had stayed in his head the whole way down: a green icon on a phone screen, still moving, still real, getting closer to the ground with every mile he covered.

Hartsfield-Jackson from the south side looked like nothing he'd ever seen it look like — no queue of cars feeding the terminal curb, no shuttle buses, no tower of jets stacked on approach. He came in past the passenger terminals without slowing, working off a half-formed memory of a business trip years back, and found the general aviation side by the signs alone: SIGNATURE FLIGHT SUPPORT, a low glass building with its own gated ramp, sitting dark and still like everything else had been all morning.

The gate arm was down and the little card reader beside it as dead as every other screen he'd seen since six a.m. He didn't waste time on it — pulled onto the grass shoulder, got out, and simply lifted the arm by hand until it gave at the hinge, then drove through onto the empty ramp and parked facing the runways.

The glass doors were unlocked, and he went through the lobby — leather chairs, a coffee station gone cold, a wall of framed photographs of airplanes — back toward the ops counter, where line crews would have coordinated fuel trucks and inbound traffic on any ordinary morning. The radio was there, a squat gray transceiver bolted into the counter, a charge light glowing steady. He'd talked on a radio just like it plenty of times from the other end, calling in from the ramp at PDK on an ordinary Saturday, and his hand found the controls without much trouble. He worked through the frequencies the way he'd have worked them from his own cockpit — Atlanta Approach first, out of habit, nothing but a dead carrier hum. Atlanta Center, the same. Then Guard, 121.5, the emergency channel.

Five hundred miles north, on the turn toward a city he could already see sitting dead and silent under the morning haze, Sam keyed the same frequency the way he had every twenty minutes since Hudson Bay, the words worn down to almost nothing by now. "Anybody — this is Delta Two-Seven, is there anybody on this frequency." He didn't expect anything back. He hadn't heard anything back in six hours of asking.

John's thumb found the transmit button before his brain had finished deciding to use it. "Delta Two-Seven, this is — " He didn't have a callsign. "This is John Lauer. I'm on the ground in Atlanta. I can hear you."

Sam's hand came off the yoke before he caught it. For a second his own throat wouldn't work at all. A voice, thin and startled and entirely human. "Say again," he finally got out, rough, urgent in a way he hadn't sounded once in six hours of talking to dead air. "Say again — who is this?"

Nothing came back on John's end for a beat too long, long enough that his stomach dropped, wondering if he'd imagined the whole thing standing alone on an empty lobby. Then he keyed it again, steadier the second time, working just as hard to hold himself together as the voice on the other end clearly was. John Lauer. Alpharetta. He'd watched the track turn around on a map that morning and driven straight to the airport on nothing but the hope of being there when it landed. He was parked on the general aviation side, south field — Signature.

"Signature." Sam said it back and felt something in his chest that had been clenched for six hours ease off half an inch. He knew that ramp — two years of charter work before Delta, in and out of that exact FBO more times than he could count, a life that felt like it belonged to somebody else now. "I have a plane full of dead people, and you are the first to respond to my radio calls, have emergency services meet the plane when I land."

John paused. "There's no emergency services," he said, as plain as he knew how to make it. "No fire crew, no ambulance, nobody to call. It's me. Just me and a dog."

The channel held quiet a second, nothing but even static, the connection itself not going anywhere. Then: "Copy that." Flatter than either of them expected, some old training taking the words and filing them away before the rest of him could react to them properly. "I'll see you on the ground." He didn't ask anything else. 

He set up for Runway 26R the way he could have blind, home base, the one airport on earth he didn't need a chart for. No tower answered his calls. No ground vehicles crawled the ramps. He flew it as a plain visual approach, gear down, flaps full, hand-flying the last thousand feet himself.

John didn't have to wait long. It came out of the haze low and steady, gear down, flaps out, exactly the picture of an ordinary arrival except for the ordinary part — no other traffic anywhere near it, nothing but one enormous airplane descending alone into a silent airport like it was the last one left. It touched down soft on 26R and rolled out, smooth, the reversers spooling down to a walk near the runway Sam had departed off less than twelve hours before as a completely different man, then turned off the runway and back onto the taxiway toward the Signature FBO.

The jet came to a stop on the FBO ramp with a long exhale of hydraulics, engines spinning down through their whine into silence. John standing outside the Signature FBO doors facing the ramp looking up at the flightdeck of the Airbus 350, both with the relief of knowing they are not alone.

Then, near the nose, a door came open, and a slide dropped and unspooled down onto the concrete with a hiss and a thump that John felt in his chest before he understood what he was looking at. A man came out onto it — a pilot's white shirt, the four gold stripes on his shoulder boards catching the morning sun — and rode it down hard, the way you'd ride something you'd never trained to ride sitting alone.

He came off the bottom of it already looking for John rather than for anything else — no reason left to look for anything else, not after the radio had already told him what was and wasn't coming — and found him thirty yards off next to the FBO, onae dog already covering the last hundred feet at a dead run.

John was already moving too, and Dan got there first — pressing himself against the pilot's legs the way he pressed himself against anyone in the family who needed it, before either man had said a word. It broke something loose in both of them at once. The pilot went down on one knee into the dog's fur, one hand splayed flat against Dan's side, and John closed the last of the distance and just stood there a second, looking at a face of another living person.

"John." Sam said holding out his hand.

"And you are?" John said.

Sam laughed — a short, broken sound that wasn't really a laugh — "Sorry, I am Samuel Reyes, call me Sam. Everybody on that airplane is dead. Three hundred and — " Chocking, He stopped there and didn't try to finish it, and John didn't make him.

The next half hour the stood on the ramp beside the giant airplane. Sam gave him the outline — Theresa, Jack, the cabin, the turn north of Hudson Bay, why he'd flown for six hours straight back to Atlanta instead of the nearest patch of gravel in Canada. John gave his own outline back — the thud at 2:05, the man in the ditch, Harry, the frozen feeds, the empty sky narrowing itself down all morning to one doing something different that turned out to be this. Somewhere in it Sam said the name Elena, and then Maya, and the direction his whole face went when he said them told John everything he needed to know about what came next.

"Marietta," Sam said. "Thirty minutes from here. I need — " His voice caught and he pushed through it. "I need to go."

"I'll drive," John said. "Let's go."

They left the airplane sitting where it had rolled to a stop and took John's car north and west into Cobb County a little past eleven thirty. Sam sat rigid in the passenger seat the whole way, watching a city he knew by heart go by in the same wrong stillness John had already spent a morning learning to expect — the cars stopped at their odd angles, the intersections holding a red light for no one, a strip mall with its lot empty and not one person crossing it. He didn't talk much. Twice he tried Elena's number and got the same flat ringing John recognized from his own morning of calls that never resolved into anything.

The house, looked like every other house John had already stood in front of that day — ordinary, undisturbed, a minivan in the driveway parked the way you park it when you expect to need it again in the morning. Sam was out of the car before John had it fully stopped.

He went in through the front door at a near run, calling their names into a house that gave nothing back, and John came up the walk slower, Dan at his heel, and stopped at the threshold. Some instinct told him this wasn't his to walk into first. He stood in the open doorway, close enough to hear, and let Sam go find it alone the way John himself had had to find Harry alone that morning — because there were some rooms a person had to walk into by themselves, whatever waited on the other side of the door.

It didn't take long. A silence from the back of the house that ran on a beat too long, and then a sound John had not heard a grown man make before — not a cry exactly, something lower and more ruined than that, coming from the direction of a bedroom down the hall. John found himself moving without deciding to.

Elena was in their bed, on her side, one arm still reaching toward the nightstand. No mark on her, nothing disturbed, exactly the gentle, terrible peace John had already seen enough times that day to recognize instantly and would never get used to recognizing. Down the hall, through a door standing half open, Maya — twelve, thirteen, a room with fairy lights strung along one wall and a half-finished school project on the desk — lay curled the way kids sleep, like nothing in the whole world had asked anything of her.

Sam was on his knees at the foot of Elena's bed when John reached the doorway, both hands pressed flat against the mattress like he could hold something down by will alone, and John stopped there too, the same way he'd stopped in the hall outside. He didn't go to him. He didn't say the things people say, because there weren't any that meant anything, and some old, right instinct told him the kindest thing he had to offer a man in this particular minute was simply not to be somewhere else. So he stood in the doorway of a room he had no place being inside of, and let a stranger he'd known for forty-five minutes fall apart in front of him, and stayed.

It started to ease — not pass, John understood already that grief like this didn't pass, only eased its grip for a while — Sam got slowly to his feet, and with a care that undid John more than the crying had, straightened the blanket over Elena's shoulder, the same small useless gesture John had made over Harry's that same morning without either of them knowing the other had done it. He went to Maya's room next and did something similar, and came back out into the hall a different man than the one who'd gone in — hollowed, steadier in some way that had nothing to do with feeling better.

"Come back to my place, we can figure out what's next," John said.

Sam looked at him a long moment, red-eyed, and something in his face that might have been the first real decision he'd made since the flight deck went quiet. "Okay," he said. "Yeah. Okay, I'll grab some of my things."

They pulled out of the driveway a few minutes after one, Sam quiet in the passenger seat with his forehead against the window, Dan's chin resting on the console between them the whole way like he understood exactly how much weight was riding in that car and had appointed himself to carry a little of it. The drive back to Alpharetta took the better part of forty minutes, through the same stopped, silent county, and neither of them said much of anything until John pulled into his own driveway at twenty minutes to two, cut the engine, and sat there a second before either of them moved.

"This is it," John said. "Come on. Let's see if we can find out how big this thing actually is."
