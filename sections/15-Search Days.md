# Search Days

### 9/18/2028, 09:20 local Pacific Time - John

The envelope outside the hospital was empty. John put the end of his pencil into each bottom corner to make sure no folded reply had caught there, then wrote the date on the notice. Sam held a second piece of tape across the top while he pressed it onto the glass.

At the airport they fixed the other notice beside the terminal door. John added *Thursday* and the date below the request for replies. Through the glass he could see the couches where they had first put their bags down, and beyond them the runway, with a gull standing on the centerline.

Sam brought the key board out of the office and set it on a couch. Some hooks were empty. He matched the numbers on the remaining tags to the hangars and wrote them on the back of the paper John had used for the notice.

They opened three before they found the other 182. It was white with a faded blue stripe and had an oil-stained towbar lying under the nose. A box of cleaning rags stood beside the door, with a dead mouse in the bottom. Nobody was inside the hangar.

John went under the wing and looked in. It had a G1000, like his, and cloth seats with the stuffing coming out at the edge of the pilot's cushion. He could read the fuel selector from outside. He put his hand on the door handle and stayed there.

"Yours was blue," Sam said.

"Green."

"I remember blue."

"You were always looking at the back of it."

Sam ducked under the wing beside him. There was a folded checklist in the pocket and a headset on the other seat. John moved the headset onto his lap and sat in the left seat without touching the master switch. The rudder pedals were too far forward. He moved the seat and tested that it had latched, then put his feet on them.

His own hangar key was on the ring with his house key, in his pocket.

Sam walked around the tail and came back to the door. "Books?"

John got out and opened the baggage compartment. The handbook was there, in a plastic wallet, but the maintenance records weren't. They found those in a steel cabinet against the hangar wall, the airframe number written along the spine of the binder. John carried them into the sunlight.

An annual inspection had been signed that summer. There were later entries for work on the brakes and an oil change. He took the book to the airplane and checked the registration and serial number against it, then held the page open while Sam read.

"It flew after this," Sam said. He pointed to the difference between the recorded hours and the meter in the panel. "We still need to look at it."

They worked through the handbook's inspection with the hangar door open. There was an old fuel stain beneath one drain. Sam wiped it clean, and they checked it again before they left. John kept the samples in a clear jar until the sediment had settled and held them against the white door. He didn't like what he saw in the first one and took another.

They left the airplane in the hangar that day. John took the keys and the books to the truck, wrote the meter reading on a sheet of paper, and went back to put a copy in the pocket beside the checklist.

The airport notice was still empty when they drove past it.

### 9/21/2028, 10:35 local Pacific Time

Sam had flown the Cessna around the field the previous afternoon and brought it back to the same hangar. He had come back to the ship with a page of notes and a complaint about the seat, and John had read the notes first. They had put the papers in a binder that went with the airplane, not in the wardroom's growing stack.

Now John sat in the right seat with a camera in his lap and the road map folded to Sequim. Sam had the airplane. They had checked the runway on foot before starting and taxied past their notice on the terminal glass.

Catherine had their route and their return time. Johan had checked the handheld Sam was carrying against the ship's radio while they were still on the ground. Tyler was staying with him and Della.

Past Port Angeles, John kept his eyes on the side roads leading inland. A driveway ended under the trees, and he leaned forward to see around the window post. By the time he found the roof, they had passed it. He turned to look back, with the camera caught between his stomach and the belt.

"Let that one go," Sam said.

"I didn't see the yard."

"Write that down."

He wrote it down. At the next open patch he remembered to look before bringing the camera up. There was a blue tarp over a boat, a white tank beside a shed, and a row of empty lawn chairs. He took a picture with the road in it, then a wider one so they could find the place again.

He had been looking for people, a hand moving over a head, somebody running. Now he tried to remember where the objects were.

Sam asked whether he wanted another look at a field off the road. John had seen pale material laid out near a gate. They came back over it from the other direction. The material lifted at one end, dropped, and lifted again. Through the camera John could see a torn edge caught on a fencepost.

"It's a sheet or a feed sack," he said. "Caught on the wire."

"Got the location?"

John marked it. Sam kept the wings level while he took the photograph.

They turned before the route on Catherine's paper ended. John had wanted to keep going toward the next cluster of houses, but the ones behind them were already hard to place without the map. He drew a line where they had turned and wrote the time beside it.

On the way back he looked out the other side. Some of the roofs were familiar now. A little farther west he could see the harbor and the Hook, with the Pilatus parked by the hangar. Sam made a call on the handheld and Catherine answered. She had heard them earlier, then lost them for a while.

"We're coming back," Sam told her.

John took a picture of the spit through the windshield, then put the camera away for the landing.

At the ship, Tyler wanted to see every photograph. He sat at the laptop while John copied them across, and stopped him at the blue tarp.

"Was somebody there?"

"We didn't see anybody."

Tyler enlarged it until the tarp broke up into squares. John took the mouse and brought it back down.

"We need the wide one, too. So we can find it."

They put the photographs in a folder with the date. John printed the route and marked the stretch where the trees had hidden most of the houses. He put no cross through it.

Johan came in with the radio and handed it back. "You were early."

"We turned around," John said.

"Good. Catherine had the clock."

She was at the galley door, eating a piece of bread with cheese folded into it. "I had two clocks. Yours and the one he kept asking about."

Tyler looked up from the photographs. "I asked twice."

"Three times," Johan said.

Catherine handed the boy the rest of the bread and went back to the sick bay.

### 9/27/2028, 19:10 local Pacific Time

John had the radio's manual held open under one elbow and a pencil behind his ear. Johan was outside the operations building with the antenna, and its cable came through the doorway to the table they had brought over from the station. They had started there rather than try to work the satellite set from inside the cutter's steel bulkheads.

The frequency sheet was in a clear sleeve beside the set. John had checked the receive entry, then the transmit entry, against what the radio displayed. He checked them again while Johan took his jacket off the back of the chair.

Arthur's tracking program ran without the internet. Its saved orbital data had dates from before the event. John had kept them separate from the orbit he had asked the model to fit to Tyler's notebook, and the two predictions didn't agree exactly. The earlier sighting from the ship gave him one observation to check them against, not a reason to trust either list for the rest of September.

He had copied both predicted windows onto the page in front of him. Johan went outside early enough for the earlier one.

Tyler had come over after supper. He sat by the door with his grandmother's Bible on his knees, one finger keeping his place while he watched John and the radio. The sky had clouded over, and there would be no sighting to put beside this attempt.

Johan brought the antenna around, stopped and adjusted it again. John listened before making a call, then released the key and listened. The speaker gave him noise. He watched the clock, wrote the time, and tried again.

During the next listening interval he could hear Johan moving his feet on the pavement outside. He had turned the antenna in his hands, then tilted it back. John stayed at the set until both predicted windows had ended. Nothing he could identify had answered.

Johan came in with the cable over his forearm. "What did you hear?"

"Noise."

Johan set the cable down beside the manual.

John put the pencil down. Tyler still had his finger in the Bible, but his eyes were on the radio.

"Does that mean we missed it?"

"We don't know," John said. "We might have."

"Or they can't hear us."

"That's possible, too."

Tyler shut the book with his finger still inside it, then pulled his hand out. "I'm going back."

"I'll walk with you," John said.

"I know the way."

John got up and took his jacket off the chair. The boy waited while he put it on. Outside, the lights along the road were on. They walked back to the pier without talking, and John held the brow's gate while Tyler went through.

When he returned, Johan had laid the antenna across two chairs and was writing in the notebook.

"Don't put zero," John said.

Johan looked up.

"For people. Or a signal. We don't know if the receiver was looking in the right place."

He turned the notebook so John could read it. There were the times, the two frequencies, the directions he had tried, and *No reply heard*.

"I have written what happened," he said.

John read it again. "Yes. Sorry."

He pulled his chair in and added which prediction lists they had used.

### 9/30/2028, 11:14 local Pacific Time

The two prediction lists disagreed by forty seconds on this one, and both put it high, fifty-eight degrees out of the southwest. It was the first daytime pass they had tried. John had worked out on Thursday that every pass they'd sat up for came over the Hook in the middle of the station's night, Greenwich time, when anyone aboard would be asleep. This one came over at a quarter past six in the evening on the station's clock.

He had the set on the table in the operations building with the frequency sheet in its sleeve beside it, the receive and transmit entries checked against the display twice. Johan was outside on the pavement with the antenna, its cable coming in through the door. Sam stood behind John's chair. Tyler sat on the floor by the door with his back to the wall and his grandmother's Bible on his knees, where he could see the radio and Johan both.

At 11:16 the speaker gave them noise. At 11:17 the noise changed.

"Hold," John said, and Johan held.

"...station, any station, this is NA1SS, the International Space Station." A woman's voice, flat and clear, under a little hiss. "Seven aboard, all well. We are listening on one four four decimal four niner zero. Any station, over."

Tyler was on his feet. He didn't come any closer to the table.

"That's Commander Pruitt," he said.

John keyed the microphone. "NA1SS, NA1SS, this is Ediz Hook, Port Angeles, Washington. We read you. Six alive at this station. Over."

He let go of the key. The hiss came back. Nobody in the room moved.

"Any station, any station, this is NA1SS," the woman said. "Seven aboard, all well. Listening on one four four decimal four niner zero. Over."

"She didn't hear you," Sam said.

John checked the display. Transmit on 144.490. Receive on 145.800. He called again, slower, and gave the position. Outside, Johan brought the antenna around a few degrees and held it. John called a third time and a fourth. Each time the woman's voice came back with the same words, as if nobody had spoken, and the fourth time it broke up halfway through and went to noise.

At 11:25 the noise went flat. John looked at the clock and wrote the time.

Johan came in with the cable over his arm and stood in the doorway. "You heard," he said.

"We heard her. She didn't hear us."

Johan sat down with the notebook. John watched him write it: the time, the frequencies, *NA1SS heard, voice, female, calling any station. 7 aboard well. Answered 4 times on 144.490. No reply heard.* There was nothing to correct.

Tyler hadn't sat back down. He had both arms around the Bible.

"Seven," he said. "That's everybody."

"That's what she said," John said.

"That's everybody," Tyler said again, and went out past Johan and down the stairs. Sam looked at John and went after him.

### 21:40 local Pacific Time

Sam came to the wardroom door in his socks. "He's on the flight deck," he said. "He asked for you."

The flight deck lights were off. Tyler was at the starboard rail in the foul-weather jacket that came to his knees, with Dan sitting against his leg and the Bible held shut against his chest. The town was dark along the foot of the mountains. Out over the Strait the sky was clear and full of stars. John came and stood beside him at the rail.

"Is it going over?" he asked.

"Not one you can see. It went over at ten. Low." Tyler kept looking at the sky. "Grandma prayed every night. Out loud, at the kitchen table, after supper. She did Mom every night, by name, since March."

John waited.

"Does God hear people?" Tyler said. "When the radio doesn't."

John put both hands on the rail. The steel was cold and wet.

"My wife would have told you yes," he said. "Naomi. She'd have told you that and meant it, and she'd have been sure." He didn't know how to say the rest to a ten-year-old, so he said it plain. "I went to church with her for twenty years. I thought I believed what she believed. When she died I found out it was hers. I'd been borrowing it." He looked at the boy. "So I don't know, Tyler. I'm sorry. I don't."

Tyler thought about that for a while. Dan shifted against his leg.

"Grandma marked one," he said. "For Mom."

He opened the Bible on the rail, and John took out his phone and held its light over the page. It was the King James, with tissue-thin paper and a lot of pencil in it. Tyler turned to the place without looking for it. In the margin beside two verses, in small careful pencil, was *K. launch 3/14/28*.

"You read it," Tyler said. "I read it already."

John read it out loud.

"*If I take the wings of the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea; even there shall thy hand lead me, and thy right hand shall hold me.*"

He read it once more to himself. Tyler was looking out over the dark water past the end of the Hook, and then up.

"We're on the sea," Tyler said. "And she's up there."

"Yes."

"So it's both."

John didn't say anything. He held the light steady until Tyler shut the book, and then he turned it off.

"Naomi had one marked too," he said after a while. "In Romans. About hoping for what you can't see. If you can already see it, she said, it isn't hope." He looked up at the sky over the Strait, where nothing was going over. "I used to think that was a nice thing to say."

"Is it?"

"I don't know yet."

Tyler picked up Dan's leash. "Commander Pruitt said seven," he said. "That's everybody. I'm going to bed."

"Okay."

The boy went across the flight deck and down the ladder with the dog ahead of him. John stayed at the rail until he heard the door below.

In his cabin he took Naomi's Bible off the shelf over the desk and turned to the Psalms. She hadn't marked the hundred and thirty-ninth. He read the two verses again in her translation, which said them a little differently, and left the book open on the desk under the lamp when he lay down.

### 10/2/2028, 10:40 local Pacific Time

Catherine had asked John to drive her up on the Monday check, and she didn't say why until they had dated the notice on the hospital glass. The envelope under it was empty.

"I want to use the blood gas machine in the ED," she said. "Bring the cart."

The machine sat on a counter in the back of the emergency department on a red outlet, the size of a small printer, with a touchscreen and a slot for the syringe. It had been running since the fifth on the hospital's generator with nobody to feed it. The screen showed a column of warnings in red: calibration overdue, quality control not performed, three more under those. Catherine read each one and cleared them.

"It wants to calibrate," she said. "Twenty minutes. Then it'll tell me if it'll take a sample."

"What are you testing?"

"Carbon monoxide." She found a box of blood gas syringes in a drawer under the counter and checked the date on it. "I said at the bridge it looked like cyanide or carbon monoxide. I want to take one of those off the list."

The calibration ran. Catherine put on a gown over her clothes, then a mask and a face shield, then two pairs of gloves, and handed John the same. They went down the hall to the closed rooms where they had put the three from the department the first night. John had written their names on the nurses' station counter, and he hadn't been back.

It was nearly four weeks. Catherine went in first and didn't stop at the door. She worked at the groin of the patient from bay 4 with the syringe for a long time, changed the angle twice, and came out with a few milliliters of something very dark. She capped it and wrote on the label. Then the nurse, and the tech. Three syringes in her gloved hand. John held the doors.

The machine had passed its calibration. She fed it the first syringe and stood back while it drew the sample in. It took about a minute.

"Read me the line that says COHb," she said. She was stripping off her outer gloves.

"One point six percent."

"That's normal. A nonsmoker's under three. Somebody who died of carbon monoxide is forty, fifty, sixty." She ran the second. "And?"

"Two point one."

The third was one point four.

"Those numbers hold up in a dead body," Catherine said. "Carbon monoxide sticks to the hemoglobin and stays. The oxygen numbers don't mean anything after this long, but that one does." She took the printouts off the machine and folded them once. "It wasn't carbon monoxide. Not in these three."

"And cyanide?"

"I can't test for it here. You send that out to a reference lab." She looked at him over the mask. "But it would have to be in every bedroom in Georgia, and on Sam's airplane at thirty-six thousand feet, and on Johan's ship in the middle of the Pacific, and on a pickup driver on an open bridge, all in the same few minutes. There's no fire on the bridge. There's no source."

They went out to the ambulance bay and took the gowns off in the air. Catherine sat on the bumper of a parked ambulance with the printouts on her knee.

"You know why they're pink," she said.

"Tell me anyway."

"Your blood picks up oxygen in your lungs, and it's bright red. Then it goes around, and your cells pull the oxygen out and use it, and the blood that comes back is dark. That's why a vein looks blue." She held up one finger. "Carbon monoxide makes the blood red because it sits on the hemoglobin in place of the oxygen, and it's red whether there's oxygen or not. That's one way to get cherry red." She held up a second finger. "Cyanide does it the other way. The blood's full of oxygen, the way it should be, and the cells can't take it out. There's a last step inside the cell where the oxygen actually gets used, and cyanide jams it. So the blood comes back to the heart still bright red, and the person suffocates sitting in clean air."

John thought about Della on the bridge, pulling in all the air she could and getting nothing from it.

"Della said it felt like the air didn't do anything," he said.

"Yes." Catherine looked at the printouts. "That's what I heard too. She was breathing fine. Her lungs were working. Her blood was probably full of oxygen. And it didn't help."

"So it's the second one."

"So it acts like the second one. Every one of them I've looked at since the fifth. And there isn't any cyanide." She folded the paper smaller. "Something stopped the cells from using oxygen. All at once, everywhere. I don't know what does that. I don't know of anything that does that."

"Can you test it on us? On me, on you?"

"I could draw yours right now and it would be normal. Mine too. We're fine. Whatever it did, it isn't doing it to us." She put the printouts in her shirt pocket. "That's the problem. The only people it shows up in are dead."

John got out his notebook and turned to the six questions. Next to *2. How did this happen?* he had *Hypoxia?* from the first night in Alpharetta. He wrote *not CO (3 dead, OMC, Oct 2)*, and under it, *cells can't use oxygen? (Catherine)*.

She read it over his arm.

"Put a question mark on the second one," she said. "A big one."

He already had. He made it bigger.

### 10/3/2028, 16:25 local Pacific Time

Sam came back from town with a roll of wire and a paper bag of apples. He set the wire on the wardroom deck and put an apple beside John's laptop.

"How much did you look at the antenna houses?" John asked.

"Both addresses. The first one's a receiver. I brought the book, not the box. The other house has a room full of stuff I can't identify."

He put photographs on the laptop from the camera. They had found the addresses in an old club directory on Arthur's shelf, on the trip back for more of his papers. John had marked the ones they could check on their errands, rather than send Sam up and down every street looking at roofs.

A radio sat beneath a shelf in one photograph, with an external speaker and its microphone lying across an open book. Sam had taken pictures of the rear connections and the cover of the manual. John enlarged that one and wrote the title down.

"We'll go together," he said. "After the next flight."

He had flown the Cessna with Sam twice since the first search. On the second trip Sam had let him bring it all the way in, then kept him aboard to explain why he had let the airplane drift toward the edge of the runway while he looked at the windsock. John had written that in the airplane's binder before he had written anything about the houses they passed.

They had repeated the first search route, and part of a second one, taking photographs from the same side where they could. John had mistaken laundry left on a line for a new signal until he found it in the earlier photograph. On days they didn't fly, they checked notices, inspected the airplane or worked at the radios. The hospital envelope remained empty. Once, a gust had torn it loose, and Catherine brought it back with wet corners and asked for something that would keep the paper dry.
