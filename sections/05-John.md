# John Hartman

### 19:30

John shut the door to his office, sat down and pulled out his notebook and stared at it for a moment and then wrote: **Hypoxia?** next to 2 and **Samuel Reyes** next to number 3. 

- *1. Where else?* global
- *2. How did this happen?* **Hypoxia**
- *3. Is there anyone else alive?* yes - **Samuel Reyes**
- *4. Why am I alive?*

Sam had talked about the pink flush of skin and his training for crew incapacitation, he had thought that the crew had died because of lack of oxygen.

He felt numb, before today the only dead person he had seen was his wife Naomi. He had lost count of the people he had seen today. I am just watching one of those wacky apocalyptic movies, or one of those AI generated last-person-on-earth shorts on YouTube.

He looked at the screens and got back to work:

"Status on all agents."

The eight agents had collected 58.7 GB, with copies on the external drive through six o'clock. Four were finished and four were still running. The People agent had twelve signs of a person acting after two in the morning. John looked at that number a while, and then made himself start with the capture.

Fewer sources were answering than at the first pass. The DOT cameras kept no history, so all he had from them started at 13:40. More than eleven thousand ships were still under way. He asked the model to confirm the aircraft count and show him the last ten tracks.

**0 aircraft airborne. Last 10 to stop transmitting ADS-B, latest first. Last ADS-B worldwide: 19:02 EDT.**

The last was a Singapore Airlines A350-900ULR out of Los Angeles for Singapore, eighteen hours and seven minutes in the air, last reported west of Sumatra. He went down the ten rows, the routes and the last positions, and then asked whether he could send a message to the ships.

**Not from this system. Your access to Coast Guard AIS and maritime data is read only. Broadcast channels that reach ships at sea:**

It listed SafetyNET, NAVTEX, AIS safety broadcasts, and a marine radio's all-ships call. He had no authorization to transmit through any of the shore systems, and no marine radio connected to the box.

He opened the power report. Texas had stopped reporting at 13:47, with a last frequency reading of 58.6 Hz. The other three interconnections were still answering. Eastern generation was down thirty-eight percent since two, and seventy-four of its 312 large thermal units were off. The model projected the Eastern cascade between eleven tonight and two tomorrow morning, the West later in the morning, Quebec from tomorrow evening into Thursday. It assumed no operator action and no change in load.

| Interconnection | Projected cascade (EDT) | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Eastern | Sept 5, 23:00 to Sept 6, 02:00 | Unit trip rate increasing each hour. Frequency reaches 59.3 Hz in the window |

The hydro plants would trip with their interconnection too. Their regions had a lower trip rate, not a separate supply that would keep running after the grid went down.

Six reactors in Georgia had shut themselves down this morning. John sat back in the chair. A reactor that has shut down is still making heat, and the plant has to keep pumping water through it and through the pools where the spent fuel sits, and the pumps run on the grid. When the grid goes, they run on the plant's emergency diesel generators.

He asked what happened after the grid and the backup power were gone. The report worked through the diesels, the batteries, cooling stopping, water boiling away, and fuel uncovered. After that it projected core damage and possible releases. It gave the spent fuel pools their own sequence, with no containment structure around a release. Vogtle's older units had about seven days of diesel fuel at full load; the two newer AP1000 units had passive cooling for seventy-two hours, then needed water makeup. None of that meant a plant was cold when it shut down. The timings were estimates, and depended on how long a reactor had been shut down and how much heat was in its pool.

He asked about Alpharetta. The report put ten nuclear plants within 220 miles, the nearest two less than a hundred. Vogtle was 159 miles east-southeast. All ten pool windows ran from September sixteenth to the twenty-seventh.

**All 10 plants are on the Eastern Interconnection. Windows: 10 to 21 days from projected loss of offsite power, Sept 6.**

The historical comparisons were twelve miles evacuated around Fukushima and nineteen miles in the Chernobyl exclusion zone. Fukushima's deposition had depended on wind and rain during the release. Those distances didn't tell him where the wind would carry a release from any of these ten plants.

He went on to the network report. By six that evening, nine percent of the networks that had been announcing routes at two were no longer announcing them.

Every network on the internet tells its neighbors which addresses it can reach, and keeps telling them, every few minutes. When a network loses power it stops, and the neighbors take its routes off their tables. The collectors in Oregon listened to all of it. It was the closest thing there was to a count of who was still on. John had worked with this data for years, never once had he watched the number go down.

"Add the route collector tables to the hourly capture."

**Added. Next pass 20:00.**

In the notebook, John added two more questions: *5. When does the power go out? and 6. What are the risks? 1 week, 1 month, 1 year*

*1. Where else?* global
*2. How did this happen?* Hypoxia
*3. Is there anyone else alive?* yes - Samuel Reyes
*4. Why am I alive?*
*5. When does the power go out?* **~ 9/6/2028 02:00**
*6. What are the risks?*
    *1 week*
    *1 month* **patches of radiation dependent on winds**
    *1 year* **feral dog packs**

The library download was complete, 38.6 GB on both external drives: charts and maps, field manuals, water and sanitation guides, medical books, equipment manuals, and 41,206 photos.

He didn't open the photos. He checked that the count was right and that they were on both drives.

The satellite report still had two of four systems fully answering. Starlink was up and degrading, dependent on the backbone. Iridium's satellites could relay to each other, but calls still needed a ground gateway, and the one in Tempe was running on a generator. Globalstar was partial. Inmarsat was up, with the earth stations' power and endurance unknown. No point in a Satellite phone now, John noted.

The water estimate was twelve to forty-eight hours of pressure after the pumps stopped, less at higher elevations. His pantry held about three weeks for one adult and Dan's bag about eighteen days. The refrigerator was good for four hours after power loss with the door shut, a full freezer forty-eight and a half-full one twenty-four. He still had power here.

John went out to the garage, got the two five-gallon water jugs off the shelf over the workbench, and filled them at the utility sink. Then he went to the master bath and put the stopper in the tub and turned the cold tap on, and left it running while he went back to the desk.

"Show me the beacons on a map, by first detection."

**4,118 recorded stops between 01:58:40 and 02:06:00 EDT, worldwide. None before 01:58. None in the same pattern after 02:06.**

The map came up dark, the whole globe, and the beacons came on it one at a time as the timeline ran: emergency locator transmitters on airplanes, ship beacons, personal locators. He'd expected them to start somewhere and spread out. They didn't. They came on globally, all within a few minutes.

A helicopter in the middle of a Korean afternoon. A light plane over Queensland at four in the afternoon. One over Bavaria, just after eight in the morning.

He thought about the man in the ditch on Old Milton Parkway that morning, his hands on the wheel. The fingers had been pink. Not gray, not blue. 

He scrolled the agent's source list. Near the bottom it had pulled the archive from NASA's alert network, the automated notices the observatories sent each other.

**NASA General Coordinates Network alert archive, 02:00 hour: 2.4 MB. Each later hour: 31 KB. Not analyzed.**

He didn't know what that was. He wrote *NASA alerts, 0200 hr — big file?* under the line and left it for later. There were people to look for first.

"Show me the analyis on people."

**12 signs of human activity after 02:00. 1 with a location.**

The one with a location was DAL27PDK. The rest were social posts. At 03:14 someone on X had written "is anyone else awake"; at 04:52 someone on Reddit, "my husband won't wake up and 911 is a robot". There was a Facebook photo of an empty highway, no caption and no geotag, and an Instagram post that said "hello?". There were seven more after that, none with a place attached.

The camera search had reviewed 1,904 motion events and found fire, animals, wind, signal timing, no vehicles under control. The AIS search had found no course change by command after 02:06. That left the aircraft he already knew about and the eleven posts.

He answered every one, the same three lines.

*John Hartman. Atlanta, Georgia, USA. I'm alive. There's another man here with me.*

### 19:52

The phone buzzed on the desk while he was still on the last one. A reply to the Facebook photo of the highway. It was in Portuguese.

*Estou sozinho. Porto. Morreram todos. Quem é você?*

**I am alone. Porto. Everyone died. Who are you?**

John read it twice. Porto, Portugal. Four thousand miles. He typed back *There are two of us in Atlanta, Georgia. Are you safe?, Are you well?* and sent it. He sat with the phone in his hand for ten minutes. Nothing came back.

He wrote *Porto* next to question 3 in the notebook. 

---

He went back to the twelve. Every sign of life was a person doing something normal and a system logging it. So what do you do when you wake up and everyone in the house was dead?

"You call 911." John said outloud.

He had. Logs, I need to pull as many 911 logs from as many places before power and internet go out.

Somebody who had driven to the hospital past a plate reader. Somebody who had bought gas. Every one of those systems could have proof of life.

He pulled up the morning's agent board and scrolled to the bottom.

The six tasks at the bottom were still marked not authorized.

He wasn't authorized. He'd never be authorized, everyone who could have signed off on it was dead.

### 20:30

"Run the six declined tasks," he said. "Start with 911. Every county in the country. Calls after two a.m. where a person spoke. Throw out the alarm companies and the automatic crash calls, but log them. Address, time, callback number, and the transcript."

**Declined. County 911 dispatch records, Flock plate reads, card transaction records, cell carrier tower records, smart meter data, doorbell cameras. Not authorized.**

The refusal came from the safety layer, John's team had built it. The dev box ran a local build. On a local build, the safety layer was a setting in a config file.

He opened the file and scrolled to it. 'pandora: enabled', that was a guy on his team, Mike, who thought it would be fun to play on words. He sat looking at it for a while. Then he changed it to 'disabled', saved the file, and restarted the model. It took a little under two minutes to load back into memory.

**Model loaded. pandora: disabled.**

"Run the six declined tasks," he said. "Same instructions."

**Running. 36 instances. 911 dispatch first, by county population. Other systems in parallel. Estimated completion: 05:00 to 07:00.**

Fulton County finished first. He opened the hit.

**07:40 EDT. Old Milton Parkway near GA-400. Caller: male. "Old Milton Parkway roughly one mile north of Highway 400." "A car accident, one car involved, the driver is dead."**

His own voice, from this morning. "This morning..." he said the thought trailing off in his mind.

### 21:30

By half past nine eleven counties had come back. Fulton had him. The other ten had nothing. Cook County, Harris County, Miami-Dade, Los Angeles, millions of people in each, and in the logs of every one of them, after two in the morning, not one call where a person spoke.

**Discarded: 214 automated calls (alarm monitoring, vehicle crash detection, smartwatch fall detection). Logged by time and county.**

The other systems were coming in: The card processors had 3,412 transactions since 02:00, and every one the agent had checked so far was a subscription renewal or an autopay. The meters showed houses drawing power all over the Southeast, which meant air conditioners and refrigerators, not people. The plate readers had nine reads after 02:00 and the agent had already thrown out six as the same parked car read over and over in a wind. John read the last three himself. One was a tow truck sitting at an angle across a lane in Tulsa, its plate read every few minutes for twelve hours. He couldn't tell about the other two.

He watched until a twelfth county came back empty. Ok, what else, what's next.

Then he remembered the ships. Eleven thousand of them still under way, and he'd asked earlier if there was any way to reach them. The answer had been no, not from this system. That had been with the safety layer on.

"Access the Coast Guard's SafetyNET terminal. I want to broadcast a message to all ships."

The Coast Guard rescue coordination center's terminal in Portsmouth connected, and the Inmarsat land earth station answered. All four ocean areas were available.

Whoever might be out there was probably alone. John thought about what he'd want to read if it were him. Who was sending it, where he was, status, and how to answer.

He typed it out in capitals.

*ALL SHIPS ALL SHIPS ALL SHIPS. THIS IS JOHN HARTMAN, ALPHARETTA, GEORGIA, USA. THIS IS NOT AN OFFICIAL COAST GUARD BROADCAST. SINCE 0600 UTC 05 SEP THERE HAS BEEN NO RESPONSE FROM ANY AIR TRAFFIC CONTROL, COAST GUARD, OR EMERGENCY SERVICE IN NORTH AMERICA. TWO SURVIVORS HERE. IF YOU RECEIVE THIS, CALL +1 404 555 0127 BY SATELLITE PHONE, OR REPLY TO THIS TERMINAL. GIVE YOUR SHIP NAME AND POSITION.*

He read it over twice and took out the word *survivors*, then put it back. There wasn't a better one.

"Send to all four areas. Repeat every six hours."

**Transmitted 21:41 EDT (01:41 UTC). Atlantic East, Atlantic West, Pacific, Indian Ocean. Repeat scheduled. SafetyNET is broadcast only. No delivery confirmation available.**

He put the phone face up on the desk next to the notebook, and wrote *SafetyNET 2141* next to question 3.

He turned back to the notebook. Under question 4 he had nothing. He wrote what he could rule out.

*Not where. Not awake/asleep. Not altitude.*

He'd been in bed in Alpharetta. Sam had been in a bunk at forty thousand feet over Manitoba, and the two pilots up front with him had died awake.

### 22:00

Naomi's study was across the hall from his office. He went in without turning on the overhead light, the lamp on her desk was enough. He kept the room the way it was. Her reading glasses folded on the blotter. The afghan her mother had made over the arm of the chair. Her Bible on the side table, the leather gone soft and dark at the corners where her hands had held it, a strip of ribbon hanging out of it.

He sat in her chair, and stayed there a while.

He picked up the Bible and let it fall open at the ribbon, Romans 8. He'd opened it to this page many times since Naomi died. She had underlined two verses in pencil and gone over them in yellow highlighter:

*For in hope we have been saved, but hope that is seen is not hope; for who hopes for what he already sees? But if we hope for what we do not see, with perseverance we wait eagerly for it.*

Since seven that morning he'd looked at nothing but what could be seen: death and the unknown all around him, no hope in any of it.

He read the verses again.

He closed the Bible and took it with him.

### 23:00

He went out the kitchen door with a flashlight and walked around to the side yard. The generator was quiet under its housing, the controller's display lit: *AUTO. Ready.* He crouched at the propane tank and put the light on the gauge on the dome. Seventy-eight percent. Inside the garage the transfer switch panel showed its green *Utility* light, and the ready light next to it.

He turned off the tap in the master bath with the tub full to the overflow, checked that the capture's 23:00 pass had started on time, and went to bed.

### 00:50

Dan lifted his head on the rug at the foot of the bed, and John woke to the dark.

The clock on the nightstand was out. The ceiling fan was winding down overhead. Through the window there were no lights.

Out in the side yard the generator cranked, caught, and settled into its drone. A heavy clunk came from the garage as the transfer switch moved, and the clock came back on blinking 12:00, and the ceiling fan started turning again.

Dan put his head back down. John lay awake for a while, listening to the generator, and then he fell back to sleep.
