# 14 — Yohan, Day 0

Nobody woke him for the watch change, and that was the first thing wrong.

Yohan had spent twenty-six years learning his body to a ship's rhythm well enough that he rarely needed the knock at his door at all, some old internal clock doing the job before the second mate's knuckles ever touched wood — but this time he surfaced late, disoriented, the light through the porthole already gray instead of black, the *Pacific Tender*'s engines running exactly as steady beneath him as they had every hour of the crossing from Yokohama. Steady was good. Steady was also, he realized once he was fully awake, the only thing on this ship that still made any sense.

He dressed fast and went up to the bridge expecting to find Okafor irritated with him, ready with some dry comment about senior mates who slept through their own relief — and found him instead slumped forward against the console, one hand still resting on the autopilot panel like he'd meant to make some small correction and simply never finished the thought. The ship was holding her course dead true, exactly where Okafor had last set her, GPS track laid out clean and unbroken on the display like nothing at all had happened up here. Yohan checked for a pulse out of the same procedural instinct that had gotten him through two typhoons and one fire in a cargo hold off Vladivostok, found nothing, and made himself keep moving instead of standing there working out what it meant.

The ship's clocks had run nine hours behind Greenwich the whole crossing, which put it at just after nine the evening before when whatever this was had happened — Yohan did the small, useless arithmetic anyway, the way a man reaches for a fact he can hold onto when the facts around him have stopped making sense.

He found Second Engineer Alvarez dead in the engine control room, upright in his chair with a logbook still open in front of him, the entry unfinished mid-sentence. He found the cook dead against the galley counter, a pot on the stove gone dry and scorched black sometime in the hours since. He worked through the accommodation block after that, cabin by cabin, the whole twenty-two of them, and got the same answer every time — no struggle, no mess, no sign that any one of them had known enough to be afraid. It took him the better part of two hours to be sure, moving through his own ship like a stranger in it, and by the end of it he understood, with the flat certainty of a man doing a job because someone had to, that he was the only living soul aboard the *Pacific Tender*.

He tried the VHF first, out of habit more than hope, working through every channel that mattered and getting nothing back but the ordinary static of an empty ocean. The single-sideband set gave him the same silence on frequencies that should have had coastal traffic on them at any hour. He pulled up the AIS repeater next and sat with it a long moment, feeling something cold move through him that had nothing to do with the weather — a dozen other vessels showing on the display within range, tracks laid out clean across the plot exactly like his own, every one of them still making way, not one of them answering a single call. A ghost fleet, steaming on through the North Pacific on whatever course its last living hand had set it, same as his.

The satellite terminal in the radio room was the last thing he tried, and for twenty minutes it gave him almost nothing — a handful of news fragments loading in slow, broken pieces before the connection finally gave out for good, half-sentences about the eastern seaboard, about Europe, about numbers that didn't finish rendering before the page died. Enough to tell him this wasn't the *Pacific Tender*. Enough to tell him it wasn't Japan, or the mainland he was steaming toward, or any one country at all. He sat with that for a while in the small blue light of a screen that had gone dark and wasn't coming back, and let himself feel exactly as small as the ocean around him actually was.

Grief came for him too, eventually, sitting alone in a radio room built for six men and currently holding one — for Okafor, for Alvarez and his unfinished logbook entry, for a cook whose name he was ashamed to realize he'd have to check the crew manifest to remember properly. He let it move through him the way he'd learned, over a life spent mostly at sea, to let weather move through him: fully, without fighting it, and then past.

What was left, once it had passed, was the actual problem, and Yohan was a practical man before he was anything else. Twenty-two men dead did not change the fact that this ship, and whatever cargo sat in her holds, was still three days from Seattle and still entirely capable of getting there — engines sound, fuel more than sufficient, autopilot holding a course that would put her somewhere reasonably close to the Strait of Juan de Fuca whether or not anyone touched the helm between now and then. He was a senior mate with an electrician's understanding of half the systems keeping her alive, and he had run watches alone before, if never for three straight days, if never with no one else on board to relieve him at all.

He climbed back up to the bridge in the flat gray light of a morning that didn't care what had happened to anyone on it, looked out at an ocean exactly as vast and exactly as indifferent as it had always been, and took the helm off autopilot just long enough to feel the ship answer to his own hand — a small, deliberate thing, more ritual than necessity, the same way a captain touches his own wheel before a hard passage even when the autopilot would do just as well.

"All right," he said, out loud, to no one, because there was no one left to hear it and saying it anyway felt like the first genuinely useful thing he'd done all morning. "Seattle, then."

He put her back on course, and settled in for the watch that would not, this time, be relieved by anyone at all.
