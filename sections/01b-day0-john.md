### 11:35 ###

An Airbus 350-900 operated by Delta airlines with departure of Atlanta and destination of Seoul Sourth Korea had landed at Edmonton, Alberta at 04:36am EDT. The other had departed Edmonton at 07:31am, a Pilatus PC-12 with no destination filed and the flight number read **DAL27PDK** and it was squaking **7700**, general emergency. "Give me full details of **DAL27PDK** PC-12 departed Edmonton at 07:31am." Response: **Arrived Minneapolis-St. Paul 10:45 EDT. Departed Minneapolis-St. Paul 11:30 EDT.** He glanced at the time, it was 11:40am, that plane had left Minneapolis 10 minutes ago! "Have any of the other 351 aircraft diverted from their flight path after 2am EDT?" Response: **No**. "Show the live track of **DAL27PDK**." The PC-12 was passing through 8,000 feet in a southeast heading. "DAL27PDK", John said outloud. He looked back at the A350-900 that landed in Edmonton, it was Delta flight 27. The pieces fell together, a pilot from DAL27 is now flying this PC-12 and the destination is PDK. John knew PDK, it was Peachtree Dekalb Airport, it is where he spent most weekends maintaining or flying his Cessna 182.

"Nautical miles from Edmonton to PDK?" Response: **1,700 miles**. "What is the range of the Pilatus PC-12?" Response: **1,803 nm with medium load**. So he stopped in Minneapolis-St. Paul for fuel. "Distance from Minneapolis-St. Paul to PDK and PC-12 flight time? Response: **780nm and 2 h 47 min flighttime at 280 kt.** "That puts him on the ground between 14:15 and 14:45." John said outloud. He pulled out his notebook and wrote next to question 1: *global* and next to question 3: *Yes*.
 
**Sam Section 3**


He had numbers in his phone from years of work with the agencies, the operations lines his project contacts had given him and told him to keep. FEMA's operations center in D.C. The FAA's Command Center out in Warrenton, Virginia, the room that ran traffic flow for the whole country. The Coast Guard's National Command Center in Washington.

The FEMA line rang until it dropped. The FAA's picked up on a recording that asked him to hold for the next available specialist, and he held for five minutes listening to it loop. He'd stood in that room in Warrenton. Rows of specialists at their consoles, the whole country's traffic up on the wall in front of them, a place that never closed. He knew how many people it took to staff a shift in there. "Please continue to hold," the recording said, and started over. He sat with the phone against his ear a while after he'd stopped listening to it, and when he finally hung up he felt his hands trembling. The Coast Guard line rang and rang. Under the first question he wrote: FEMA, FAA, USCG. Nobody.

He started the way he started with any system he hadn't built: find out what was actually there before trusting any of it. He had the model walk every connector his credentials reached and ping each one. It took a few minutes. The FAA's surveillance feeds answered, NOAA's weather and the beacon alerts out of Suitland, the space weather alerts out of Boulder, the USGS earthquake feed out of Golden. The orbital element sets the Space Force published for everything in orbit `[verify: Space-Track account]`. The automated notices NASA pushed out to observatories, gamma-ray and neutrino alerts meant for telescopes, open to anyone who registered for a key `[verify: SNEWS alerts distributed via GCN]`. The Coast Guard's ship tracking, the state DOT camera networks state by state, the Energy Information Administration's hourly grid tables, and the frequency readings a university lab in Knoxville collected off sensors plugged into ordinary wall outlets across the country. A handful of state camera networks timed out; he had it note which ones and keep trying them. Everything he touched was something his credentials already let him read. He checked that twice.

Then the capture. A live system was a thing that would stop, and whatever it was showing would go with it. You took its state while you could and worked out what it meant afterward. Collect first, while the pipes are still open. Analyze later. He put his hands on the keyboard, and by the end of the first line they were steady again. He wrote out the job for the model the way he'd have written a spec for his team: every hour on the hour, a snapshot of every feed that answered, written to the dev box's internal storage, each hour in its own folder, nothing overwritten. He had it time a full pass first. Twenty-two minutes, most of it waiting on slow state camera servers, so he set it to hourly. That left room for a pass to run slow without stepping on the next one, and it was fine enough to catch anything that moved on purpose. He checked the free space on the drives against an hour's worth of data, a few gigabytes, mostly camera frames, and the drives would hold years of it.

Then he pointed it backward. The flight-tracking and AIS services kept history, and so did the EIA's tables, the beacon logs, and the alert streams, so the model could go back and fill in the hours already gone, one snapshot per hour from 2:00 a.m. on. He watched the folder names stack up in the directory list, 0200, 0300, 0400. In the 0200 folder the file from NASA's observatory alerts was bigger than that feed's files for every hour after it put together. He noted it and didn't open it. The cameras kept nothing. Their record would start with the first pass, at 9:40 that morning, and everything before that existed only in what he'd seen on his walk.

He didn't stop to look at what the feeds were showing him. He knew himself well enough to know that once he started looking he'd go too deep and wouldn't come back up for hours. Looking could wait. Whatever answered the first question would be on the drives.

He looked at the pad again. He couldn't answer any of the four this morning, and whatever answered them later would have to come out of what he saved now. So he went after everything else that lived on somebody else's server and would be gone when that server went dark. He started with the FAA's charts, the current cycle: every approach plate in the country, the Chart Supplement with every airport's runways and frequencies and fuel, the sectionals and the IFR enroute charts, onto the dev box and onto the iPad he flew with. The FAA's download site was slow, then fine.

Then maps. Every map he'd ever pulled up on a screen came as tiles off somebody's server, over cell towers that ran on the same grid as everything else. He pulled a full OpenStreetMap extract for North America, fourteen and a half gigabytes compressed, and the open-source software to draw it, maps that would work with no signal at all.

Then the manuals and how-tos, everything he could think of that he might need to fix or run with nobody to call. The service manual for his generator. The 182's maintenance manual. The Army's survival and field manuals, which had always been public. EPA guides on treating water. The Merck Manual's free online edition, page by page, for the day somebody got hurt with no doctor to call. Manuals for radios he didn't own yet. He kept adding to the list on the pad as he went, and every time he crossed one off he thought of two more.

Somewhere in the middle of it Dan came down the hall and lay across the office doorway with his chin on his paws, where he could see him. After a while he got up and put his nose under John's hand on the desk, and John scratched behind his ears without looking away from the screen until Dan went back to the doorway and lay down again.

Then the agency data he was cleared for. The Homeland Security infrastructure datasets, every power plant, hospital, airport, fuel terminal, and dam in the country as points on a map. The public version had been shut down a few years back; the restricted one still took his login and the data-use agreement a DHS sponsor had signed for his team. NOAA's latest forecast runs. The EIA's tables on every generating plant. He glanced at the grid numbers while they came down, since they were the thing he understood best. His own corner of the Southeast was still posting every hour, an hour or so behind the clock the way the EIA's tables always ran `[verify: EIA-930 posting lag]`, and the Knoxville sensors had the frequency still sitting near sixty. He knew enough about how a grid was run to know it wouldn't stay there with nobody running the plants. He thought about the generator in the side yard and the propane tank next to it, and wrote *generator* at the bottom of the pad, for later.

He copied everything onto two external drives, and when they were done he put one in his flight bag.

He almost didn't think of the photos. Twenty-some years of them, his and Naomi's, all of it sitting on a server somewhere like everything else. He started the download and let it run.

Somewhere in there he made himself a sandwich he ate standing up in the office doorway, and tried Mark again. Voicemail. He didn't leave another message.

He kept FlightAware open on the second screen the whole time. At a quarter to eleven DAL27PDK slowed over the Twin Cities and stopped at Minneapolis-St. Paul. He went back to work and checked it every few minutes, and at eleven-thirty it moved again, climbed out, and settled on a heading southeast. He measured it on the chart: Minneapolis to PDK was a little under eight hundred nautical miles, and at the ground speed it was showing, call it two hours forty-five. Somewhere around 2:15. He wrote *leave 12:15* on the pad and underlined it.

At noon the capture job wrote its noon snapshot, on time. He finished the last of his coffee while he spot-checked a few cameras in the folder against the live feed to make sure they matched. They did. But three of the state camera networks that had timed out that morning were still timing out, and that bothered him. He dug into the first one and found the state had moved its cameras behind a new address sometime in the last year and never updated the old one, so he rewired the connector and watched the frames start to come in. The second one was the same problem with a different fix. He was halfway into the third when he looked up at the clock in the corner of the screen. 12:41.

He took Dan's leash off the hook. "Come on, buddy, we're going for a ride." Dan was at the door before he was. 



He took 400 south. A mile down, the smoke he'd seen that morning was what was left of a fuel tanker, burned down to the frame. The trees along 400 were on fire, and it was spreading toward the buildings beyond. That may have been the thud at 2:05. Most of the cars had drifted off to the side, into the median cables, off the right into the ditch, a few all the way into the trees, and the ones still in the lanes sat at angles he had to steer around. He drove it at fifty anyway, then sixty, picking the open lane a quarter mile ahead and changing lanes early. 

Back on 400 he stopped looking into the cars he passed. He took the ramp onto Interstate 285 east, and the stalled cars thinned out, and he sped up. Sixty, seventy, swinging wide around a minivan nosed into the median, a box truck on its side across the right two lanes. Twice he took the shoulder, gravel rattling in the wheel wells, Dan braced on the back seat with his nails dug into the upholstery.

Past the Ashford-Dunwoody exit the road bent left under an overpass, and on the far side of it a sedan sat dead in the middle lane. He was doing seventy-five. He stood on the brake and felt the pedal buzz under his foot as the ABS chattered, the tires hissing, the car sliding a little sideways before it caught, and Dan went off the back seat into the footwell with a yelp. He got it stopped about a car length short. He sat there a second with both hands on the wheel, breathing hard. In the sedan there was the shape of somebody slumped against the driver's window.

"Sorry, buddy," he said. Dan climbed back up onto the seat. John went around the sedan and brought it back up to sixty, and kept it there.

He came off at Peachtree Industrial and went south toward the airport. He had made this drive most Saturday mornings for twenty years, to the hangar and the Cessna 182. The daily walks with Dan and the weekend flights balanced out the long hours of concentration his work took. DeKalb-Peachtree had become the place he saw people most. In the past sixteen years he had eaten a lot of meals at the two restaurants on the field, the Downwind upstairs over Epps, the FBO, and the 57th Fighter Group down by runway 3.
