# 7 — John, Day 2

John didn't have an answer for him, and said so. "Get him through today. Then figure out how you talk to a space station."

"That's about where I landed too." Sam let it sit a moment longer, then pulled the high-altitude enroute chart out of the side pocket, the one he'd taken off the rack in the Pilatus dealer's lobby at PDK, and folded it open across his kneeboard so both of them could see it. On the center pedestal the two little touchscreen controllers glowed, the route already keyed in: Huntsville, Memphis, Little Rock, Oklahoma City, Amarillo, Tucumcari, then southwest across New Mexico and down into Phoenix. The box said twelve hundred eighty-five nautical miles. "That's the easy part. Now the part the box can't do for us."

"Which is?"

"Wind." Sam tapped the blank strip along the top of the flight plan page where the winds-aloft data would normally have loaded. "No uplink, no forecast. Last one anybody published is from Monday night and it's worthless now. So I plan off what the jet stream usually does over the southern plains this time of year, which is blow right in our face. Say thirty knots at flight level two-eight-zero, and I'm guessing." He pencilled the numbers down the margin of the kneeboard card as he talked, the same unhurried tap of the pencil John had watched him use on a very different airplane two days ago. "Max cruise, climb, descent, call it five hours and change in the air. Burn for the trip itself comes out around twenty-three, twenty-four hundred pounds. Then the reserve."

"The legal one?"

"Mine. Ninety minutes, not forty-five. Nobody on the ground to tell us the runway's clear, nobody to tell us the weather, and if there's a dead airliner sitting across the numbers at Sky Harbor I want enough gas to go somewhere else and still land with something in the tanks." He wrote the total at the bottom and circled it. "Thirty-one hundred pounds. We carry twenty-seven hundred and four, full. That's not close. That's four hundred pounds of airplane that doesn't exist."

"So we don't make it."

"Not in one bite, no." Sam didn't sound bothered, which John was starting to recognize as its own kind of information: a man who would rather find the hole on paper than find it at twenty-eight thousand feet over New Mexico with the fuel totalizer counting down. "Good news is it's one stop, not the two I guessed last night." His pencil came down on the chart about four-fifths of the way along the first long westbound line. "Amarillo. It sits almost dead on the route. Seven hundred thirty-five miles out, five hundred twenty-something from there to Phoenix. Both legs land with more than my ninety minutes, even if the wind's worse than I'm guessing."

"Don't launch with known holes," John said, half to himself. He'd heard Sam say it often enough now that it was starting to come out in his own voice.

Sam glanced over. "Now you're flying it right."

---

They lifted off Runway 18L at 8:40 local with the sun behind them and Huntsville falling away gray-green and quiet under the right wing. Dan had claimed the aft cabin floor between the last two seats before the door was even latched; Tyler was asleep again inside of ten minutes, curled against the window in a NASA T-shirt that had clearly been through a lot of washes, his curls flattened on one side where he'd slept on them.

Sam leveled them at FL280, let the autothrottle settle the torque, and watched the engine page for a full minute: ITT steady, oil pressure and temperature in the green, the fuel flow numbers matching what he'd pencilled. Then he looked across the pedestal.

"Hand-flying. You want it?"

John's hands were already on the yoke. "I've got it."

"Autopilot's coming off." Sam pressed the red disconnect button on his own yoke; the aural warning chirped once. "Your airplane."

"My airplane."

"Heading two-seven-zero, hold two-eight-zero. Yaw damper stays on. That's all you're doing."

It should have been easy. John had flown more hard IFR out of PDK in his 182 than most people he knew, but the 182 had never been above fourteen thousand feet, and nothing in it had prepared him for how little it took up here. The first time he pressed on the yoke to correct a hundred-foot climb, the vertical speed tape jumped past eight hundred feet a minute down and the altitude trend vector shot off the bottom of the tape. He pulled back, overshot, gained sixty feet going the other way. Thin air, high true airspeed, a heavy airplane with a lot of momentum: every input came back bigger than he'd put it in.

"Fingertips," Sam said. "Don't fly the altimeter. Put the flight path marker where it belongs and trim off the pressure. The altimeter follows."

John tried it. He found the flight path marker on the synthetic-vision display, parked it on the horizon line, and held it there with pressure he could barely feel, thumbing the trim until even that went away. Somewhere over the brown seam where Arkansas gave way to Oklahoma he noticed he'd held twenty-eight thousand plus or minus forty feet for ten minutes without once looking at the altitude numbers, and a while after that he stopped thinking about his hands at all. Sam didn't say much. He watched, and once or twice reached over and tapped the heading readout when it drifted a couple of degrees, and otherwise let him fly.

Tyler woke somewhere past Oklahoma City. After a while he unbuckled and came forward to kneel in the aisle behind the two front seats, chin on his folded arms, looking down at a patchwork of wheat stubble and dry creek beds a little more than four and a half miles below. "Is that Texas?"

"Panhandle," Sam said. "We're starting down in about ten minutes. Twenty after that we're on the ground. Just for fuel."

"Do you think—" Tyler started, then stopped, the way John had noticed he did whenever a sentence was headed somewhere he wasn't sure he wanted to go. His face gave the rest of it away before he said it. He tried again, smaller. "If you went up high enough, could you see the space station? For real, not just a dot."

"Not from here," Sam said, gentle about it. "This airplane tops out at thirty thousand feet. She's two hundred and fifty miles up. And you can't see her in daylight from anywhere. You need the ground dark and the station still up in the sun, about an hour after sunset, or before sunrise." He glanced back over his shoulder. "But if the sky's clear tonight, wherever we end up, I'll help you find her. She moves fast once you know where to look."

"I had an app," Tyler said. "It told you when she was coming over. It doesn't work without the internet."

John spoke without taking his eyes off the flight path marker. "It might, still. If it saved the orbit data before the network went down, it can keep predicting passes for a few days before the error gets big. Let me look at it when we land."

Tyler nodded like that was a fair trade for now, and stayed kneeling there a while longer, watching Texas slide by underneath him, a boy whose mother was somewhere overhead and impossibly far away at the same time.

---

Sam flew his low pass at Amarillo anyway, down the length of Runway 22 at two hundred feet, looking for debris, vehicles, anything on the surface. There was nothing: a wide, empty field under a hot, flat sky. He came around and landed, and as they taxied in, the terminal slid past with the airport's full name across the front in tall letters. Tyler read it aloud: "Rick Husband." Then, to nobody in particular: "He was the commander on Columbia." Nobody answered him. Sam's hand stayed on the power lever a beat longer than it needed to.

On the GA ramp, the fuel farm's self-serve kiosk was dark, its card reader dead along with everything else on a grid that had gone down two nights ago. Sam had expected that. What he'd hoped for was parked forty yards away along the fence line: a Jet-A refueler truck, keys hanging from the ignition, the tank gauge reading a little under two-thirds full.

"Ground vehicles," Sam said, the way another man might have said *thank God*. The truck's engine turned over on the first try. Its pump ran off the truck's own drive, so no grid required.

John got the nozzle. Sam made him clip the static bonding cable to the airframe grounding point before anything else ("Always first. Jet fuel and a spark is a short conversation") and then stopped him with a look at the foot of the stepladder.

"Not on my wing in those."

John looked down at his flip-flops. He went back to the cabin for the one pair of boots he'd thought to pack and came back in them, cap pulled low against the glare. The Texas sun came straight down off the concrete. Then he stood on the ladder with the heavy overwing nozzle braced against his hip and watched the meter on the truck roll: a hundred and ninety gallons into the left tank and the right, about thirteen hundred pounds. It was a small, deliberate handoff and neither of them commented on it. He was doing something useful, not just keeping Sam company. While he pumped, Sam sumped both tanks and held each sample tube up to the light, clear and pale straw, no water, no sediment, then worked the second leg on his kneeboard. Tyler walked Dan along the grass strip by the fence and waited patiently while Dan inspected every fence post twice.

Forty minutes on the ground. At 12:25 local they were rolling again, the sun high and white, and the number on Sam's kneeboard for the leg to Phoenix was two hours and six minutes, with the ninety minutes of reserve still in the tanks when they got there.

---

They picked up the desert a little over an hour later, the ground turning red and folded and enormous under them, and by the time Sky Harbor came up out of the heat shimmer the outside air temperature on the display read 41 degrees Celsius a few hundred feet above the field. A hundred and six. Sam ran the density altitude in his head out loud for John's benefit (eleven hundred feet of real elevation behaving like about forty-five hundred) and added a few knots to the approach for the gusts coming off the baked concrete.

He flew the low pass anyway, down Runway 26 at a couple of hundred feet: eleven thousand feet of runway, a real international field, wide enough that the PC-12 felt small over it. It was clear end to end. The airliners sat at the gates exactly where they'd been parked on Monday night, and in all that concrete not one thing was moving. He called his turn to final on the tower frequency out of habit; nobody answered. Guard stayed silent. The city spread out dead in every direction, block after block of it, and not one car on any of it.

He put them down on 26, rolled long to the high-speed exit, and was taxiing toward the GA ramp when John saw it: a single car, a gray Subaru, coming fast across the dirt infield from the north perimeter road, trailing a plume of dust, aimed at a stretch of perimeter fence as if the fence weren't part of the decision. It hit the chain-link at forty miles an hour and went through, dragging a folded panel of fence across its hood for twenty feet before it shook loose, fishtailed once on the ramp concrete, and stopped broadside about a hundred feet ahead of their nose.

Sam was already on the brakes, power back to ground idle, the airplane rocking to a stop on its trailing-link gear. He held it there with the five blades turning, watching the car the way he'd watch anything that might get anywhere near a spinning propeller.

"Is she—" John started.

"Alive," Sam said. "Driving like it." Only when the driver's door opened and a woman stepped out and stood clear did he shut the engine down. The propeller slowed and wound down to feather, and the silence on the ramp came back so complete that John could hear the Subaru's engine ticking as it cooled.

She was maybe fifty, tall and straight-backed, silver-streaked dark hair pinned up in a twist that had been holding for more than one day, thin titanium glasses, navy scrubs, a hospital ID still clipped at her hip. She didn't run at the airplane. She walked at it the way someone walks who has spent thirty years going toward emergencies instead of away from them, fast and level and without a wasted step. In the car's cup holder behind her, John saw a clear plastic cup of iced coffee, sweating in the heat, the ice not melted yet. He filed that away before he knew why: somewhere in this city something was still making ice.

"I heard your engine from the hospital," she called before either of them had the door fully open. "Three miles. I've been there since Tuesday morning because it was the only thing left that made sense to do. Yours is the first engine I've heard in three days that wasn't my own car." A glance back at the fence, at the torn chain-link laid over on the dirt. "Hence."

She stopped a few feet off the wingtip, looking from the airplane to the two men and to the boy peering out from behind them, and to the dog shoving past all of them to get to her first. Something in her face came open for a second. Then she caught it and put it back, the way John was already learning everyone out here eventually had to.

"Catherine Navarro," she said. "ER. Or I was." She let Dan push his head into her hand. "I'm extremely glad to see you."

"John," he said. "This is Sam. And this is Tyler. We found him in Huntsville yesterday."

Catherine looked at Tyler longer than at either of the men, and her professional composure eased. "Huntsville to here in a day," she said. "You've been busy."

"The ice," John said, before he could stop himself. "In your coffee."

"Hospital's on its generators. The ice machine in the doctors' lounge doesn't know anything happened." She looked at him more closely. "That's what you noticed first?"

"It's what he notices," Sam said. "We came a long way to find you."

"Find me how? I didn't tell anyone I was here. There isn't anyone to tell."

"You called 911 Tuesday," John said. "Something answered. It kept a record."

She was quiet for a moment. "I argued with it," she said. "For a while."

"That's how we found you."

Catherine looked at the torn fence behind her, then back at the three of them and the dog, and something that might have been the first real laugh any of them had heard in days came out of her, short and disbelieving. "Well," she said. "You found me."
