# John and Sam

John woke at six-thirty to the steady drone of the generator on the side of the house. He showered and came out to the kitchen. Sam was already at the counter with a coffee.

"Morning, Sam."

Sam looked up. "You were up late. Sounded like you were talking to yourself."

John smirked as he got out eggs, bacon, and hash browns.

"Till eleven, yeah. Sorry about that. I talk to the computer. Ever since AI, it's so much quicker to have the machine take speech to text," John said. "I was able to pull a lot of data before things started shutting down."

John put blueberries, avocado, beet juice, and spinach in the blender, ran it for about forty-five seconds, poured it into a glass, and offered some to Sam.

"No thanks, that looks awful. But your version of Waffle House is spot on." Sam watched John cook for a minute. "What exactly do you do?"

"I've been a systems engineer and architect for thirty-odd years. The last stretch at Google, on the public-sector side out of the D.C. office. My team builds AI models for the FAA, DOT, Coast Guard, NOAA, Homeland Security, FEMA. I have access to the data from those agencies."

"What have you found out?" Sam asked.

"Yesterday morning, after finding my neighbors dead and going for a drive and seeing more of the same, I came back home and started gathering data. That's when I found your flight. I broadened the search yesterday afternoon, and last night I started looking at what came back."

John told Sam what he'd found in the feeds he'd reviewed. The DOT camera samples hadn't given him another driver to look for. In the AIS records, ships were holding their courses, and many showed stopped along shorelines, not at ports.

"The most striking thing to me is that the feeds still answering showed no aircraft airborne. Last ADS-B reception in that collection was at 19:02 yesterday. The 02:00 snapshot had 9,500 aircraft. The only landings I've identified in the records I checked were yours."

Sam stared at him, his forehead furrowed.

"Yeah. I'm not sure what to make of it either." He paused. "The national power grid is shutting down fast." He talked through the four interconnections, the frequency sagging on each, the model putting the Eastern collapse around midnight. "We lost power here at 12:50. An ice storm a couple years ago inspired me to get the whole-house generator and Starlink."

He flipped the bacon. "Plant Vogtle is about a hundred and sixty miles southeast of here. All four reactors scrammed by 09:30 yesterday. The control rods drop into the core and the reaction stops, automatic, no operator required. But the spent fuel sitting in the cooling pools is still hot, and it still needs pumps to keep water moving over it. The pumps run on the site's emergency diesels and batteries. Three days, maybe a week, with nobody there to refuel them. Best real number I've got is somewhere between ten and twenty-one days before the Vogtle pools boil down far enough to expose the rods to open air. Then it's a local contamination event."

"Fallout?" Sam asked. "What do you mean?"

"Nothing like a bomb. Think Fukushima, not Hiroshima. Without the water the fuel heats up and the cladding starts to burn. Then you get cesium, iodine, strontium, into the soil and the water table for years, and drifting downwind as far as the wind takes it."

Sam didn't say anything for a second. "Do we need to get out of dodge?"

"It probably wouldn't reach this far, but I can't say that with a hundred percent certainty. It's definitely on my list of risks. It'd be safer to head west, away from most of the nuclear power plants."

At a quarter past seven Dan was at the door with his leash in his mouth. "You coming?" John asked. Sam was already reaching for his shoes.

They walked out into first light, Dan pulling at the leash. There was less barking than yesterday, and no sound at all from the Morrisons' house. They turned the corner at the bottom of the street. Dan stopped to examine the stop sign, and John waited and went on with his debrief. "From the records I've got, it looks like it happened within a few minutes in places all over the world. Most of that comes from the aircraft data. I couldn't get very deep on other systems outside the continental United States."

"Theresa and Ruth were fine at twelve-thirty," Sam said. "At 3:20ish when I came back forward neither of them had made a radio call or sent a message." He walked a few steps. "Whatever it was, they didn't have time."

They got to the subdivision entrance at Old Milton Parkway.

"Any leads on other people?" Sam asked.

"The search flagged eleven posts from other accounts. I answered them all. One wrote back, a man in Porto, Portugal. Alone. Haven't heard from him since. I asked it to post on my accounts on the drive to PDK, too, hadn't got the confirmations last night. I'd already had it researching the 911 records. Last night I took the safety check off and had it query what it could reach. It found one of my calls, but my gas purchase wasn't in what I'd reviewed."

They walked a while without talking. A dog barked in the house they were walking past, Dan answered it once.

"So what are you thinking?" Sam asked.

John had already decided for himself at eleven the night before, standing at the generator with the flashlight. "Staying here is risky. I'm hopeful the 911 logs give us some leads, somewhere to start."

"And if they don't?" Sam asked.

"Then we pick a less risky location and go." 


"Sounds good," John said.

**07:50**

Back at the house they went to the office. A lot of the 911 systems he'd been querying had stopped answering overnight. The returned records listed somewhere over four thousand calls after 02:00 Tuesday. Most were flagged as alarm panels or crash sensors, kept separate from human-call candidates. In the material reviewed, three new leads had locations they could try.

Someone in Huntsville, Alabama, had called sixteen times. The returned material had no audio or transcript, just an address, in a neighborhood a few miles northeast of the airport.

John dialed Huntsville first on Microsoft Teams, which would go out through Starlink and connect through any ground gateway still up. The call failed. "I'm guessing the cell towers near that address have lost power," he said. "They run on batteries when the grid goes. Four to eight hours, maybe."

He tried the other two numbers, same result.

Sam looked at John, purpose replacing the sadness that had been on his face since yesterday. "We have someone who needs our help pronto. We get the doctor in Phoenix and then get her up to Port Angeles." Sam got up. "Let me get my iPad and plan this out."

Sam put in PDK to PHX. "About six hours to Phoenix, then four and a half PHX to CLM. We'll be flying west, which will extend our daylight. Hang on, let me do the math. Say we get out of PDK by 12:00, figuring the drive is an hour or more and then we need to top off the plane. Hmm. Not going to work. That puts us in Port Angeles close to 9 p.m. Pacific, and we can't risk flying past sunset. Let's roll, John. We get out of PDK as soon as we can, and we can make Nashville for fuel."

John was already powering down the dev box and gathering everything he could think of, including his favorite picture of him and Naomi, and Naomi's Bible.

"There's a cooler in the garage. Will you grab it and bring it to the kitchen? It's DC powered. We'll bring my portable power station with us."

Sam grabbed his backpack and went to the garage for the cooler. John met him in the kitchen and packed it. "There are a couple of large mesh grocery bags in the pantry. We'll pack those as well."

Sam and John loaded John's Ridgeline and plugged the cooler into the power station. Before they left John went out to the side yard and turned the generator's controller from AUTO to OFF. The engine ran down and stopped, and the house behind him went dark. He locked the front door. They high-fived and were on the road in under forty minutes.

**09:20**

The wrecks were where they'd been yesterday. The fire on 400 had mostly burned out, and with nothing left to surprise him John could go faster. On 285 yesterday's smoke was gone, so it would be faster to take 285 to Peachtree Industrial. They drove slowly through a black patch in the road where the remnants of an aircraft tail smoldered on the westbound lanes. On the way John brought up the PC-12 that had arrived at the Pilatus dealer on Sunday, brand new. Maybe it was already topped off? Sam agreed they should check it out.

They talked about what they might encounter, the weather, getting fuel at other airports, the quickest way to find Dr. Dahl, what they should say.

Sam was already firming up the plan on his iPad and adding alternates.

**10:05**

John pulled around on Aviation Way to the Pilatus hangar. The big doors were shut and the walk-in door beside them was locked. Sam went back to the Epps shop for a pry bar and had it open in under a minute, the frame splitting at the strike plate. Inside it was dark and warm, the only light coming through the translucent panels high in the doors. The airplane sat in the middle of the floor, white with a gray stripe, N612PR on the tail.

The sales office was through a door at the back of the hangar. A delivery folder lay on the desk: a company in Charlotte, delivery Friday the eighth. Sam read the name on it and put the folder back where it had been. The keys were in the top drawer on a ring tagged with the tail number, and the document pouch and logbooks were in a file box beside them. Sam sat in the sales manager's chair with a headlamp and went through the logbooks page by page. Thirty-one point four hours total time, airframe and engine: acceptance flights at the factory in Switzerland, the ferry across the Atlantic, the completion center in Colorado, one leg to Atlanta. No open squawks. Every inspection signed off.

They climbed into the cockpit with the headlamps, Sam in the left seat. He looked at the panel for a long time without touching anything. "Honeywell I know," he said. "This I don't."

"I do." John put a finger on the glass of the nearest display. "My 182 has a G1000. This is its big brother. Same company, same logic. I'll know where the pages are." He reached up to the overhead panel and tapped a guarded button. "And there's this."

Sam read the placard. EMERGENCY AUTOLAND.

"If the pilot stops flying, it notices," John said. "Nobody touches the controls for long enough, it asks once, and then it takes the airplane. Or anybody in a passenger seat lifts the guard and pushes it. It picks the nearest runway long enough for the airplane, flies the approach, lands, brakes to a stop, and shuts down the engine. It needs GPS and a runway in its database. That's all it needs. If something happens to you, I push that."

Sam took the airplane's flight manual out of the pouch and opened it on his knee. "Give me fifteen minutes with the book." John checked the fuel while Sam skimmed: full tanks, as he'd guessed.

Sam read the manual the way he had at Edmonton, normal procedures front to back, then the limitations, then the emergency procedures. The engine was the same Pratt & Whitney he'd flown out of Edmonton, with the same electronic control behind the power lever. Most of what was new was the panel, and John sat in the right seat with the Garmin pilot's guide from the pouch and answered what Sam asked him. An hour and ten minutes later Sam closed the manual. "All right."

They pulled the hangar doors open. A battery tug sat on its charger in the corner, still near full. Sam towed the airplane out into the sun.

They moved the Jackery out of John's car and up through the cargo door, a two-man lift again, and strapped it to the floor rings forward of the aft bulkhead, the solar panels flat beside it. John's flight bag with the dev box in it went on the floor behind the right seat, where he could reach it. Then the packs, John's bags, the cooler, and the pantry bags with the dog food. Sam did the weight and balance on his iPad from the numbers in the airplane's own manual: two pilots, the cargo, a forty-five-pound dog on the cabin floor, full fuel. It came out inside the envelope with room to spare. He did it a second time anyway.

**10:45**

Dan settled on the cabin floor behind the cockpit. Sam ran the checklist aloud with John following on the card. The engine lit and spun up into a turbine whine. Without thinking, Sam keyed the mic. "Peachtree Tower, Pilatus Six One Two Papa Romeo at Epps, taxi for departure." Then he looked over at John with a wry grin. "Habit."

The windsock out past the hangars was lifting gently out of the north. Sam taxied out past C-GKPX at the edge of the ramp to runway 3 Right, still announcing on the tower frequency, lined up, and pushed the power lever forward, talking John through the numbers and what to watch on the engine instruments.
