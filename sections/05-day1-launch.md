# 5 — John, Day 1

John woke a little after six on the couch, stiff, the office door still open down the hall and the workstation's fans still turning over their steady work. Sam was already up — first light through the guest room curtains had apparently done what an alarm didn't need to — sitting at the kitchen counter with two mugs of coffee poured, one of them clearly meant for John, like the man had decided somewhere in the night that making himself useful in small ways was the only kind of useful currently on offer.

They went back to the office together. The machine had finished most of what it was going to finish; a dozen threads had spent the night reaching into county and regional dispatch systems across the country, filtering out the noise — the systems that never had the AI layer, the systems that had gone dark themselves in the last two days and taken their logs with them, call after call that was exactly what it looked like, somebody going quiet mid-sentence the same way the whole world had gone quiet. Out of something the model itself put, with heavy caveats, at somewhere over four thousand calls nationwide that matched the pattern, three had survived everything John threw at them.

A repeat caller in Huntsville, Alabama, who'd argued with the triage voice for almost six minutes straight before the line cut out mid-word. A woman in Phoenix, Arizona, who'd called back four separate times across two hours, each call shorter and more desperate than the last. And a single call from Portland, Oregon — oddly calm, giving map coordinates instead of a street address. Someone who knew exactly where she was and exactly how much trouble that was.

"No names," John said, reading it off the screen for Sam the way he'd have wanted it read to him. "A name on an account isn't the same thing as knowing a person, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Three addresses. Three mobile numbers that still belong to live accounts, as far as the model can tell."

He dialed Huntsville first, on a phone that had one bar and was losing even that by the hour.

Nothing. Not a ring, not a busy tone, not even the dead-air hum of a call going nowhere — a flat failure notice, the sound a call makes when there's no tower left standing anywhere on the far end to catch it. Phoenix gave him the identical failure, word for word.

He dialed Portland last, some stubborn part of him half-hoping distance alone might buy it a few more hours than the other two. It didn't work that way, and some other part of him had known that going in.

Same flat failure.

He set the phone down and neither of them said anything for a moment. "Nobody answering doesn't mean nobody's there," John said finally, working it out loud because saying it helped. "It means there's no network left standing in any of those three places to carry a call into. The collapse already got there before we did."

"So it was never really going to be a phone call," Sam said. Not a question.

"No. It's three points on a map that somebody has to go stand on. No clock I can read on any of them — could be a week, could already be gone." John looked at the notebook a long moment: three addresses, three dead numbers, a few lines of transcript under each one. A man who'd argued for six minutes. A woman who'd called back and back. Someone calm enough, hurt or not, to read out coordinates like she still believed a search team was coming. "That's the whole of what we have."

Sam didn't flinch from it, which John was starting to understand was simply how the man was built. "Then we go stand on them. Order?"

"Routing, not who can wait — I can't tell who can wait. Huntsville's almost a straight line west of here. Then Phoenix. Portland last, because it's the far corner of the map."

"Huntsville, Phoenix, Portland." Sam said it back the way John was already learning he confirmed things, not by arguing but by checking them against himself and finding no objection. "That's the order I'd have picked."

"You fly?" The question came a beat later, like it had only now occurred to Sam that he'd never actually asked. "I mean — you've got an airport out your back door and a house that looks like it's owned by someone who reads charts for fun."

"Private pilot. Instrument current — I fly a 182 out of PDK most months, plenty of it in actual." John felt strangely exposed saying it, the way you confess a hobby nobody's asked about. "Nothing with a turbine. Nothing anywhere near what you fly."

Something in Sam's whole posture eased. "You have any idea what that's worth right now? I've been the only pilot on this continent for a day and a half, as far as I know. You're not typed in anything I'd trust us to, not yet — but you can hold a heading and fly an approach and not kill us both in a cloud, and that part takes years. The airplane itself I can teach you. I'll check you out as we go — systems, numbers, real stick time in the right seat. Give it long enough and you'll be worth having up there for real, not just for company."

"I'd rather be useful than just be company," John said, and meant it more than he expected to.

They loaded what needed loading into John's car — the notebook, the duffel with Naomi's Bible in it, the workstation's power supply and the little battery station he kept for storms, though the workstation itself, a hundred-odd pounds of compute in a case built to sit still, would have to wait for a second lift at PDK — and drove the twenty minutes north with the sun still low and gold through the trees, the same still county rolling by that John had already spent a day and a half learning to expect.

PDK looked the way every field had looked since Tuesday — a scatter of parked airplanes, a dark terminal, a beacon that had stopped turning. Past the FBO, on the far side, a low building with a stylized mountain-and-crest logo and PILATUS SALES & SERVICE stenciled under the glass caught the morning light. Behind the showroom wall, under lights that had plainly never gone off, sat a PC-12 that still had the faint chemical smell of new paint coming off it from twenty feet away — full ACE avionics suite lit on standby, not a scratch anywhere on the airframe, the kind of airplane that existed to be looked at by somebody with the money to actually order one.

"Brand new," Sam said, something almost reverent in it. "Nobody's ever going to buy this now, and it's not doing anybody any good behind glass." He found the service door around the side, tried the handle out of habit, and moved to the small gray lockbox beside it when it held. It took him under a minute with a multitool from his flight bag — no bypass, no fight, nothing like what either of them had already been through for far less — and the box popped open to a single key fob on its hook, tagged with the tail number in a dealer's careful sales-floor hand.

"That's it? That easily?"

"Not everything gets to be hard," Sam said, and for the first time since the ramp at Hartsfield, he sounded like he almost believed it. The fuel gauges, checked from the cockpit a minute later, read full — a demo aircraft kept topped off and ready to impress a customer who was never coming, one more small mercy neither of them examined too closely.

They spent the day on it. Sam ran a full walkaround with the same unhurried thoroughness he'd have given a widebody, then took John through the panel three generations past the steam gauges in his 182 — systems, speeds, the numbers he'd need to know before he'd need them. In the middle of the afternoon they broke off to move the workstation, which turned out to be a two-man lift and then some: a hundred-odd pounds of compute wrestled out of John's car and up through the PC-12's cargo door, strapped down hard against the aft bulkhead, its power supply and the storm battery after it, the duffel with Naomi's Bible in it, the notebook. How they'd keep the thing fed once they left the last working outlet behind was a problem for a later hour; they left it there. Dan settled into the back like he'd flown a hundred times, which as far as John knew he had not.

They ate a late lunch standing up in the dead kitchen of the restaurant on the field — a low sandbagged building done up like a forward airbase, revetment walls and a Jeep out front, its walk-in still cold off whatever kept the terminal's emergency lights burning. John found bread that hadn't turned and made something out of nothing while Sam finished the preflight outside, and they ate it fast, standing at the pass, both of them already thinking about the sky.

"Huntsville's close," Sam said, spreading a chart across the hood of John's car once they were both back outside, working the numbers out loud the way John was learning he did everything that mattered. "Hundred and fifty miles, give or take. Half hour, thirty-five minutes in this thing. We launch in the next hour, we're on the ground there with real daylight left — hours of it. If that math came out different, we'd be having a conversation about waiting for morning."

"And if it had?"

"Then we'd sit until first light and go the second the sun came up, same as always. We don't launch into the thing we can already see coming. Only the ones we can't." Sam folded the chart and looked toward the airplane. "It didn't come out different. We go."

He ran the checklist with John following a beat behind, under the patient, exacting voice of a man who'd apparently decided teaching John to fly this airplane was every bit as urgent as flying it himself. The engine lit and spun up into a turbine whine John had only ever heard from a passenger seat, and the brand-new PC-12 rolled forward past its own showroom window, out toward a runway neither of them needed a tower to clear them for.

"Huntsville," Sam said, lining up with the low gold light of the evening laid out ahead of them. "Then Phoenix. Then Portland."

"Then the people," John said.

"Then the people." Sam pushed the power lever forward, and the ground fell away beneath two men who'd been total strangers twenty-nine hours before and were already, somehow, the crew they needed to be for what came next.
