# 5 — John, Day 1

John woke a little after six, the office door still open down the hall and the workstation's fans still turning over their steady work. Sam was already up — first light through the guest room curtains had apparently done what an alarm didn't need to — sitting at the kitchen counter with two mugs of coffee poured, one of them clearly meant for John, like the man had decided somewhere in the night that making himself useful in small ways was the only kind of useful currently on offer.

John showered, and made the same brain shake he made every morning of his life — blueberry, avocado, beet, spinach, a color that had earned more than one funny look over twenty years of making it in other people's kitchens. Some habits didn't ask what day it was, and doing this one anyway felt like the smallest act of defiance he had available to him. Sam watched him blend it with an expression John couldn't quite read. "Every day?" "Every day." "Huh." Whatever Sam thought about that, he kept it to himself and drank his coffee instead.

Dan was already at the door with his leash in his mouth by the time John reached for it, tail going hard enough to knock against the wall — two miles, same as every morning, rain or shine or the end of the world, apparently, and no one had explained the new rules to him yet. "You coming?" John asked, more out of courtesy than any real expectation. Sam was already reaching for his shoes.

They walked it together in the same low gold light John had watched come up over this street for sixteen years, Dan ranging out to the end of his leash and back the way he always did, and for the first quarter mile neither man said much — enough, for once, just to be moving through ordinary motion instead of talking about what came next. The neighborhood held the same wrongness John had stopped being surprised by: driveways still full, a lawn sprinkler ticking through its cycle on a timer nobody was left to reset, a porch light still burning gold against the morning like it hadn't gotten the news either. Sam took it in without comment, the way John imagined he'd have taken in a strange airport on a layover — cataloguing, filing it away, not reacting until something actually asked him to. At the corner where the Pattersons' kids used to catch the bus, he finally said something. "Elena used to walk Maya to one just like that. Two blocks from the house. I used to complain about getting up for it." He didn't say anything else, and John didn't ask him to.

Back at the house, they went into the office together. The machine had finished most of what it was going to finish; a dozen threads had spent the night reaching into county and regional dispatch systems across the country, filtering out the noise — the systems that never had the AI layer, the systems that had gone dark themselves in the last two days and taken their logs with them, call after call that was exactly what it looked like, somebody going quiet mid-sentence the same way the whole world had gone quiet. Out of something the model itself put, with heavy caveats, at somewhere over four thousand calls nationwide that matched the pattern, three had survived everything John threw at them.

A repeat caller in Huntsville, Alabama, who'd called 16 times. Another in Phoenix, Arizona, who'd called four times. And a single call coordinates on the Washington Olympic Penninsula.

"No names," John said, reading it off the screen for Sam the way he'd have wanted it read to him. "A name on an account isn't the same thing as knowing a person, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Three leads. Three mobile numbers that still belong to live accounts, as far as the model can tell."

He dialed Huntsville first, nothing. Not a ring, not a busy tone, not even the dead-air hum of a call going nowhere — a flat failure notice, the sound a call makes when there's no tower to forward his call.

He set the phone down and neither of them said anything for a moment. "Systems are failing, cell towers can operate for a while on backup, but looks like the closest to us is dead." John said finally, working it out loud because saying it helped. "It also means there may be no network left standing in any of those three places to carry a call into. The cascading power failure has started."

Sam didn't flinch from it, which John was starting to understand was simply how the man was built. 

"Huntsville, Phoenix, Portland." Sam said.

They loaded what needed loading into John's car — the workstation's power supply and the little battery station he kept for storms, though the workstation itself, a hundred-odd pounds of compute in a case built to sit still — it took 40 minutes south with the sun still low and gold through the trees, everything the same as yesterday.

PDK looked the way every field had looked since Tuesday — a scatter of parked airplanes, a dark terminal, a beacon that had stopped turning. They found what they were looking for behind the training center John had been to hundreds of times over the years. In the hangar opposite EPPS sat a PC-12 that still had the faint chemical smell of new paint coming off it from twenty feet away — not a scratch anywhere on the airframe, a 6 or 7 million dollar airplane.

"Brand new," Sam said, something almost reverent in it. "Do you think they will be ok if we borrow it?" He found the service door around the side, tried the handle out of habit, and moved to the small gray lockbox beside it, the box popped open to a single key fob on its hook, tagged with the tail number in a dealer's careful sales-floor hand.

"That's it? That easily?", said John

"Guess they say its ok." Sam said with a smile, and for the first time since the ramp at Hartsfield, he laughed. 

Sam ran a full walkaround with the same unhurried thoroughness he'd have given the Airbus, then took John through the panel three generations past the gauges in his 182 — systems, speeds, the numbers he'd need to know before he'd need them. Sam had flew an earlier version version of the PC-12 on night flights for a medical test company.

They moved the workstation, which turned out to be a two-man lift: a hundred-odd pounds of compute wrestled out of John's car and up through the PC-12's cargo door, strapped down hard against the aft bulkhead, its power supply and the storm battery after it. 

John made burgers for lunch in the kitchen of the restaurant perched on the second floor above the EPPS hangar. Its walk-in still cold in a patch of grid still working. John made some sandwiches for later. Sam finished the preflight outside.

Sam came up the steps to the resturant, "Huntsville's close," he said, spreading a chart across the table eating his burger, working the numbers out loud the way John was learning he did everything that mattered. "Hundred and fifty miles, give or take. Half hour, thirty-five minutes in this thing. Wow, that's a good burger, thanks John." They packed up the extra food and headed to the plane.

Dan settled in on the floor directly behind the cockpit. Sam ran the checklist with John following a beat behind, under the patient, exacting voice of a man who'd apparently decided teaching John to fly this airplane was every bit as urgent as flying it himself. The engine lit and spun up into a turbine whine John had only ever heard from a passenger seat, and the brand-new PC-12 rolled forward past its own showroom window, out toward a runway neither of them needed a tower to clear them for. Without thinking Sam queued the radio mic, "Peachtree Tower, November Echo 538 at Pilatus", then he looked over at John with a wry grin, "Habit."

Sam taxied out and checked the wind sock, gentle out of the north, he turned and taxied toward runway 3 Right. Sam still checked traffic and announced departure on the tower frequency, lined up on the runway toward the north and pushed the power lever forward, the ground fell away beneath two men who'd been total strangers twenty-nine hours before and were already, somehow, the right crew for what was coming.
