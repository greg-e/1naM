# 5 — John, Day 1

John woke a little after six, Sam was already up — sitting at the kitchen counter with two mugs of coffee poured, one of them clearly meant for John.

John showered, and made the same brain shake he made every morning of his life — blueberry, avocado, beet, spinach. Some habits didn't ask what day it was, and doing this one felt like the smallest act of defiance he had available to him. Sam watched him blend it with an expression John couldn't quite read. "Every day?" "Every day." "Huh." Whatever Sam thought about that, he kept it to himself and declined Johns offer to share.

Dan was already at the door with his leash in his mouth by the time John reached for it, tail going hard enough to knock a hole in the wall — two miles, same as every morning, rain or shine or the end of the world, apparently, and no one had explained the new rules to him yet. "You coming?" John asked, more out of courtesy than any real expectation. Sam was already reaching for his shoes.

They walked together in the same low gold light John had watched come up over this street for sixteen years, Dan ranging out to the end of his leash and back the way he always did, and for the first quarter mile neither man said much — enough, for once, just to be moving through ordinary motion instead of talking about what came next. The neighborhood held the same wrongness John had stopped being surprised by: driveways still full, a lawn sprinkler ticking through its cycle on a timer nobody was left to reset, a porch light still burning gold against the morning like it hadn't gotten the news either. Sam was quiet.

Back at the house, they went into the office together. The machine had finished; the agents had spent the night reaching into county and regional dispatch systems across the country, filtering out the noise — the systems that never had the AI layer, the systems that had gone dark themselves in the last two days and taken their logs with them. Out of something the model itself put, with heavy caveats, at somewhere over four thousand calls nationwide that matched the pattern, three had survived all the parameters John had established.

A repeat caller in Huntsville, Alabama, who'd called 16 times. Another in Phoenix, Arizona, who'd called four times. And a single call, coordinates somewhere on the Olymic peninsula.

"No names," John said, reading it off the screen. "Three locations, three phone numbers that still belong to live accounts, as far as the model can tell."

He dialed Huntsville first, nothing. Not a ring, not a busy tone, not even the dead-air hum of a call going nowhere — a flat failure notice, the sound a call makes when there's no tower to forward his call.

He set the phone down and neither of them said anything for a moment. "Systems are failing, cell towers can operate for a while on backup, but looks like the closest to us is dead." John said finally, working it out loud because saying it helped. "It also means there may be no network left standing in any of those three places to carry a call into. The cascading power failure has started."

Sam didn't flinch from it, which John was starting to understand was simply how the man was built. 

"Huntsville, Phoenix, Portland." Sam said.

They loaded what needed loading into John's car — the workstation's power supply and the little battery station he kept for storms, the workstation itself a hundred-odd pounds of compute in a case built to sit still. The drive took 40 minutes south with the sun still low and gold through the trees, everything the same as yesterday.

John had a last-minute thought as they pulled out of the neighborhood — satellite phones. Cell towers were failing one by one, and if the three leads they'd pulled out of the 911 logs went dark on him the way Huntsville already had, plain phones weren't going to be worth carrying past today. "There's an REI on the way," he said, already changing lanes. "Perimeter Mall, ten minutes off our route. If anybody stocks the real thing, it's them."

The parking lot was as empty and undisturbed as every other lot they'd passed that morning, the automatic doors dead easily pryed open. Inside, the lights were still on, motion-sensor fixtures flickering awake overhead as they walked the aisles, the air conditioning still running cold and antiseptic over gear that no longer had anybody left to sell it. John found the electronics wall himself, past the tents and the kayaks — Garmin inReach Minis hanging in rows, two-way satellite messengers, no voice, but a text channel and an SOS button bounced off a constellation that didn't answer to a cell tower or a power grid, one he'd spent enough of his career staring at on a screen to trust more than he trusted most people right now. He pulled four off their pegs, then a fifth after a second's thought, and a fistful of the little solar chargers racked beside them.

Sam came back from the other side of the store with an armful that told John exactly how the man's mind worked once he stopped being a pilot for a minute and started being someone thinking two weeks out instead of two hours — headlamps, a water filter, a first-aid kit heavier than anything most people kept in a car, a folding knife, thermal layers in both their sizes. "Figured you're not the type to think about socks until your feet are already wet," he said, dropping it all on a checkout counter that wasn't going to ring anything up. John didn't argue. He added his own armful to the pile, and between them they filled two hiking packs neither of them had planned on needing an hour before, and carried it all back out to the car, the store's doors left standing open.

Dekalb Peachtree Airport was completely deserted, like a ghost town abandoned for 10 years. They found what they were looking for behind the training center John had been to hundreds of times over the years. In the hangar opposite EPPS sat a PC-12.

"Brand new," Sam said, "6 or 7 million, do you think they will be ok if we borrow it?" He found the office door around the side, tried the handle out of habit, and moved to the small gray lockbox beside it, the box popped open to a single key fob on its hook, tagged with the tail number NE 538.

"That's it? That easily?", said John

"Guess they say its ok." Sam said with a smile, and for the first time since the ramp at Hartsfield, he laughed. 

Sam ran a full walkaround with the same unhurried thoroughness he'd have given the Airbus, then took John through the panel three generations past the gauges in his 182 — systems, speeds, the numbers he'd need to start memorizing. Sam had flown an earlier version of the PC-12 on night flights for a medical supply company right out of college before enlisting in the airforce, he's logged 800 hours in the type.

They moved the workstation, which turned out to be a two-man lift: a hundred-odd pounds of compute wrestled out of John's car and up through the PC-12's cargo door, strapped down hard against the aft bulkhead, its power supply and the storm battery after it. 

John made burgers for lunch in the kitchen of the restaurant perched on the second floor above the EPPS hangar, that still had power.  Sam finished the preflight outside.

Sam came up the steps and joined John at a table in the restaurant, "Huntsville's close," he said, spreading a chart across the table eating his burger, working the numbers out loud the way John was learning he did everything that mattered. "Hundred and fifty miles, give or take. Half hour, thirty-five minutes in this thing. Wow, that's a good burger, thanks, John." Dan looked up at John, "Hey buddy, did you like it too?" John said. John made some sandwiches for later and they packed up and headed down to the plane.

Dan settled in on the floor directly behind the cockpit. Sam ran the checklist with John following along, under the patient, exacting voice of a man who'd apparently decided teaching John to fly this airplane was every bit as urgent as flying it himself. The engine lit and spun up into a turbine whine John had only ever heard from a passenger seat, and the brand-new PC-12 rolled forward past its own showroom window, out toward a runway neither of them needed a tower to clear them for. Without thinking Sam keyed the radio mic, "Peachtree Tower, November Echo 538 at Pilatus", then he looked over at John with a wry grin, "Habit."

Sam taxied out and checked the wind sock, gentle out of the north, he turned and taxied toward runway 3 Right. Sam still checked traffic and announced departure on the tower frequency, lined up on the runway toward the north and pushed the power lever forward, the ground fell away beneath two men who'd been total strangers twenty-nine hours before and were already, somehow, the right crew for what was coming.
