# 1 — John, Day 0

The sound woke him at 2:05, a distant, percussive thud that rattled the window in its frame and was gone before he'd fully surfaced. He lay there a minute in the dark, waiting for it to resolve into something — a siren, a second explosion, anything that would tell him what he'd just heard. Nothing came. No fire trucks, no police, no distant alarm working itself up the way alarms usually did in a neighborhood like his. Just the ordinary hum of the house around him, the dog's tags shifting faintly somewhere down the hall as Dan resettled himself. He decided it was a transformer, or somebody's car doing something stupid out on Old Milton, and let the dark pull him back under.

He woke again at six, same as every morning, no different from twenty thousand mornings before it — fifty-eight years old, alone in the house except for the dog, which was exactly how he'd arranged his life and exactly how he liked it. He didn't check his phone right away — coffee before screens, an old habit that had survived every other change in his life — and took Dan out for the walk that started his day the same way it always did, leash in one hand, travel mug in the other, the big dog's tail already going before they'd cleared the driveway.

The neighborhood was quiet in a way he registered before he could name it. More cars sitting in more driveways than usual, as if half the street had decided to work from home. No sign of the Pattersons' kids at the corner where the bus usually idled — no kids at all, actually, not on his stretch, not on the cross street either. He walked further than he meant to, some low-grade curiosity pulling him along past his usual turnaround, and found the same stillness holding all the way to the elementary school two streets over. Empty lot. No bus. No parents in bathrobes standing at the end of driveways with travel mugs of their own.

*Must be a holiday,* he thought, then caught himself — Labor Day had been yesterday, and there wasn't another one due for months. He ran through the calendar in his head anyway and came up just as empty the second time. That bothered him more than it should have, a small itch he carried the rest of the walk home.

Back in the kitchen, Dan settled into his bed with a grunt, and John pulled up the news on his phone mostly to solve the holiday question, nothing more urgent than that. The headline feed hadn't moved. He scrolled, frowning, checked the timestamp on the top story — nine hours old, no correction, no update layered over it the way a real morning's news always accumulated by six a.m. He switched to a second app. Same frozen feed. A third. The anchor thumbnail on the local station's live page showed a paused frame, some mid-sentence expression locked in place like a screenshot standing in for a broadcast that wasn't happening.

He moved to his laptop and widened the search, methodical the way thirty years of engineering had trained him to be — gather everything observable before you let yourself form a theory — checking source after source, national feeds, international ones, social platforms that should have been a chaotic scroll of overnight activity and instead sat still, timestamps clustering in the hours before whatever this was. Nothing anywhere had moved since sometime around two in the morning.

He sat with that for a second, and then noticed the thing underneath it, the thing that had actually been bothering him the whole walk without surfacing all the way — he hadn't heard a single aircraft. Not one. He lived under enough of a flight corridor that some low hum of traffic was almost always present if you knew to listen for it, and he did know, had trained himself to for years, ever since he'd started flying himself. This morning there'd been nothing. Not distant, not close. Nothing at all.

He opened FlightAware without entirely deciding to, some instinct ahead of his conscious reasoning, and the map that loaded in stopped him cold. Empty airspace over the entire country. Empty almost everywhere, the whole global map reduced to a scatter of maybe fifty aircraft, most of them long, slow arcs across oceans — flights that had left far enough out that whatever this was hadn't caught up to them yet.

One of those tracks was doing something the others weren't. A Delta flight out of Atlanta, well out over the North Atlantic, its track line already bent north and west of its filed route toward Frankfurt, and its position marker — live, not historical, actually crawling as he watched — circling low and slow over an airport on the Newfoundland coast, doing something that looked, even to eyes that hadn't read an altimeter in real anger for years, less like approach and more like someone checking the field before committing to it. He refreshed the page once, twice, watched the little aircraft icon keep moving, keep circling. St. John's, the label read, once he zoomed in far enough to catch the airport code. He didn't know yet what any of this meant — didn't have the context to feel relief exactly, not this early — but it snagged at him regardless, one clean, specific fact in a morning that had otherwise given him nothing but absence. *Somebody up there is making a decision right now. Whatever this is, it's not nothing.*

He filed it away — a fact worth holding onto, not yet a thing to act on. There was too much else in front of him still making no sense, and the methodical part of him, the part that never trusted a single data point when more were available, wanted to keep gathering before he did anything else.

On a hunch, he pulled up the local DOT traffic camera feeds next, the ones he sometimes checked before a bad commute — and this was where the wrongness stopped being ambiguous. By quarter past seven on a weekday, GA-400 and the Perimeter should have already been thick with the leading edge of rush hour, brake lights strung out for miles the way they always were. Instead the main arteries sat nearly empty, a handful of vehicles scattered across lanes that should have been packed bumper to bumper — and several of those vehicles weren't moving at all. One camera near a Perimeter interchange showed what looked like three or four cars clustered strangely close together mid-lane, doors closed, nothing about the image explaining itself at that resolution. Another, closer to his own exit, caught a stopped truck straddling two lanes, no flashers, no movement anywhere in frame, traffic that should have been backing up behind it simply... not there to back up. He sat very still in his kitchen chair, doing the math his own commute had taught him a thousand times over — this was not a light morning. This was a road that should have been full, and wasn't, except for the vehicles that weren't going anywhere at all.

He didn't go out yet. Some careful, methodical part of him wanted to exhaust every remote way of checking before he committed to anything.

At nine o'clock, his first scheduled call of the day came due — a standing internal sync at Anthropic he'd been on a hundred times before, the kind of meeting that started within thirty seconds of the hour whether anyone wanted it to or not. He logged in and watched the participant grid fill with names and no faces, camera icons crossed out one after another failing to connect, the meeting timer running past five minutes, then ten, with no one else ever joining. He sat there staring at his own video tile, alone in a call meant for six people, and felt something in the shape of the morning finally, fully tip over.

He started calling people after that — not systematically, just working down the list of everyone who mattered. His brother Mark first, the only family he had left worth calling, three time zones and a decade of drifted-apart holidays notwithstanding. Then a short list of friends. Then coworkers he barely knew but tried anyway, some instinct insisting that surely, surely one of them would pick up. Every call rang through to nothing. No voicemail greeting, no busy signal, just an open line ringing into a silence that never picked up, over and over, until he'd gone through nearly everyone he could think to call and the silence had started to feel less like bad luck and more like an answer.

He grabbed his keys.

The main road, two turns from his house, should have been busy by now — it always was on a weekday morning, a steady creep of commuters working toward GA-400. Instead it held only a scatter of vehicles, stopped at odd, wrong angles across a road built for a hundred times that many. A sedan sat nose-first in the guardrail on the curve everyone took too fast, engine still running, headlights still on, throwing a useless cone of light across the grass in full daylight. Further up, cars sat stalled in both lanes, some drifted onto the shoulder, most still idling, radios or dashboard displays still glowing — the absence of everyone else was, somehow, almost worse than the wrecks themselves.

He pulled over at the first one and called 911 before he'd even gotten out of his own car, phone pressed to his ear, eyes on the driver slumped against the wheel of the sedan ahead of him. It picked up on the second ring — not a person, a calm, synthetic voice already mid-sentence about call volume and estimated response times, running through a script that had clearly never been built to handle whatever had actually happened to the dispatch center behind it. He tried to explain: a man, unresponsive, not breathing, right here, right now. The voice thanked him for the information, assured him a unit would be dispatched as priority allowed, and looped back to the beginning of the same script when he tried to push past it for a real answer. He said "operator" twice, then "human," then just stopped talking and let it run out the silence on its own, staring at the phone like it might explain itself if he waited long enough. It didn't.

He got out anyway, walked up to the sedan on legs that didn't feel entirely his, and made himself do the thing every part of him wanted to avoid — reached in through the open window and checked, two fingers against the side of the driver's neck the way he vaguely remembered from some long-ago first aid course. Nothing. No pulse, no breath moving the chest, skin already cool in a way that told him this hadn't just happened in the last few minutes. He stood there a moment with his hand still half-raised, the confirmation landing heavier than the sight of the body alone had. He didn't get much closer to the others after that. Didn't need to — the first one had told him everything the rest would only have repeated.

He drove home slower than he'd driven out, hands tight on the wheel, panic sitting cold and constant in his chest now in a way it hadn't back at the kitchen table, no matter how bad the traffic cameras had looked. Dan met him at the door, tail going, entirely unaware that anything in the world had changed, and John crouched down and put both hands into the dog's fur for a long moment before he made himself stand back up.
