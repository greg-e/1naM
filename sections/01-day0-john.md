# 9/5/2028 — John Lauer, AI Engineer & Researcher, Day 0, 2:05am

The sound woke him at 2:05 a.m., a distant thud that rattled the window in its frame and was gone before he'd fully woken up. He lay there a minute in the dark, waiting for something — a siren, a repeat, anything that would tell him what he'd just heard. Nothing came. No sirens, no distant alarm. Just the ordinary sounds of the house around him, Dan, his dog, tags shifting faintly somewhere down the hall as he resettled himself. He decided it was a transformer, or somebody's car doing something stupid out on Old Milton Parkway, rolled over and faded back to sleep.

He woke again at six, same as every morning: fifty-eight years old, alone the last 16 years, well not alone, there was always a dog, not just any dog, always a Mountain Cur. 

Morning routine... to the bathroom, hot shower, then down to what he liked to think of as first breakfast — the same every day, a blueberry-avocado-beet-spinach concoction he called a brain shake. Early dawn light shone through the kitchen window; the days were getting shorter and real light didn't come until 7:15. Necessary but always enjoyable was getting Dan out for their 2 mile morning walk. He intentionally tried to not think about work, and just enjoy Dan and the fresh air and first light. The dog's tail was already going dangerously fast back and forth before they'd cleared the driveway. Happy dog, happy day.

The neighborhood was quiet, more cars in driveways than a normal Tuesday — half the street working from home, maybe. Yesterday had been Labor Day, things were usually a bit more busy, a short week compressed schedules. At the corner where the Pattersons' kids caught the 7:15 bus like clockwork, there were no kids and no bus, he double checked his watch, it was just past 7:20. He kept going, past his usual turnaround, and the stillness held all the way to where his subdivision met Old Milton Parkway. Old Milton Parkway hadn't been genuinely quiet at any hour in years, since it was expanded to a four lane divided highway. So quiet, John was always aware of airplanes flying over, his morning walk with Dan was always highlighted by the expected hum of a propeller or the sound of a biz jet, his house was on the approach to Peachtree Dekalb airport and off to the east there always seemed to be a commercial airline headed toward Atlanta Hartsfield Airport. This morning, nothing, quiet below and above, he had a eerie remembrance to a day 27 years ago when the skies had been cleared because of the attack by islamic terrorists.

"Hm," he said, out loud. Then, "Ummm" — the flat working hum he made when the inputs on a problem hadn't added up to anything yet, like the processing circle that annoyed him on the screens he looked at everyday. He walked out to the edge of Old Milton Parkway. In both directions something was wrong. To the north, a hundred yards off on the blind curve, a car sat nose-down in the drainage ditch, lights on, and he could just make out its engine running high and uneven. To the south a box truck had stopped square in the median and blocked the rest of the view that way. He went north first — a car in a ditch with the engine still going, no emergency vehicles, no sirens, must have just happened. It took him a few minutes. The closer he got the clearer it was: the engine pinned at a fast idle, the rear wheels spinning free in the air above the ditch, turning and turning against nothing. He had Dan sit and wait a few yards back. He reached the door and pulled it open.

A man, sixty-odd, sitting almost upright, held there by the shoulder belt. One hand had slid off the wheel into his lap. No blood, no broken glass — nothing about him matched a car in a ditch. Dan, ears flat, a thin whine building in his chest.

John leaned across the man's knees, found the ignition, and shut it off. The quiet that came in behind it was total. He pressed two fingers to the side of the man's neck. His hand was steady — steadier than the rest of him had any right to be. Forty years back he'd worked the overnight grill at a Waffle House off North Avenue to get himself through Tech, and the one thing that job had left him with was this: panic and competence could run at the same time, the hands finishing the work while the rest of a man came apart. No pulse, no rise in the chest, the skin cool in a way that meant hours, not minutes — this had happened a few hours ago, not a few minutes ago. Around the time, some back part of his mind noted, of the thud that woke him.

He backed off from the car and called 911, Dan pressed against his leg.

It answered on the second ring, and it wasn't a person — a calm synthetic voice already mid-sentence about elevated call volume and estimated response times. He knew the cadence of it he had been on the periphery of decisions about how 911 centers were implementing AI agents to triage the queue when the human dispatchers were on other calls. He had sat in the rooms where deployments like this one were argued line by line. He gave the synthetic voice a flat / clean explanation — a man, deceased, alone, in a vehicle off Old Milton Parkway just north of GA Highway 400. The voice thanked him and said a unit would be dispatched as priority allowed. He asked for a dispatcher. It repeated the line about call volume. He asked again — an operator, then - how about a person, in the back of his mind the thought that AI really is going too far — and it looped cleanly back to the top of its script and started over.

He went back south and crossed to the box truck in the median — a white contractor's rig, nose against the cable barrier. The driver was slumped the same way the first man had been, belt on, no mark on him. John opened the door and checked to be sure what his thoughts were already telling him... dead. "Ummm," he said, to no one. His chest tightened and he began to feel light headed.

He kept walking. South, toward the next bend over the hill — some part of him still insisting there'd be a version of this morning that made sense a little further on. A road crew. A cop waving traffic around a wreck. Dan pulled back against the leash the whole way and John kept coaxing him on.

Old Milton Parkway passed under GA Highway 400. An eighteen-wheeler had jackknifed across the southbound lanes and folded against the bridge rail. The tractor had gone through the rail and hung nose-down off the edge, out over the road below, held by nothing he could see. Every car on 400 he could make out was stopped — no brake lights. On an ordinary Tuesday at this hour this highway moved more people past this one spot in a minute than lived on his whole street.

He stood there until Dan leaned hard into his leg and whined, John turned and walked home fast, leash short, not quite running. 

Back at his house, Dan curled up under the kitchen table. John pulled up the news on his phone. The headline feed hadn't moved. He checked the timestamp on the top story: nine hours old, no correction stacked on it, no update layered over it the way a real morning's coverage always thickened by six. He switched apps. The same frozen feed. A third. The local station's live page held a single paused frame, an anchor caught mid-word, standing in for a broadcast that wasn't happening.

He moved to the laptop. The same frozen feed, wider: national wires, overseas outlets, the social platforms that should have been a roar of overnight noise and instead sat still, every timestamp clustered in the hours before 2:00am. Nothing anywhere had moved since. He didn't try yet to make it mean anything — just clocked it, the way you clock a gauge reading you don't like, and kept looking.

He opened FlightAware a flight tracking app. He knew what that map was supposed to look like at eight on a weekday. The airspace over the country was empty. The whole global map was down to a scatter of maybe fifty aircraft, most of them long shallow arcs over open ocean. A few of the arcs were wrong in a way he didn't quite understand. He clicked into one: a FedEx 767, Memphis to Jacksonville, a fifty-minute run, now four hundred miles out over the Atlantic and still going — well past its destination. He checked another, the same, routes that would never be flown like this.

He pulled up a handful of the state DOT traffic cameras — the interchange where 400 met the Perimeter, then a few farther out in other cities. Every one showed the same thing he'd walked through that morning: open lanes at an hour that should have been solid rush hour, a scatter of stopped vehicles across them, nothing moving, nothing burning. He didn't work it any further than that. It was enough to tell him the shape of the thing; how far it reached and how far down could wait until he had somewhere steadier to stand than a kitchen chair.

He logged in to his 8:30am online standing sync meeting — his group at Google, the public-sector team he ran engagements for out of the D.C. office. The meeting time ran past five minutes, then ten, and no one else joined. He sat looking at his own video tile — pale, jaw set, mouth thin. John left the meeting.

He started calling people — his brother Mark first, then down the mental list of everyone he talked to in an ordinary week. Voicemail mostly; he left a few messages. Some numbers just rang and rang, no busy signal anywhere. He got a dozen names deep and the silence had already started to feel like the answer.

He set the phone down. The house was very quiet. Dan lifted his head off the floor and watched him, and John scooted back from the table and Dan stood and John held Dan's head in his hands rubbing his ears, "What are we going to do dog?". 

"Harry" he said outloud. He left Dan in the house and went next door, Harry had been a friend for 20 years... not just that guy over the fence always giving advice about the lawn, a real friend who had helped John through many tough times.  Rang the door bell, waited a minute, nothing... knocked... nothing.  Tried the door and it was open, stepping in "Hello? Harry? are you here?" nothing. Walking through the living room to the kitchen, no Harry... down the hall to the master bedroom, "Harry?" There he was in bed, check pulse, just like the others... gone.

He stood a minute in the doorway of a dead man's bedroom, the house around him exactly as tidy as Harry always kept it — a coffee mug on the nightstand from the morning before, the last inch in it gone cold and skinned over. There was nothing left to check. He pulled the blanket up over Harry's shoulder, a small useless thing, the same small useless thing he hadn't been able to reach for in the car on Old Milton Parkway, and let himself back out through the kitchen and across the strip of grass between the two driveways.

Dan met him at his own door like it had been a week. John didn't sit down this time. He went straight to the laptop, and this time he didn't skim it.

He pulled FlightAware back up, wider than he'd looked at eight, studing patterns and checking each aircraft information to see orgination and destination. The map was still down to a scatter, most of it the same long-haul arcs over open ocean he'd already half-noticed — each icon still crawling forward in real time holding a heading that never varied, never corrected, never slowed. He was ready to write the whole screen off the same way when one track stopped him.

An airplane doing something different. He clicked into it. **DAL27**, an Airbus 350-900 out of Atlanta, filed for Seoul, and somewhere over Hudson Bay four hours into that routing it had turned around. A hard bank off the great-circle track and a new heading laid out clean and deliberate, arrowing back south across Canada.

FlightAware pulled from a patchwork of ADS-B receivers and airline feeds stitched together for a phone screen, and John had spent enough of his career picking apart exactly that kind of pipeline to know it could just as easily be showing him a caching bug as a real airplane. He opened a chat window on the laptop instead — not the workstation down the hall, just the model itself, the same access his job gave him into things most people couldn't touch — and asked it to pull the track independently against the government's own flight-tracking data, the FAA feed everything else downstream was only ever a copy of. It came back in a few seconds: same tail number, same squawk, same turn, timestamped against a source that had no reason on earth to agree with FlightAware unless the airplane really was doing what FlightAware said it was doing. Not a fluke. Not stale. Real.

He did the math without meaning to: departed a little after eleven the night before, turned back sometime around three thirty, five, six hours of flying left to cover the distance home. If they were coming back to Atlanta it would be here within two hours.

He grabbed Dan's leash off the hook and called him to the car and had the engine running before he'd fully decided the drive made any more sense than the phone calls had. It did. It was the first thing all morning that felt like it might.

Back out on Old Milton Parkway south toward highway 400 where the 18 wheeler was still there hanging off the bridge, still nothing that made sense, nothing had changed since he first laid eyes on this scene... thinking of that John drove under 400 carefully avoiding the dangling tractor and headed a mile further south to the local firehouse. A ladder truck and two Paramedic Vans were parked in the garage, doors open, John walked into the building... "Hello? anyone here?" silence, a flat non-echo dense silence. He found two uniformed firefighters on the floor in the kitchen, both cold and no pulse. Upstairs there were eight more all in their bunks, he checked each to be sure, all gone and cold. Shivers went up John's back.  "Ok, stay on task, I have something to do, let's go, John." the sound of his voice flat against the immense silence of the room.  

In the car onto GA Highway 400... headed south.  It was slow going, most of the cars were off to the side either run into the median wires or off the right in the ditch or all the way into the trees.  He stopped to check a couple and tried 911 again with the same results as earlier. He realized the gas tank was almost empty... exit 8 had a QuickTrip a few blocks from 400. 

He filled his car like normal, the pump took his credit card and turned on.  Realizing he could use something to drink and some more coffee would be good, he went into the store.

The lights were on, the coolers still humming along the back wall, country music playing low over the speakers. He poured a coffee without really thinking about it, cream, no sugar, the same as always. He grabbed a couple water bottles. "Hello? Anybody here?" Nothing.

A sound behind him made him jump out of his skin — a flat, cheerful voice from the side aisle: "Caution. Cleaning in progress." He whipped around. A floor-scrubbing robot, disc-shaped, knee-high, was working its way up the aisle on whatever schedule somebody had set it to once and never touched again, brushes turning, a thin sheet of water and cleaner sheeting out ahead of it. It said the line again, unhurried, unbothered, aimed at absolutely nobody, nosed into the base of a shelf, backed off two inches, corrected itself, and kept going. John stood there with his heart still going too fast for a talking vacuum, watching the single most normal thing he'd seen since six that morning — a machine doing exactly the job it was built for, competently, indifferently, on schedule — the same as the airplanes he'd just been watching hold their headings out over open ocean, the same as whatever was flying that Airbus south right now.

He found her on the floor behind the counter, wedged between the register and a rack of cigarettes. Twenties, maybe younger, a QuickTrip polo a size too big for her. He checked anyway. Cold, same as everyone else that morning had been cold. Nothing on her told him a name, and he didn't go looking for one. He said something quiet to her anyway, the way he'd started doing without deciding to, some scrap of decency that didn't cost him anything and didn't help her either.

Back in the car, back on 400, the coffee going cold in the cupholder before he'd taken a second sip. Every stalled car he worked around now was a name he didn't have and wasn't going to get — the firehouse, the girl on the floor, the man in the ditch, Harry, all of it stacking up behind him the same way the traffic wasn't. He made himself stop counting. One thing at a time. He double-checked FlightAware... DAL27 still on a course for Atlanta, over Kentucky now, he didn't have much time.
