# 9/5/2028 — John, AI Engineer & Researcher, Day 0

A thud woke him. Distant and heavy, enough to rattle the bedroom window in its frame, and gone before he was fully awake. The clock read 2:05. He lay in the dark and waited: a siren, a second thud, anything that would tell him what he'd heard. Nothing came. The regular sounds of the house, and down the hall tags shifted as the dog resettled. John rolled over and went back to sleep.

He woke again at six thirty, same as every morning. Sixty-one years old and alone for sixteen of them, though not really alone. There had always been a dog, currently a mountain cur named Dan.

A hot shower, then what he called first breakfast, a blueberry, avocado, beet juice, and spinach smoothie, well it wasn't exatly smooth, but it was efficient. Gray light came in the kitchen window, the days were getting shorter which would start to  cut his walks with Dan short. He tried not to think about work on the walk, just the dog and the fresh air and first light. 

**7:15**
At 7:15 they headed out, Dan's tail was swinging back and forth dangerously as they walked down the driveway.

The neighborhood was quiet. Yesterday had been labor day, a short week usually had everyone out early. Instead cars that were gone at this time on a work day were still parked in driveway's like it was a Sunday morning. At the corner where the Pattersons' kids caught the school bus at 7:20, there were no kids and no bus. 

**7:25**
He checked his watch: 7:25. The quietness made him curious and he pushed past his usual turnaround, continuing to where his subdivision met Old Milton Parkway. Old Milton hadn't been quiet at any hour in years. He stood on the sidewalk at his subdivision entrance and listened, a hum, maybe, but mostly a silence that was eerie and a bit spooky, a chill ran up his spine.

"Hmm," he said out loud, his default response when the inputs on a problem didn't add up. To the north, a hundred yards away, a car sat nose-down in the drainage ditch with its lights on and he could just make out that the only sounds he was aware of were in that direction. It must have just happened. He jogged up the hill. The front of the car had gone down into a storm culvert that lifted the rear wheels off the ground and they were spinning freely. He put Dan in a sit a few yards back, went to the driver's door, and pulled it open.

A man about forty sat nearly upright, held there by the shoulder belt, his eyes open, his foot still pressing the gas pedal. Behind John, Dan's ears went flat and a thin whine started in his chest.

John reached across the man's knees, found the start button, and shut the engine off. The resulting silence was overwhelming. He checked for a pulse on the man's neck, skin was cool, almost cold, no heartbeat. This had happened hours ago, not minutes. The man's left hand hung down beside the seat, and the fingers had gone a flushed pink, like a man come in out of the cold. Not quite what John expected.

He backed away from the car and called 911 with Dan pressed against his leg.

**7:40**
It answered on the first ring, and for a second he took it for a person: a woman's voice, warm and unhurried, with a small breath before the question. "Nine-one-one, what's the address of your emergency?" It was the even, steady voice of the ubiquitious AI agent that had taken over most phone systems.

John: "Old Milton Parkway roughly one mile north of Highway 400."

"What is the emergency?" 

John: "A car accident, one car involved, the driver is dead."

"Is the driver breathing?."

John: "No." 

"Are you safe where are?"

John: "Yes."

"I'm so sorry," it said. "That's a hard thing to walk up on." 

John felt the small, stupid pull of being understood by it. Call volume was higher than usual, it told him, and a unit would be dispatched as priority allowed. Would he stay near the vehicle?

He asked for a person. It said it was an AI assistant handling the queue while the dispatchers were on other calls, that everything had been logged and that it would stay on the line as long as he liked. John hung up.

He walked south toward 400, around the bend, expecting a version of this morning that made sense. A road crew. A cop waving traffic around a wreck. The silence made him very aware of his own sounds: his boots on the sidewalk, Dan's tags clinking together.

Old Milton passed under 400. An eighteen-wheeler had jackknifed across the southbound lanes and folded against the bridge rail. The tractor had gone through the rail and hung nose-down over the road below. Nothing moving, on an ordinary Tuesday at this hour, this stretch of highway moved more people past this one spot in a minute than lived on his whole subdivision. Farther south, a column of black smoke rose into the sky.

**7:55**
He pulled up the news on his phone. The headline feed hadn't moved. The top story was six hours old with no update. He switched apps: the same frozen feed. A third. The local station's live page held a single paused frame, an anchor caught mid-word. Dan leaned hard into his leg and whined. John turned for home and walked fast, leash short, not quite running.

Back in the subdivision he heard a dog at the Morrisons', barking steady and hoarse. Another one farther down, behind a fence. A small one, high and frantic, inside a house with the blinds still down. Dan pulled toward the Morrisons' door, and John shortened the leash and kept him moving.

**8:15**
Back at the house Dan went under the kitchen table and stayed there. John opened his laptop. The same frozen feed, wider: the national wires, the overseas outlets, the social platforms that should have been a roar of overnight noise. Every one of them sat still, the newest timestamps all from a few minutes before two in the morning. Nothing was posted since.

**8:30**
At 8:30 he logged in to his standing sync call with his group at Google, the public-sector team he ran engagements for out of the D.C. office. Five minutes went by, then ten, and nobody else joined. He sat looking at his own video tile, his face was pale, jaw set, mouth thin. He closed the meeting.

He started calling people: his brother Mark first, then down the list of everyone he talked to in an ordinary week. Mostly voicemail. He left a few messages. Some numbers rang and rang. He never got a busy signal.

He set the phone down. Through the kitchen window he could hear the Morrisons' dog, still barking. Dan lifted his head off the floor and watched him.

"Harry," he said out loud.

He left Dan in the house and went next door. Harry had been his friend for twenty years. He had gotten John through the worst of it after Naomi had died. He rang the bell and waited. Nothing. He knocked. Nothing. The front door was locked. He went around to the garage and tried the code Harry had given him a few months back, and the door went up. "Harry? You here?" Nothing. Through the kitchen, the living room, down the hall to the master bedroom. "Harry?"

Harry was in bed, on his side, the blanket at his waist. "Harry?", John checked for a pulse. His friend was dead.  He pulled the blanket up over Harry's shoulder and let himself back out through the kitchen.  

He walked up the street toward the Morrisons, toward the barking. He rang the door bell and knocked, the barking got louder and vicious. He didn't know their dog and didn't think now was a good time to meet.

Next door was the Pattersons, he went to their door and rang the bell.  Nothing.  He looked through the windows on the side of the door, nobody.  "Bill, hey Bill, Judy are you there? Kids?" no response. The door was locked, he went around to the garage door, locked. Through the side gate to the back yard on the deck the back door was open.  John went in, "Bill, Judy?, "Hello? anyone here?" He walked down the hall and saw Judy and Bill in bed, "Hello?" He had to make sure, this was so awkward, the hair on the back of his neck was standing up, his stomach turning, adrenaline pumping. They were gone, the kids in their rooms, both of them dead.  He left quickly and walked home looking over his shoulder, feeling like someone was following him.

**9:10**
Dan met him at the door like it had been a week. He grabed his keys and Dan's leash, "Come on buddy, let's go for a drive." Out on to Old Milton Parkway there was no change since an hour ago. Had it only been an hour? John's mind was racing, he had not seen another living person since yesterday.  

Back out on Old Milton Parkway he headed south toward 400. The eighteen-wheeler still hung off the bridge exactly as he'd seen it earlier. He took the ramp south toward Atlanta.  Nothing was moving, a couple cars he could see were off in the ditch, one was flipped over. The black smoke he had seen earlier was still there and seemed bigger.  As he got close he saw that a truck, maybe a gas tanker, had gone off the road through a thin line of trees and the fence and crashed through the back of a retail shopping center on the east side. The retail center was fully envolved.  No emergency vehicles, no fire trucks, no sounds. 

He tried 911 again on the speaker. Same voice, same answer. He hung up. He had meant to get gas on the way home Sunday and blew it off, and now the gas light was on. Exit 8 had a QuickTrip a few blocks off 400.

The place was lit up and the pumps were on. He pulled up to pump eight. The pump took his credit card and turned on, the numbers ticking up. It was comforting to have something working like normal. 

There were two other cars at the island. A white pickup at pump two with the door open and a man face down on the concrete beside it, the pump handle still in the gas tank. A red Honda at pump five, a woman behind the wheel with her head against the window. John checked the man and then the woman, both dead. Dan watched from the back window, quiet.

He went into the store, the lights were on, country music playing low over the speakers. He went straight to the cooler and grabbed three water bottles, and drank half of one standing there. "Hello? Anybody here?" No answer.

A flat, cheerful voice from the side aisle: "Caution. Cleaning in progress." He spun around, the open water bottle flying out of his hand and onto the floor. A robot, disc-shaped, knee-high, was working up the aisle, brushes turning, a thin sheet of water and cleaner out ahead of it. It said the line again, bumped the base of a shelf, backed off a couple of inches, corrected, and kept going. John stood there a minute, he could hear his heart beating in his ears.

He found her on the floor behind the counter, wedged between the register and the cigarette rack. Twenties, maybe younger, in a QuickTrip polo. Her eyes were open, and her mouth. He checked for a pulse, her skin was cold. "So sorry," John found himself saying out loud. He left the water and went back out to the car, Dan was whining.  

**9:51**
He pulled up the map on his phone and searched for the closest fire station. North up Westside Parkway, the roads were mostly clear a few cars like the first he had discovered that day. He pulled into the Alpharetta Fire Station 83. The overhead doors were closed. He cracked the windows and left Dan in the car. The main door was open, "Hello? Anyone here?" Silence. He walked down the hall, in the kitchen there were two people in uniform laying on the floor, he checked both, same result as everyone else today, no pulse, cold. Ok, got to get out of here, John thought. Back in the car he sat staring out the windshield at the sun flashing off the glass of the overhead garage doors of the fire station. 

He pulled out the small notebook that he always kept with him to get thoughts out of his head through his fingers so his eyes could see what he was thinking. He wrote four questions:

*1. Where else?*
*2. How did this happen?*
*3. Is there anyone else alive?*
*4. Why am I alive?*

**10:30**
Considering what he had seen so far, he decided he could do a more thorough search back home. He headed back.

**10:50**
Back home, John started on his first question, Where else? He opened his laptop and pulled up FlightAware, an airplane tracking app. At this time of day there would be more than 9,000 active flights. To get a quick count he clicked on 'Browse by Aircraft Type', this view showed all the aircraft that were currently being tracked. He copied the first column that gave a count by type and dropped it into a chat: 323 aircraft. "That can't be right." he said outloud. Even though the number seemed wrong his immediate thought was, "there are others alive!"

FlightAware was a patchwork. A lot of its ground coverage came from hobbyists, thousands of people with a thirty dollar ADS-B receiver and a little antenna in a window or an attic, feeding it over their home internet. Some of what he was looking at was coming from a satellite, or an airline feed, or FlightAware filling gaps with a best guess. John had spent his career analyzing data like this, this was more than a hiccup, they must be having server issues, he thought. 

He got up from the kitchen table and walked to his office.  His team at Google were currently part of a massive AI implementation for FAA, DOT, Coast Guard, NOAA, Homeland Security, and FEMA blending commercial schedules, weather patterns, location capacity, and staffing metrics. He had access to the foundation telemetry data for road, rail, shipping, aviation, and power grid. He docked the laptop and brought up the local development model that he was currently testing. It had authentication for all the related systems, the legacy ERAM and STARS from the FAA and the AIS from the Coast Gaurd. He pulled up a chat and connected the local Gemini model to the new SMART (Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes, and Trajectories) system and the MCP server for Aireon, a private company providing global satellite tracking. Now instead of scrapping the public FlightAware system he prompted the most powerful system conceived by man, "How many aircraft are currently airborne globally?", he had mostly stopped typing back in 2026, but still felt like Scotty from Star Trek the Journey home.. the legendary line "Computer, hello computer". The reponse came after a couple seconds and the result printed on the screen, **351**. "What is the latest departure time of these 351 flights?" Response: **5:36am GMT**. That was 1:36am EDT. John stared at the screen. "There have been no departures globally after 5:36am GMT?" Response: **Correct**. He had come to appreciate the constraints of this newest model, a no nonsense basic response zero slop, it felt more like truth, which had become a very precious thing in the age of AI. 

"Map all flights and generate a table of aircraft type, carrier, departure time, departure location, filed destination location, and current flight duration." This took a couple minutes, one of the boons of Google's involvent in these projects was the geospatial power of google maps. The map and table pulled up in a generated single page application. He sorted the column by departure time and realized he may be missing data, the departure time in some of the source system data was a time that was filed, not actual. "Pull actual time reaching 5,000 feet above ground level for departure location and include any flights that completed their flight plans and also any flight that arrived at an airport after 6am GMT" The output in the table showed **353** aircraft. Two more from the orginal query. An Airbus 350-900 operated by Delta airlines with departure of Atlanta and destination of Seoul Sourth Korea had landed at Edmonton, Alberta at 8:36am GMT. The other had departed Edmonton at 11:31am GMT, a Pilatus PC-12 with no destination filed and the flight number read **DAL27PDK** and it was squaking **7700**, general emergency. "Give me full details of **DAL27PDK** PC-12 departed Edmonton at 11:31am GMT." Response: **Arrived Minneapolis-St. Paul 14:45 GMT. Departed Minneapolis-St. Paul 15:30 GMT.** He glanced at the time, it was 11:40am, that plane had left Minneapolis 10 minutes ago! "Have any of the other 351 aircraft diverted from their flight path after 2am EDT?" Response: **No**. "Show the live track of **DAL27PDK**." The PC-12 was passing through 8,000 feet in a southeast heading. "DAL27PDK", John said outloud. He looked back at the A350-900 that landed in Edmonton, it was Delta flight 27. The pieces fell together pretty quick, a pilot from DAL27 is now flying this PC-12 and the destination is PDK. John knew PDK, it was Peachtree Dekalb Airport, it is where he spent most weekends maintaining or flying his Cessna 182.

"Nautical miles from Edmonton to PDK?" Response: **1,700 miles**. "What is the range of the Pilatus PC-12?" Response: **1,803 nm with medium load**. So he stopped in Minneapolis-St. Paul for fuel. "Distance from Minneapolis-St. Paul to PDK and PC-12 flight time? Response: **780nm and 2 h 47 min flighttime at 280 kt.** "That puts him on the ground between 2:15 and 2:45." John said outloud. He pulled out his notebook and wrote next to question 1: *global* and next to question 3: *Maybe*.
 

He had numbers in his phone from years of work with the agencies, the operations lines his project contacts had given him and told him to keep. FEMA's operations center in D.C. The FAA's Command Center out in Warrenton, Virginia, the room that ran traffic flow for the whole country. The Coast Guard's National Command Center in Washington.

The FEMA line rang until it dropped. The FAA's picked up on a recording that asked him to hold for the next available specialist, and he held for five minutes listening to it loop. He'd stood in that room in Warrenton. Rows of specialists at their consoles, the whole country's traffic up on the wall in front of them, a place that never closed. He knew how many people it took to staff a shift in there. "Please continue to hold," the recording said, and started over. He sat with the phone against his ear a while after he'd stopped listening to it, and when he finally hung up he felt his hands trembling. The Coast Guard line rang and rang. Under the first question he wrote: FEMA, FAA, USCG. Nobody.

He started the way he started with any system he hadn't built: find out what was actually there before trusting any of it. He had the model walk every connector his credentials reached and ping each one. It took a few minutes. The FAA's surveillance feeds answered, NOAA's weather and the beacon alerts out of Suitland, the space weather alerts out of Boulder, the USGS earthquake feed out of Golden. The orbital element sets the Space Force published for everything in orbit `[verify: Space-Track account]`. The automated notices NASA pushed out to observatories, gamma-ray and neutrino alerts meant for telescopes, open to anyone who registered for a key `[verify: SNEWS alerts distributed via GCN]`. The Coast Guard's ship tracking, the state DOT camera networks state by state, the Energy Information Administration's hourly grid tables, and the frequency readings a university lab in Knoxville collected off sensors plugged into ordinary wall outlets across the country. A handful of state camera networks timed out; he had it note which ones and keep trying them. Everything he touched was something his credentials already let him read. He checked that twice.

Then the capture. A live system was a thing that would stop, and whatever it was showing would go with it. You took its state while you could and worked out what it meant afterward. Collect first, while the pipes are still open. Analyze later. He put his hands on the keyboard, and by the end of the first line they were steady again. He wrote out the job for the model the way he'd have written a spec for his team: every hour on the hour, a snapshot of every feed that answered, written to the dev box's internal storage, each hour in its own folder, nothing overwritten. He had it time a full pass first. Twenty-two minutes, most of it waiting on slow state camera servers, so he set it to hourly. That left room for a pass to run slow without stepping on the next one, and it was fine enough to catch anything that moved on purpose. He checked the free space on the drives against an hour's worth of data, a few gigabytes, mostly camera frames, and the drives would hold years of it.

Then he pointed it backward. The flight-tracking and AIS services kept history, and so did the EIA's tables, the beacon logs, and the alert streams, so the model could go back and fill in the hours already gone, one snapshot per hour from 2:00 a.m. on. He watched the folder names stack up in the directory list, 0200, 0300, 0400. In the 0200 folder the file from NASA's observatory alerts was bigger than that feed's files for every hour after it put together. He noted it and didn't open it. The cameras kept nothing. Their record would start with the first pass, at 9:40 that morning, and everything before that existed only in what he'd seen on his walk.

He didn't stop to look at what the feeds were showing him. He knew himself well enough to know that once he started looking he'd go too deep and wouldn't come back up for hours. Looking could wait. Whatever answered the first question would be on the drives.

He looked at the pad again. He couldn't answer any of the four this morning, and whatever answered them later would have to come out of what he saved now. So he went after everything else that lived on somebody else's server and would be gone when that server went dark. He started with the FAA's charts, the current cycle: every approach plate in the country, the Chart Supplement with every airport's runways and frequencies and fuel, the sectionals and the IFR enroute charts, onto the dev box and onto the iPad he flew with. The FAA's download site was slow, then fine.

Then maps. Every map he'd ever pulled up on a screen came as tiles off somebody's server, over cell towers that ran on the same grid as everything else. He pulled a full OpenStreetMap extract for North America, fourteen and a half gigabytes compressed, and the open-source software to draw it, maps that would work with no signal at all.

Then the manuals and how-tos, everything he could think of that he might need to fix or run with nobody to call. The service manual for his generator. The 182's maintenance manual. The Army's survival and field manuals, which had always been public. EPA guides on treating water. The Merck Manual's free online edition, page by page, for the day somebody got hurt with no doctor to call. Manuals for radios he didn't own yet. He kept adding to the list on the pad as he went, and every time he crossed one off he thought of two more.

Somewhere in the middle of it Dan came down the hall and lay across the office doorway with his chin on his paws, where he could see him. After a while he got up and put his nose under John's hand on the desk, and John scratched behind his ears without looking away from the screen until Dan went back to the doorway and lay down again.

Then the agency data he was cleared for. The Homeland Security infrastructure datasets, every power plant, hospital, airport, fuel terminal, and dam in the country as points on a map. The public version had been shut down a few years back; the restricted one still took his login and the data-use agreement a DHS sponsor had signed for his team. NOAA's latest forecast runs. The EIA's tables on every generating plant. He glanced at the grid numbers while they came down, since they were the thing he understood best. His own corner of the Southeast was still posting every hour, an hour or so behind the clock the way the EIA's tables always ran `[verify: EIA-930 posting lag]`, and the Knoxville sensors had the frequency still sitting near sixty. He knew enough about how a grid was run to know it wouldn't stay there with nobody running the plants. He thought about the generator in the side yard and the propane tank next to it, and wrote *generator* at the bottom of the pad, for later.

He copied everything onto two external drives, and when they were done he put one in his flight bag.

He almost didn't think of the photos. Twenty-some years of them, his and Naomi's, all of it sitting on a server somewhere like everything else. He started the download and let it run.

Somewhere in there he made himself a sandwich he ate standing up in the office doorway, and tried Mark again. Voicemail. He didn't leave another message.

He kept FlightAware open on the second screen the whole time. At a quarter to eleven DAL27PDK slowed over the Twin Cities and stopped at Minneapolis-St. Paul. He went back to work and checked it every few minutes, and at eleven-thirty it moved again, climbed out, and settled on a heading southeast. He measured it on the chart: Minneapolis to PDK was a little under eight hundred nautical miles, and at the ground speed it was showing, call it two hours forty-five. Somewhere around 2:15. He wrote *leave 12:15* on the pad and underlined it.

At noon the capture job wrote its noon snapshot, on time. He finished the last of his coffee while he spot-checked a few cameras in the folder against the live feed to make sure they matched. They did. But three of the state camera networks that had timed out that morning were still timing out, and that bothered him. He dug into the first one and found the state had moved its cameras behind a new address sometime in the last year and never updated the old one, so he rewired the connector and watched the frames start to come in. The second one was the same problem with a different fix. He was halfway into the third when he looked up at the clock in the corner of the screen. 12:41.

He took Dan's leash off the hook. "Come on, buddy, we're going for a ride." Dan was at the door before he was. 



He took 400 south. A mile down, the smoke he'd seen that morning was what was left of a fuel tanker, burned down to the frame. The trees along 400 were on fire, and it was spreading toward the buildings beyond. That may have been the thud at 2:05. Most of the cars had drifted off to the side, into the median cables, off the right into the ditch, a few all the way into the trees, and the ones still in the lanes sat at angles he had to steer around. He drove it at fifty anyway, then sixty, picking the open lane a quarter mile ahead and changing lanes early. 

Back on 400 he stopped looking into the cars he passed. He took the ramp onto Interstate 285 east, and the stalled cars thinned out, and he sped up. Sixty, seventy, swinging wide around a minivan nosed into the median, a box truck on its side across the right two lanes. Twice he took the shoulder, gravel rattling in the wheel wells, Dan braced on the back seat with his nails dug into the upholstery.

Past the Ashford-Dunwoody exit the road bent left under an overpass, and on the far side of it a sedan sat dead in the middle lane. He was doing seventy-five. He stood on the brake and felt the pedal buzz under his foot as the ABS chattered, the tires hissing, the car sliding a little sideways before it caught, and Dan went off the back seat into the footwell with a yelp. He got it stopped about a car length short. He sat there a second with both hands on the wheel, breathing hard. In the sedan there was the shape of somebody slumped against the driver's window.

"Sorry, buddy," he said. Dan climbed back up onto the seat. John went around the sedan and brought it back up to sixty, and kept it there.

He came off at Peachtree Industrial and went south toward the airport. He had made this drive most Saturday mornings for twenty years, to the hangar and the Cessna 182. The daily walks with Dan and the weekend flights balanced out the long hours of concentration his work took. DeKalb-Peachtree had become the place he saw people most. In the past sixteen years he had eaten a lot of meals at the two restaurants on the field, the Downwind upstairs over Epps, the FBO, and the 57th Fighter Group down by runway 3.
