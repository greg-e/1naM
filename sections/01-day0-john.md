# 1 — John, Day 0

The sound woke him at 2:05 a.m., a distant thud that rattled the window in its frame and was gone before he'd fully woken up. He lay there a minute in the dark, waiting for something — a siren, a repeat, anything that would tell him what he'd just heard. Nothing came. No sirens, no distant alarm. Just the ordinary sounds of the house around him, Dan, his dog, tags shifting faintly somewhere down the hall as he resettled himself. He decided it was a transformer, or somebody's car doing something stupid out on Old Milton Parkway, rolled back to his side and faded back to sleep.

He woke again at six, same as every morning: fifty-eight years old, sixteen years in the house without his wife, Naomi, alone in it except, always, for a dog. Bathroom, hot shower, then down to what he liked to think of as first breakfast — the same every day, a blueberry-beet-spinach concoction he called a power shake. The early dawn had started; the days were getting shorter and real light didn't come until seven. Then the part he looked forward to, taking Dan out. The dog's tail was already going dangerously before they'd cleared the driveway. Happy dog, happy day.

The neighborhood was quiet, and John found himself, without quite meaning to, running explanations at it. More cars in the driveways than a normal Tuesday — half the street working from home, maybe. A holiday he'd lost track of — no; yesterday had been Labor Day, he'd had Monday off, today was the morning everything went back to normal. At the corner where the Pattersons' kids caught the 7:15 bus like clockwork there were no kids and no bus, and when he checked his watch it was just past 7:20. He kept going, past his usual turnaround, and the stillness held all the way to where his subdivision met Old Milton Parkway. A low hum, but an overarching silence. Old Milton hadn't been genuinely quiet at any hour in years. 

"Hm," he said, out loud, to the dog or to himself. Then, "Ummm" — the flat working hum he made when the inputs on a problem hadn't added up to anything yet, a noise standing in for a conclusion he didn't have. He walked out to the edge of the parkway. In both directions something was wrong. To the north, a hundred yards off on the blind curve, a car sat nose-down in the drainage ditch, lights on, and he could just make out its engine running high and uneven. To the south a box truck had stopped square in the median and blocked the rest of the view that way. He went north first — a car in a ditch with the engine still going, someone might need pulling out. It took him a few minutes. The closer he got the clearer it was: the engine pinned at a fast idle, the rear wheels spinning free in the air above the ditch, turning and turning against nothing. He reached the door and pulled it open.

A man, sixty-odd, sitting almost upright, held there by the shoulder belt. His head had tipped toward the far window and one hand had slid off the wheel into his lap. No blood, no broken glass — nothing about him matched a car in a ditch. The car looked like an afterthought, like it had drifted off the road on its own some while after he was already gone. Dan had stopped ten feet back on the shoulder and would come no closer, ears flat, a thin whine building in his chest.

John reached in and pressed two fingers to the side of the man's neck. His hand was steady — steadier than the rest of him had any right to be. Forty years back he'd worked the overnight grill at a Waffle House off North Avenue to get himself through Tech, and the one thing that job had left him with was this: panic and competence could run at the same time, the hands finishing the work while the rest of a man came apart. He held the two fingers on the carotid the way you're taught, longer than he needed to. Nothing. No pulse, no rise in the chest, the skin cool in a way that meant hours, not minutes — this had happened in the night, not the morning. Around the time, some back part of his mind noted, of the thud that woke him.

He straightened up. The engine was still running, a high patient idle, the back wheels still turning smoothly in the open air, the one moving thing on the whole road. He leaned across the man's knees, found the ignition, and shut it off. The quiet that came in behind it was total.

He called 911 from the shoulder, Dan pressed against his leg.

It answered on the second ring, and it wasn't a person — a calm synthetic voice already mid-sentence about elevated call volume and estimated response times. He knew the cadence of it the way you know a colleague's voice through a wall: a model-backed triage agent, the kind that had been going quietly into 911 centers for two years now to hold the queue when the human dispatchers were buried. He had sat in the rooms where deployments like this one were argued line by line. He gave it what he had, flat and clean — a man, deceased, alone, in a vehicle off Old Milton Parkway near the north curve. The voice thanked him and said a unit would be dispatched as priority allowed. He asked for a dispatcher. It repeated the line about call volume. He asked again — an operator, then just a person, *is there anyone actually there?* — and it looped cleanly back to the top of its script and started over, and he understood that it would keep doing that, patiently, into an empty room, for as long as the building had power. He went back south and crossed to the box truck in the median — a white contractor's rig, nose against the cable barrier. The driver was slumped the same way the first man had been, belt on, no mark on him. John opened the door and checked to be sure what his thoughts were already telling him... dead. "Ummm," he said, to no one.

He kept walking. South, toward the next bend — some part of him still insisting there'd be a version of this morning that made sense a little further on. A road crew. A cop waving traffic around a wreck. One working thing. Dan pulled back against the leash the whole way and John kept coaxing him on.

The parkway dipped ahead of him to pass under GA Highway 400, and where it dipped he got his sightline up onto the bridge, and that was the image that stayed with him a long time after. An eighteen-wheeler had jackknifed across the southbound lanes up there and folded against the bridge rail. The tractor had gone through the rail and hung nose-down off the edge, out over the road he was standing on, held by nothing he could see, one front wheel still turning slowly in the air. Past it 400 ran on out of sight, and every car he could make out on it was stopped — no brake lights, no drift, no one picking a way through. On an ordinary Tuesday at this hour that highway moved more people past this one spot in a minute than lived on his whole street.

He stood there until Dan leaned hard into his leg and whined, then turned and walked home fast, leash short, not quite running. 

Back in the kitchen Dan went to his bed and stayed there. John pulled up the news on his phone — not to be told what had happened, just to find the edges of it, how far out it ran. The headline feed hadn't moved. He checked the timestamp on the top story: nine hours old, no correction stacked on it, no update layered over it the way a real morning's coverage always thickened by six. He switched apps. The same frozen feed. A third. The local station's live page held a single paused frame, an anchor caught mid-word, standing in for a broadcast that wasn't happening.

He moved to the laptop and widened it out, methodical the way thirty years of building safety-critical systems — and breaking them on purpose, to find where they failed — had trained him to be. Never trust one sensor. Get a second source, a third, and see where they disagree before you believe any of them. National feeds, international ones, the social platforms that should have been a roar of overnight noise and instead sat still, every timestamp clustered in the hours before two in the morning. Nothing anywhere had moved since.

And there was the other silence, the one he'd been standing inside all morning without turning to look at it: he hadn't heard a single aircraft. Not one. He lived under enough of a corridor that some thread of engine noise overhead was almost always there if you knew to listen, and he knew — he flew out of PDK most weekends, single-engine, instrument-rated and current, and spent the rest of the week elbow-deep in the data those same aircraft threw off: ADS-B, transponder returns, the broadcast feeds the FAA and half a dozen other agencies leaned on to see the sky at all. This morning there had been nothing up there. Not distant, not close.

He opened FlightAware. He knew what that map was supposed to look like at eight on a weekday — he'd watched the live version move across a wall of monitors in an FAA facility more times than he could count — and this wasn't it. The airspace over the country was empty. The whole global map was down to a scatter of maybe fifty aircraft, most of them long shallow arcs over open ocean. A few of the arcs were wrong in a way it took him a second to name. He clicked into one: a FedEx 767, Memphis to Jacksonville, a fifty-minute run, now four hundred miles out over the Atlantic and still going — well past its destination, holding a heading no route chart would ever draw. He checked another. The same. Airplanes flying themselves out to sea.

One track wasn't behaving like a machine running out its program. A Delta flight out of Atlanta, well east over the North Atlantic — its line bent hard north and west off the great-circle route to Paris, run down toward the Newfoundland coast, and then, an hour back by the timestamps, doing something no failed autopilot does: pulling up off an approach to one airport and tracking a hundred miles inland to another. The marker was crawling as he watched now, circling low and slow over a field in the interior. To a man who still shot approaches in hard weather a couple of times a month, it didn't read as an approach. It read as someone looking the field over before they committed to it. Deliberate. Hand-flown, or close to it. He refreshed once, twice; the icon kept circling, then steadied onto a final and came down, the altitude and groundspeed readouts stepping down in the clean orderly way of a controlled landing, and then the track slowed and turned off the runway and began to taxi. Gander, the label read, once he zoomed in far enough to catch the code — not a random piece of pavement but the transatlantic diversion field, the one every jet over that water plans around. An Atlanta–Paris run would fly an augmented crew, more than two up front. Somebody who knew exactly what they were doing had put that airplane on the ground there, on purpose, in the last ten minutes. It was one specific, deliberate fact in a morning that had given him nothing but absence, and he sat looking at it. *Somebody's still making decisions out there. Whatever this is, it's not nothing.*

He filed it away — worth holding onto, not yet worth acting on — and made himself keep gathering. The part of him that never trusted one data point when more were available wasn't finished.

He pulled up the state DOT traffic cameras next. He'd spent years on the government side of exactly these systems, and that work taught you the roads showed you trouble before anyone got around to announcing it. He'd seen one stretch of 400 with his own eyes; the cameras were to find out how much further it went. It went everywhere. By this hour 400 and the Perimeter and every artery between them should have been thick with rush hour, brake lights strung for miles. Instead the freeways sat nearly empty, a scatter of vehicles across lanes that should have been packed solid, and none of them were moving. One camera near a Perimeter interchange caught three or four cars nosed together mid-lane, doors shut, the image too coarse to explain itself. Another, near his own exit, held a box truck stopped across two lanes, no flashers, nothing stacking up behind it because there was nothing left to stack. He sat very still in the kitchen chair and did the arithmetic his own commute had drilled into him a thousand times. Not a light morning. A road that should have been full and wasn't, except for the cars that were never going anywhere again.

At nine his standing sync came due — his group at Anthropic, the public-sector team he ran engagements for out of the D.C. office, a call that started within thirty seconds of the hour whether anyone wanted it to or not. He logged in and waited for the rest of the team. The timer ran past five minutes, then ten, and no one else joined. He sat looking at his own video tile — pale, jaw set, mouth thin — alone in a call built for six, and the thing that had been rising in him all morning finally arrived and had a name. He'd worked disaster scenarios his whole career, on paper, for money. He had never once been inside one. Horror, it turned out, was mostly the arithmetic finishing.

He started calling people, working down the mental list of everyone he talked to in an ordinary week. His brother Mark first. Then friends. Then coworkers he barely knew, on the theory that somebody, surely somebody, would pick up. Every call rang into nothing — no voicemail, no busy signal, just an open line ringing into a silence that never broke, over and over, until he'd worked through nearly every name he had and the silence had stopped feeling like bad luck and started feeling like the answer.

He set the phone down. The house was very quiet. Dan lifted his head off the bed and watched him, and John went over and crouched and pushed both hands into the dog's fur and stayed like that. 

"Harry"... he left Dan in the house and went next door, Harry had been a friend for 20 years... that guy over the fence always giving advice about the lawn.  Rang the door bell, nothing... knocked... nothing.  Tried the door and it was open, stepping in "Hello? Harry? you here man?" nothing. Walking through the living room to the kitchen, no Harry... down the hall to the master bedroom, Harry? There was Harry in bed, check pulse, just like the others... gone.

Back to the house, Dan at the door waiting. Something came up out of his chest then — not the flat working hum from the roadside, the one that stood in for a conclusion he didn't have. This one had words in it. He hadn't made a sound like it since Naomi died, sixteen years ago, and he heard himself say it into the dog's coat, quiet, with nothing behind it but need: "Oh, God. Please help me."
