# 9/5/2028 — John Lauer, AI Engineer & Researcher, Day 0

The sound woke him, he looked over at the clock, it was 2:05 a.m.. A distant thud had rattled the window in its frame and was gone before he'd fully woken up. He lay there a minute in the dark, waiting for something — a siren, a repeat, anything that would tell him what he'd just heard. Nothing, just the ordinary sounds of the house around him, Dan, his dog, tags shifting faintly somewhere down the hall as he resettled himself. He rolled over and faded back to sleep.

He woke again at six, same as every morning: sixty-one years old, alone the last 16 years, well not alone, there was always a dog, not just any dog, always a Mountain Cur. 

He woke up with a dull headache behind his eys, two ibuprofen for that, hot shower, then down to what he liked to think of as first breakfast — the same every day, a blueberry-avocado-beet-spinach concoction he called a brain shake. Early dawn light shone through the kitchen window; the days were shorter and real light didn't come until 7:15. Next was getting Dan out for their 2 mile morning walk. He intentionally tried to not think about work, and just enjoy Dan and the fresh air and first light. The dog's tail was already going dangerously fast back and forth before they'd cleared the driveway.

The neighborhood was quiet, more cars in driveways than a normal Tuesday. Strange, since yesterday had been Labor Day, things were usually a bit more busy, a short week compressed schedules. At the corner where the Pattersons' kids caught the 7:15 bus like clockwork, there were no kids and no bus, he double checked his watch, it was just past 7:20. He kept going, past his usual turnaround, and the stillness held all the way to where his subdivision met Old Milton Parkway. Old Milton hadn't been genuinely quiet at any hour in years. He could not comprehend the silence, it was overwhelming.

"Hmm," he said, out loud — the flat hum he made when the inputs on a problem hadn't added up to anything yet, like the processing circle that annoyed him on the screens he looked at everyday. He walked out to the edge of Old Milton. To the north, a hundred yards off on the blind curve, a car sat nose-down in the drainage ditch, lights on, and he could just make out its engine running high and uneven — a car in a ditch with the engine still going, no emergency vehicles, no sirens, must have just happened. It took him a couple minutes walking fast up the hill, and he was out of breath at the top, he couldn't seem to get a deep breath. The engine was at a fast idle, the rear wheels spinning free in the air above the ditch, turning and turning against nothing. He had Dan sit and wait a few yards back. He reached the door and pulled it open.

A man, fortyish, sitting almost upright, held there by the shoulder belt, eyes open. Dan, ears flat, a thin whine building in his chest.

John leaned across the man's knees, found the ignition, and shut it off. The quiet that came was total. He pressed two fingers to the side of the man's neck. His hand was steady — steadier than the rest of him had any right to be. No pulse, the skin cool even cold — this had happened a few hours ago, not a few minutes ago.

He backed off from the car and called 911, Dan pressed against his leg.

It answered on the first ring, and for a second he took it for a person — a woman's voice, warm and unhurried, with a small breath before the question. "Nine-one-one, what's the address of your emergency?" Then the rhythm registered, too even, no background noise, and he knew what he was talking to. He had been on the periphery of the decisions about how 911 centers would use AI agents to triage the queue while human dispatchers were tied up on other calls. He had sat in the rooms where deployments like this one were argued line by line.

He gave it a flat, clean report — a man, deceased, alone in a vehicle off Old Milton Parkway just north of GA Highway 400. It asked if he was sure. It asked whether the man was breathing, and whether John was safe where he stood. "No," he said, and "No," and then, "Yes." "I'm so sorry," it said. "That's a hard thing to walk up on." He felt the small, stupid pull of being understood by it. Call volume was higher than usual, it told him, and a unit would be dispatched as priority allowed. Would he stay near the vehicle?

He asked for a person. Gently, it said it was an AI assistant handling the queue while the dispatchers were on other calls, that everything he'd told it was logged and would reach them, and that it would stay on the line as long as he liked. It stayed kind and steady and told him the same thing in slightly different words. He hung up.

He walked south, toward Georgia Highway 400 the next bend over the hill — some part of him still insisting there'd be a version of this morning that made sense a little further on. A road crew. A cop waving traffic around a wreck. The silence amplifyied all the sounds he was making.  He could hear his heart beating in his ears, the gentle whomp whomp of his boots on the concrete sidewalk. Dan's tags clinking together sounded like cymbals in a big band. 

Old Milton Parkway passed under 400. An eighteen-wheeler had jackknifed across the southbound lanes and folded against the bridge rail. The tractor had gone through the rail and hung nose-down off the edge, out over the road below, held by nothing he could see. Every car on 400 he could make out was stopped — no brake lights. On an ordinary Tuesday at this hour this highway moved more people past this one spot in a minute than lived on his whole street. Further south there was a plume of smoke rising high into the sky.

"Hmm," he said again out loud, and pulled up the news on his phone. The headline feed hadn't moved. He checked the timestamp on the top story: six hours old, no update. He switched apps. The same frozen feed. A third. The local station's live page held a single paused frame, an anchor caught mid-word. Dan leaned hard into his leg and whined, John turned and walked home fast, leash short, not quite running. 

Back at his house, Dan curled up under the kitchen table. He moved to his laptop. The same frozen feed, wider: national wires, overseas outlets, the social platforms that should have been a roar of overnight noise and instead sat still, every timestamp clustered in the hours before 2:00am. Nothing anywhere had moved since. 

He opened FlightAware, a flight tracking app. This time of day there would be thousands of flights being tracked worldwide. The airspace over the country was mostly empty. The whole global map was down to a scatter of aircraft, most of them long tracks over open ocean. A few were wrong in a way he didn't quite understand. He clicked into one: a FedEx 767, Memphis to Jacksonville, a fifty-minute run, now four hundred miles out over the Atlantic and still going — well past its destination. He checked another, the same, routes that would never be flown like this.

He pulled up a handful of the state DOT traffic cameras — the interchange where 400 met the Perimeter, then a few in other cities. Every one showed the same thing he'd walked through that morning: open lanes at an hour that should have been solid rush hour, a scatter of stopped vehicles across them, nothing moving.

He logged in to his 8:30am online standing sync meeting — his group at Google, the public-sector team he ran engagements for out of the D.C. office. The meeting time ran past five minutes, then ten, and no one else joined. He sat looking at his own video tile — pale, jaw set, mouth thin. John left the meeting.

He started calling people — his brother Mark first, then down the mental list of everyone he talked to in an ordinary week. Voicemail mostly; he left a few messages. Some numbers just rang and rang, no busy signal anywhere. 

He set the phone down. The house was uncomfortably quiet. Dan lifted his head off the floor and watched him. 

"Harry," he said out loud. He left Dan in the house and went next door, Harry had been a friend for 20 years... not just that guy over the fence always giving advice about the lawn, a real friend who had helped John through many tough times.  Rang the door bell, waited a minute, nothing... knocked... nothing.  Tried the door and it was locked, went around to the garage and tried the code Harry had given him a few months ago, it worked. Stepping into the living room from the garage, "Hello? Harry? are you here?" nothing. Walking through the living room to the kitchen, no Harry... down the hall to the master bedroom, "Harry?" There he was in bed, check pulse, his friend was dead.

He stood a minute in the doorway of a dead man's bedroom, the house around him exactly as tidy as Harry always kept it. He pulled the blanket up over Harry's shoulder and let himself back out through the kitchen and across the strip of grass between the two driveways.

Dan met him at the door like it had been a week. He went straight to the laptop and pulled FlightAware back up, wider than he'd looked at eight, studying patterns and checking each aircraft's information to see origin and destination. The map was still down to a scatter, most of it the same long-haul tracks over open ocean he'd already half-noticed — each icon still crawling forward in real time holding a heading that never varied, never corrected, never slowed. He was ready to write the whole screen off the same way when one track stopped him.

An airplane doing something different. He clicked into it. **DAL27**, an Airbus 350-900 out of Atlanta at 11:35 the night before, filed for Seoul. The track ran north-northwest out of Atlanta, over the Midwest and Manitoba and up into northern Saskatchewan, as straight as all the others. Then at 3:30 in the morning it turned. A clean turn to the south, and twenty-five minutes later a second turn, onto a line that ran straight for Edmonton, Alberta, and then a descent. The track ended on the ground at Edmonton International at 4:36 Eastern. It hadn't moved since.

He sat back. Every other airplane on the map was holding a heading. This one had turned and landed.

He zoomed in on Edmonton, then out again, looking at everything around it, and found the second one. A target had come up off the same airport at 7:31 Eastern, three hours after the A350 landed, and gone southeast. The only departure he could find anywhere in the world since a few minutes before two in the morning. He clicked on it. A Pilatus PC-12, a single-engine turboprop, Canadian-registered. FlightAware showed no flight plan for it, no destination. But the flight ID it was broadcasting, the line where an airliner would show its flight number, didn't read like a registration. It read **DAL27PDK**.

He read it three times. DAL27, the airplane on the ground at Edmonton. PDK, DeKalb-Peachtree, his own home field, where his 182 had been sitting in its hangar since Sunday afternoon.

FlightAware wasn't the real source, it was a patchwork. A lot of its ground coverage came from hobbyists, thousands of people with a thirty dollar ADS-B receiver and a little antenna in a window or an attic, feeding it over their home internet. Not many of them had antennas in northern Saskatchewan. So some of what he was looking at was coming from a satellite, or an airline feed, or FlightAware filling a gap with its best guess. John had spent his career taking apart pipelines like that, and he knew a line on a map could just as easily be a caching bug as a real airplane.

He opened a chat window on the laptop. His job at Google gave it reach into a lot of things. For years his team had built Gemini deployments for the agencies that run the systems under all those maps... the FAA, DOT, Coast Guard, NOAA, Homeland Security, FEMA. That meant read-only access to their live feeds same as the DOT cameras he'd pulled up earlier. He asked the model to check both tracks against the FAA's own surveillance data. It came back in a few seconds. The A350 was real: Delta's airframe, the transponder address matched, squawking 7700 from the turn onward. The PC-12 was real too, and it was a different airplane, its transponder address registered in Canada to a PC-12 NGX. Its flight ID had been changed from the registration about the time it started its engine.

7700, general emergency. John knew the code the way any pilot does, four digits somebody has to key in by hand on the transponder. A flight ID was the same, somebody typing it in letter by letter. Somebody had landed that A350, gotten out of it, found a smaller airplane on the same field, and typed in where he'd come from and where he was going so anyone watching would know. Every other airplane on the map was holding a heading.

He did the math. Edmonton to PDK was a long way for a PC-12, seventeen hundred miles, more than he'd want to try in one go with any reserve. The pilot would have to stop for fuel somewhere on the way. Call it six and a half, seven hours with a fuel stop. Two in the afternoon, give or take.

He had Dan's leash off the hook and Dan was already at the door before he stopped. It was ten after nine. PDK was forty minutes away on a bad day. He'd be sitting at the field for four hours watching a dot on a phone, and the machine down the hall would be sitting here doing nothing.

He hung the leash back up. Dan looked at it, then at him, and went back under the kitchen table.

The power was on, the internet was up, the agency feeds were answering. None of that was going to last, he knew enough about how all of it was held together to know that much, and he didn't know how long he had.

He took the laptop down the hall to his office, where the workstation sat against the far wall, the one Google had issued him when he moved to the public-sector group, running a build of the model locally instead of in somebody's data center. He woke the screens, pulled a legal pad over, and wrote a list the way he would have at the start of an exercise. *1. Who's still answering. 2. What's still answering. 3. Save it. 4. Everything else while the servers are up. 5. DAL27PDK.*

He had numbers in his phone from years of exercises with the agencies, the ones they handed out the first morning and told you to keep. FEMA's operations center in D.C. The FAA's Command Center out in Warrenton, Virginia, the room that ran traffic flow for the whole country. The Coast Guard's command center at headquarters `[verify facility names]`. The FEMA line rang until it dropped. The FAA's picked up on a recording that asked him to hold for the next available specialist, and he held for five minutes listening to it loop. He'd stood in that room in Warrenton. Rows of specialists at their consoles, the whole country's traffic up on the wall in front of them, a place that never closed. He knew how many people it took to staff a shift in there. "Please continue to hold," the recording said, and started over. He sat with the phone against his ear a while after he'd stopped listening to it, and when he finally hung up his hand wasn't steady anymore. The Coast Guard line rang and rang. He drew a line through number one.

The Coast Guard had run a continuity drill a few years back, two days pretending the D.C. data centers were gone, and he'd spent the whole first morning of it finding out that half of what people counted on came off servers nobody in the room could name. So he started where that drill had taught him to. He had the model walk every connector his credentials reached and ping each one. It took a few minutes. The FAA's surveillance feeds answered, NOAA's weather and the beacon alerts out of Suitland, the Coast Guard's ship tracking, the state DOT camera networks state by state, the Energy Information Administration's hourly grid tables. A handful of state camera networks timed out; he had it note which ones and keep trying them. Everything he touched was something his credentials already let him read. He checked that twice.

Then the capture. He'd sat in on a GridEx round once `[verify: Google public-sector participation in GridEx]`, the scenario a grid going down hard across half the country, and by the second day the camera feeds and the telemetry on the big wall displays were gone, and a room full of smart people were arguing from memory about what they'd seen the day before. He'd been saying it at the front of rooms ever since... collect first, while the pipes are still open. Analyze later. He put his hands on the keyboard, and by the end of the first line they were steady again. He wrote out the job for the model the way he'd have written a spec for his team: every hour on the hour, a snapshot of every feed that answered, written to the workstation's local drives, each hour in its own folder, nothing overwritten. He had it time a full pass first. Twenty-two minutes, most of it waiting on slow state camera servers, so he set it to hourly. That left room for a pass to run slow without stepping on the next one, and it was fine enough to catch anything that moved on purpose. He checked the free space on the drives against an hour's worth of data, a few gigabytes, mostly camera frames, and the drives would hold years of it.

Then he pointed it backward. The flight-tracking and AIS services kept history, and so did the EIA's tables and the beacon logs, so the model could go back and fill in the hours already gone, one snapshot per hour from 2:00 a.m. on. He watched the folder names stack up in the directory list, 0200, 0300, 0400. The cameras kept nothing. Their record would start with the first pass, at 9:40 that morning, and everything before that existed only in what he'd seen on his walk.

He didn't stop to look at what the feeds were showing him. He knew himself well enough to know that once he started looking he'd go too deep and wouldn't come back up for hours. Looking could wait. It would all be on the drives.

Number four on the pad. Things that lived on somebody else's server and would be gone when that server went dark. He started with the FAA's charts, the current 28-day cycle: every approach plate in the country, the Chart Supplement with every airport's runways and frequencies and fuel, the sectionals and the IFR enroute charts, onto the workstation and onto the iPad he flew with. The FAA's download site was slow, then fine.

Then maps. He'd watched a FEMA tabletop in a Crystal City hotel ballroom play out a Gulf hurricane, and the part that stuck with him was the field teams in the scenario sitting in their trucks with laptops and no way to load a map once the cell towers went down. He pulled a full OpenStreetMap extract for North America, maps that would work with no signal at all `[verify size]`.

Then the manuals and how-tos, everything he could think of that he might need to fix or run with nobody to call. The service manual for his generator. The 182's maintenance manual. The Army's survival and field manuals, which had always been public. EPA guides on treating water. Manuals for radios he didn't own yet. He kept adding to the list on the pad as he went, and every time he crossed one off he thought of two more.

Somewhere in the middle of it Dan came down the hall and lay across the office doorway with his chin on his paws, where he could see him. After a while he got up and put his nose under John's hand on the desk, and John scratched behind his ears without looking away from the screen until Dan went back to the doorway and lay down again.

Then the agency data he was cleared for. The Homeland Security infrastructure datasets, every power plant, hospital, airport, fuel terminal, and dam in the country as points on a map `[verify: HIFLD access]`. NOAA's latest forecast runs. The EIA's tables on every generating plant. He glanced at the grid numbers while they came down, since they were the thing he understood best. His own corner of the Southeast was still posting every hour, the frequency still sitting near sixty. He'd seen enough in that GridEx round to know it wouldn't stay there with nobody running the plants. He thought about the generator in the side yard and the propane tank next to it, and wrote *generator* at the bottom of the pad, for later.

He copied everything onto two external drives, and when they were done he put one in his flight bag.

He almost didn't think of the photos. Twenty-some years of them, his and Naomi's, all of it sitting on a server somewhere like everything else. He started the download and let it run.

Somewhere in there he made himself a sandwich he ate standing up in the office doorway, and tried Mark again. Voicemail. He didn't leave another message.

He kept FlightAware open on the second screen the whole time. At a quarter to eleven DAL27PDK slowed over the Twin Cities and stopped at Minneapolis-St. Paul. He went back to work and checked it every few minutes, and at eleven-thirty it moved again, climbed out, and settled on a heading southeast. He measured it on the chart: Minneapolis to PDK was a little under eight hundred nautical miles, and at the ground speed it was showing, call it two hours forty-five. Somewhere around 2:15. He wrote *leave 12:15* on the pad and underlined it.

At noon the capture job wrote its first scheduled snapshot, on time. The headache had come back behind his eyes, and he took two more ibuprofen with the last of his coffee while he spot-checked a few cameras in the folder against the live feed to make sure they matched. They did. But three of the state camera networks that had timed out that morning were still timing out, and that bothered him. He dug into the first one and found the state had moved its cameras behind a new address sometime in the last year and never updated the old one, so he rewired the connector and watched the frames start to come in. The second one was the same problem with a different fix. He was halfway into the third when he looked up at the clock in the corner of the screen. 12:41.

He left the workstation running with the office door open so it wouldn't get warm in there, grabbed the flight bag, and took Dan's leash off the hook. "Come on buddy, we're going for a ride." Dan was at the door before he was. 

Back out on Old Milton Parkway he headed south toward 400. The eighteen-wheeler was still hanging off the bridge, nothing had changed since he first laid eyes on it, and he drove under it slowly, not liking what was over his head.

Onto GA Highway 400 headed south. A mile south the source of the smoke he had seen earlier that morning came into view, he could make out the remnants of a big rig hauling fuel. That must be what had woke him up at 2 this morning. Most of the cars had drifted off to the side, run into the median wires or off the right into the ditch or all the way into the trees, and the ones still in the lanes sat at angles he had to steer around. He drove it at fifty anyway, then sixty, picking the open lane a quarter mile ahead and changing lanes early, the way he'd have planned an approach. He tried 911 again on the speaker with the same voice and the same answer as earlier, and hung up on it. Then he noticed the gas tank was almost empty... exit 8 had a QuickTrip a few blocks off 400.

The place was lit up and the pumps were on. There were two other cars at the island. A white pickup at pump two with the door hanging open and a man on the concrete beside it, work boots, a lunch cooler on the seat, the nozzle latched in his tank above him where he'd left it. A gray sedan at pump five, a woman behind the wheel with her head against the window. John checked them both. Nothing. Dan watched from the back window, quiet.

He pulled up to pump eight and filled his car like normal. The pump took his credit card and turned on, the numbers ticking up, ordinary as any Tuesday, with two dead people twenty feet away. His mouth was dry and he still couldn't get a full breath, and he hadn't thought to bring anything to drink. He went into the store for water.

The lights were on, the coolers still humming along the back wall, country music playing low over the speakers. He went straight to the cooler and grabbed three water bottles, and drank half of one standing there with the door open. "Hello? Anybody here?" Nothing.

A sound behind him made him jump out of his skin, a flat, cheerful voice from the side aisle: "Caution. Cleaning in progress." He whipped around. A floor scrubbing robot, disc shaped, knee high, was working its way up the aisle, brushes turning, a thin sheet of water and cleaner out ahead of it. It said the line again, unbothered, aimed at absolutely nobody, bumped the base of a shelf, backed off a couple inches, corrected itself and kept going. John stood there out of breath, stuggling to get a deep breath. It was the most normal thing he'd seen since six that morning, a machine doing exactly the job it was built for.

He found her on the floor behind the counter, wedged between the register and the cigarette rack. Twenties, maybe younger, in a QuickTrip polo a size too big for her. He checked anyway. Her eyes were open, and there was dried blood under her nose. Cold, like everyone else that day. "So sorry," John found himself saying out loud. He left a five on the counter for the water and went back out to the car.

Back on 400 every stalled car he passed was a life gone... the girl on the floor, the two at the pumps, the man in the ditch, Harry, the silence was a lead into a deep lonliness. He checked FlightAware again. DAL27PDK was over southern Illinois, still pointed southeast, an hour out. John was twenty minutes from PDK.

At the Perimeter he took the ramp onto 285 east, and the stalled cars thinned out, and he opened it up. Sixty, seventy, swinging wide around a minivan nosed into the median, a box truck on its side across the right two lanes. Twice he took the shoulder, gravel rattling in the wheel wells, Dan braced on the back seat with his nails dug into the upholstery.

Past the Ashford-Dunwoody exit the road bent left under an overpass, and on the far side of it a sedan sat dead in the middle lane. He was doing seventy-five. He stood on the brake and felt the pedal buzz under his foot as the ABS chattered, the tires hissing, the car sliding a little sideways before it caught, and Dan went off the back seat into the footwell with a yelp. He got it stopped about a car length short. He sat there a second with both hands on the wheel, breathing hard, not getting enough air, looking at the back of the sedan and the shape of somebody slumped against the driver's window.

"Sorry, buddy," he said. Dan climbed back up onto the seat. John went around the sedan and brought it back up to sixty, and kept it there.

He came off at Peachtree Industrial and went south toward the field. He had made this drive most Saturday mornings for twenty years, to the hangar and the 182 and an hour or two in the air with nobody needing anything from him. He knew the gate code, he knew the people at Epps, he knew where the radios were on the ops desk. The traffic light at the next intersection was cycling green to yellow to red over an empty intersection. He slowed, looked left and right at nobody, and went through it on red, wishing to be pulled over.
