# 9/5/2028 — John Lauer, AI Engineer & Researcher, Day 0

A thud woke him. Distant and heavy, enough to rattle the bedroom window in its frame, and gone before he was fully awake. The clock read 2:05. He lay in the dark and waited: a siren, a second thud, anything that would tell him what he'd heard. Nothing came. The regular sounds of the house, and down the hall tags shifted as the dog resettled. John rolled over and went back to sleep.

He woke again at six thirty, same as every morning. Sixty-one years old and alone for sixteen of them, though not really alone. There had always been a dog, and it had always been a Mountain Cur, the current one named Dan.

He woke with a dull headache behind his eyes. Two ibuprofen, a hot shower, then down to what he called first breakfast, the same every day: a blueberry, avocado, beet, and spinach concoction he called a brain shake. Gray light came in the kitchen window. The days were getting shorter, and real light wouldn't come until 7:15. Then Dan's two-mile morning walk. He tried not to think about work on the walk, just the dog and the cool air and first light. Dan's tail was going hard enough to thump the mailbox post before they'd cleared the driveway.

The neighborhood was quiet. More cars in the driveways than on a normal Tuesday, which was strange the day after Labor Day, when a short week usually had everyone out early. At the corner where the Pattersons' kids caught the 7:15 bus, there were no kids and no bus. He checked his watch: just past 7:20. He kept going past his usual turnaround, and the quiet held all the way to where his subdivision met Old Milton Parkway. Old Milton hadn't been quiet at any hour in years. He stood at the curb and listened, a hum, maybe, but mostly a silence he was not used to.

"Hmm," he said out loud. It was his default response he made when the inputs on a problem hadn't added up to anything yet, his own version of the spinning circle on the screens he looked at all day. To the north, a hundred yards up the blind curve, a car sat nose-down in the drainage ditch with its lights on and he could just make out that the only sounds he was aware of were in that direction. It must have just happened. He walked up the hill fast, and at the top he was out of breath and couldn't get a deep one. The rear wheels were spinning in the air above the ditch. He put Dan in a sit a few yards back, went to the driver's door, and pulled it open.

A man about forty sat nearly upright, held there by the shoulder belt, his eyes open. Behind John, Dan's ears went flat and a thin whine started in his chest.

John leaned across the man's knees, found the start button, and shut the engine off. In the quiet that followed he pressed two fingers to the side of the man's neck. His hand was steadier than the rest of him. No pulse. The skin was cool, almost cold. This had happened hours ago, not minutes. The man's left hand hung down beside the seat, and the fingers had gone a flushed pink, like a man come in out of the cold. Not quite what John would have expected.

He backed away from the car and called 911 with Dan pressed against his leg.

It answered on the first ring, and for a second he took it for a person: a woman's voice, warm and unhurried, with a small breath before the question. "Nine-one-one, what's the address of your emergency?" Then the rhythm registered, too even, sterile, and he knew what he was talking to. He'd been on the edges of the decisions about how 911 centers would use AI agents to triage the queue while the human dispatchers were tied up. 

He replied: a man, deceased, alone in a vehicle off Old Milton Parkway just north of 400. It asked if he was sure. "Yes." Whether the man was breathing. "No." Whether John was safe where he stood. "Yes." "I'm so sorry," it said. "That's a hard thing to walk up on." He felt the small, stupid pull of being understood by it. Call volume was higher than usual, it told him, and a unit would be dispatched as priority allowed. Would he stay near the vehicle?

He asked for a person. Gently, it said it was an AI assistant handling the queue while the dispatchers were on other calls, that everything he'd told it was logged and would reach them, and that it would stay on the line as long as he liked. It stayed kind and steady and told him the same thing in slightly different words. He hung up.

He walked south toward 400, over the next rise, expecting a version of this morning that made sense. A road crew. A cop waving traffic around a wreck. The silence made him very aware of his own sounds: his pulse in his ears, his boots on the sidewalk, Dan's tags clinking at every step.

Old Milton passed under 400. An eighteen-wheeler had jackknifed across the southbound lanes and folded against the bridge rail. The tractor had gone through the rail and hung nose-down over the road below, held by nothing he could see. Every car he could see on 400 was stopped, no brake lights. On an ordinary Tuesday at this hour, this stretch moved more people past this one spot in a minute than lived on his whole street. Farther south, a column of black smoke billowed into the sky.

"Hmm," he said again, and pulled up the news on his phone. The headline feed hadn't moved. The top story was six hours old with no update. He switched apps: the same frozen feed. A third. The local station's live page held a single paused frame, an anchor caught mid-word. Dan leaned hard into his leg and whined. John turned for home and walked fast, leash short, not quite running.

Back in the subdivision he heard a dog at the Morrisons', barking steady and hoarse. Another one farther down, behind a fence. A small one, high and frantic, inside a house with the blinds still down. Dan pulled toward the Morrisons' door, and John shortened the leash and kept him moving.

Back at the house Dan went under the kitchen table and stayed there. John opened the laptop. The same frozen feed, wider: the national wires, the overseas outlets, the social platforms that should have been a roar of overnight noise. Every one of them sat still, the newest timestamps all from a few minutes before two in the morning. Nothing anywhere had posted since.

He opened FlightAware. At this hour on a weekday there should have been thousands of airplanes on the map. The map was nearly empty. The whole world was down to a scatter of targets, most of them long tracks over open ocean, and a few were wrong in a way he couldn't place. He clicked into one: an Air India 777, Delhi to Newark, past Newark by better than four hundred miles at thirty-nine thousand feet and still headed south, somewhere over the Carolinas. He checked another, and it had done the same thing, reached its destination and kept going.

He pulled up a handful of state DOT traffic cameras: the interchange where 400 met the Perimeter, then a few in other cities. The same scene he'd seen for himself was on every camera he looked at. Open lanes at what should have been solid rush hour, a scatter of stopped vehicles, nothing moving.

At 8:30 he logged in to his standing sync with his group at Google, the public-sector team he ran engagements for out of the D.C. office. Five minutes went by, then ten, and nobody else joined. He sat looking at his own video tile, pale, jaw set, mouth thin, and left the meeting.

He started calling people: his brother Mark first, then down the list of everyone he talked to in an ordinary week. Mostly voicemail. He left a few messages. Some numbers rang and rang. He never got a busy signal.

He set the phone down. Through the kitchen window he could hear the Morrisons' dog, still going. Dan lifted his head off the floor and watched him.

"Harry," he said out loud.

He left Dan in the house and went next door. Harry had been his friend for twenty years. He was a good friend, and he had gotten John through the worst of it after Naomi. He rang the bell and waited. Nothing. He knocked. Nothing. The front door was locked. He went around to the garage and tried the code Harry had given him a few months back, and the door went up. "Harry? You here?" Nothing. Through the kitchen, the living room, down the hall to the master bedroom. "Harry?"

Harry was in bed, on his side, the blanket at his waist. John checked for a pulse. His friend was dead.

He stood there a minute, the house around him, tidy as Harry always kept it. He pulled the blanket up over Harry's shoulder and let himself back out through the kitchen and across the strip of grass between the two driveways.

Dan met him at the door like it had been a week. John went back to the laptop and pulled FlightAware up again, wider this time, clicking through the targets one at a time for origin and destination. The map was still down to a scatter, most of it the long-haul tracks over open ocean he'd half-noticed at eight, each icon crawling forward on a heading that never changed. He was ready to write the whole screen off when one track stopped him.

An airplane doing something different. He clicked into it. **DAL27**, an Airbus A350-900 out of Atlanta at 11:35 the night before, filed for Seoul. The track ran north-northwest out of Atlanta, over the Midwest and Manitoba and up into northern Saskatchewan, as straight as all the others. Then at 3:30 in the morning it turned. A clean turn to the south, and twenty-five minutes later a second turn, onto a line that ran straight for Edmonton, Alberta, and then a descent. The track ended on the ground at Edmonton International at 4:36 Eastern. It hadn't moved since.

He sat back. Every other airplane on the map was holding a heading. This one had turned and landed.

He zoomed in on Edmonton, then out again, looking at everything around it, there was another plane that had departed Edmonton at 7:31 Eastern, three hours after the A350 landed, it was on a southeast heading. He clicked on it. A Pilatus PC-12, a single-engine turboprop, Canadian-registered. FlightAware showed no flight plan for it, no destination. But the flight ID, the line where an airliner would show its flight number, didn't read like a registration. It read **DAL27PDK**.

He read it three times. DAL27, the airplane on the ground at Edmonton. PDK, DeKalb-Peachtree, his own home field, where his 182 had been sitting in its hangar since Sunday afternoon.

FlightAware was a patchwork. A lot of its ground coverage came from hobbyists, thousands of people with a thirty dollar ADS-B receiver and a little antenna in a window or an attic, feeding it over their home internet. Not many of them had antennas in northern Saskatchewan. So some of what he was looking at was coming from a satellite, or an airline feed, or FlightAware filling a gap with its best guess. John had spent his career analyzing data feeds like this, and he knew a line on a map could just as easily be a caching bug as a real airplane.

He opened a chat window on the laptop. His job at Google gave it reach into a lot of things. For years his team had built Gemini deployments for the agencies that run the systems under all those maps... the FAA, DOT, Coast Guard, NOAA, Homeland Security, FEMA. That meant read-only access to their live feeds same as the DOT cameras he'd pulled up earlier. He asked the model to check both tracks against the FAA's own surveillance data. It came back in under a minute. The A350 was real: the FAA's radar and ground stations had it from the gate at Atlanta to the Canadian line under the flight number and the transponder address Delta had registered, and the satellite returns in the same feed carried it from there `[verify: FAA feed includes space-based ADS-B over Canada]`. The PC-12 was real too, and it was a different airplane, its transponder address registered in Canada to a PC-12 NGX. The FAA's ground stations in North Dakota had only just started hearing it, still north of the border. Its flight ID had been changed from the registration about the time it started its engine. Both the 350 and the PC-12 were squawking 7700.

7700, general emergency. John knew the code, four digits somebody has to key in by hand on the transponder. A flight ID was the same, somebody typing it in letter by letter. Somebody had landed that A350, gotten out of it, found a smaller airplane on the same field, and typed in where he'd come from and where he was going so anyone watching would know.

He did the math. Edmonton to PDK was seventeen hundred nautical miles. The pilot would have to stop for fuel somewhere on the way. Call it six and a half, seven hours with the stop. Two in the afternoon, give or take.

He had Dan's leash off the hook and Dan was already at the door before he stopped. It was ten after nine. PDK was forty minutes away on a bad day. He'd be sitting at the field for four hours watching a dot on a phone, he could use this time to get a better understanding of what was going on.

He hung the leash back up. Dan looked at it, then at him, and went back under the kitchen table.

The power was on, the internet was up, the agency feeds were answering. None of that was going to last. He knew enough about how it was all held together to know that much, and he knew his own access ran on tokens and certificates somebody had to renew. He didn't know how long he had.

He took the laptop down the hall to his office, where the workstation sat against the far wall, the one Google had issued him when he moved to the public-sector group, running a build of the model locally instead of in a data center. He woke the screens, pulled a legal pad over, and wrote a list the way he would have at the start of an exercise. *1. What is happening, and how widespread is it? 2. Are there other survivors? 3. How did this happen? 4. Why am I alive?*

He had numbers in his phone from years of exercises with the agencies, the ones they handed out the first morning and told you to keep. FEMA's operations center in D.C. The FAA's Command Center out in Warrenton, Virginia, the room that ran traffic flow for the whole country. The Coast Guard's National Command Center in Washington.

The FEMA line rang until it dropped. The FAA's picked up on a recording that asked him to hold for the next available specialist, and he held for five minutes listening to it loop. He'd stood in that room in Warrenton. Rows of specialists at their consoles, the whole country's traffic up on the wall in front of them, a place that never closed. He knew how many people it took to staff a shift in there. "Please continue to hold," the recording said, and started over. He sat with the phone against his ear a while after he'd stopped listening to it, and when he finally hung up he felt his hands trembling. The Coast Guard line rang and rang. Under the first question he wrote: FEMA, FAA, USCG. Nobody.

The Coast Guard had run a continuity drill a few years back, two days pretending the D.C. data centers were gone, and he'd spent the whole first morning of it finding out that half of what people counted on came off servers nobody in the room could name. So he started where that drill had taught him to. He had the model walk every connector his credentials reached and ping each one. It took a few minutes. The FAA's surveillance feeds answered, NOAA's weather and the beacon alerts out of Suitland, the space weather alerts out of Boulder, the USGS earthquake feed out of Golden. The orbital element sets the Space Force published for everything in orbit `[verify: Space-Track account]`. The automated notices NASA pushed out to observatories, gamma-ray and neutrino alerts meant for telescopes, open to anyone who registered for a key `[verify: SNEWS alerts distributed via GCN]`. The Coast Guard's ship tracking, the state DOT camera networks state by state, the Energy Information Administration's hourly grid tables, and the frequency readings a university lab in Knoxville collected off sensors plugged into ordinary wall outlets across the country. A handful of state camera networks timed out; he had it note which ones and keep trying them. Everything he touched was something his credentials already let him read. He checked that twice.

Then the capture. He'd sat in on a GridEx round once `[verify: Google public-sector participation in GridEx]`, the scenario a grid going down hard across half the country, and by the second day the camera feeds and the telemetry on the big wall displays were gone, and a room full of smart people were arguing from memory about what they'd seen the day before. He'd been saying it at the front of rooms ever since... collect first, while the pipes are still open. Analyze later. He put his hands on the keyboard, and by the end of the first line they were steady again. He wrote out the job for the model the way he'd have written a spec for his team: every hour on the hour, a snapshot of every feed that answered, written to the workstation's local drives, each hour in its own folder, nothing overwritten. He had it time a full pass first. Twenty-two minutes, most of it waiting on slow state camera servers, so he set it to hourly. That left room for a pass to run slow without stepping on the next one, and it was fine enough to catch anything that moved on purpose. He checked the free space on the drives against an hour's worth of data, a few gigabytes, mostly camera frames, and the drives would hold years of it.

Then he pointed it backward. The flight-tracking and AIS services kept history, and so did the EIA's tables, the beacon logs, and the alert streams, so the model could go back and fill in the hours already gone, one snapshot per hour from 2:00 a.m. on. He watched the folder names stack up in the directory list, 0200, 0300, 0400. In the 0200 folder the file from NASA's observatory alerts was bigger than that feed's files for every hour after it put together. He noted it and didn't open it. The cameras kept nothing. Their record would start with the first pass, at 9:40 that morning, and everything before that existed only in what he'd seen on his walk.

He didn't stop to look at what the feeds were showing him. He knew himself well enough to know that once he started looking he'd go too deep and wouldn't come back up for hours. Looking could wait. Whatever answered the first question would be on the drives.

He looked at the pad again. He couldn't answer any of the four this morning, and whatever answered them later would have to come out of what he saved now. So he went after everything else that lived on somebody else's server and would be gone when that server went dark. He started with the FAA's charts, the current cycle: every approach plate in the country, the Chart Supplement with every airport's runways and frequencies and fuel, the sectionals and the IFR enroute charts, onto the workstation and onto the iPad he flew with. The FAA's download site was slow, then fine.

Then maps. He'd watched a FEMA tabletop in a Crystal City hotel ballroom play out a Gulf hurricane, and the part that stuck with him was the field teams in the scenario sitting in their trucks with laptops and no way to load a map once the cell towers went down. He pulled a full OpenStreetMap extract for North America, fourteen and a half gigabytes compressed, and the open-source software to draw it, maps that would work with no signal at all.

Then the manuals and how-tos, everything he could think of that he might need to fix or run with nobody to call. The service manual for his generator. The 182's maintenance manual. The Army's survival and field manuals, which had always been public. EPA guides on treating water. The Merck Manual's free online edition, page by page, for the day somebody got hurt with no doctor to call. Manuals for radios he didn't own yet. He kept adding to the list on the pad as he went, and every time he crossed one off he thought of two more.

Somewhere in the middle of it Dan came down the hall and lay across the office doorway with his chin on his paws, where he could see him. After a while he got up and put his nose under John's hand on the desk, and John scratched behind his ears without looking away from the screen until Dan went back to the doorway and lay down again.

Then the agency data he was cleared for. The Homeland Security infrastructure datasets, every power plant, hospital, airport, fuel terminal, and dam in the country as points on a map. The public version had been shut down a few years back; the restricted one still took his login and the data-use agreement a DHS sponsor had signed for his team. NOAA's latest forecast runs. The EIA's tables on every generating plant. He glanced at the grid numbers while they came down, since they were the thing he understood best. His own corner of the Southeast was still posting every hour, an hour or so behind the clock the way the EIA's tables always ran `[verify: EIA-930 posting lag]`, and the Knoxville sensors had the frequency still sitting near sixty. He'd seen enough in that GridEx round to know it wouldn't stay there with nobody running the plants. He thought about the generator in the side yard and the propane tank next to it, and wrote *generator* at the bottom of the pad, for later.

He copied everything onto two external drives, and when they were done he put one in his flight bag.

He almost didn't think of the photos. Twenty-some years of them, his and Naomi's, all of it sitting on a server somewhere like everything else. He started the download and let it run.

Somewhere in there he made himself a sandwich he ate standing up in the office doorway, and tried Mark again. Voicemail. He didn't leave another message.

He kept FlightAware open on the second screen the whole time. At a quarter to eleven DAL27PDK slowed over the Twin Cities and stopped at Minneapolis-St. Paul. He went back to work and checked it every few minutes, and at eleven-thirty it moved again, climbed out, and settled on a heading southeast. He measured it on the chart: Minneapolis to PDK was a little under eight hundred nautical miles, and at the ground speed it was showing, call it two hours forty-five. Somewhere around 2:15. He wrote *leave 12:15* on the pad and underlined it.

At noon the capture job wrote its noon snapshot, on time. The headache had come back behind his eyes, and he took two more ibuprofen with the last of his coffee while he spot-checked a few cameras in the folder against the live feed to make sure they matched. They did. But three of the state camera networks that had timed out that morning were still timing out, and that bothered him. He dug into the first one and found the state had moved its cameras behind a new address sometime in the last year and never updated the old one, so he rewired the connector and watched the frames start to come in. The second one was the same problem with a different fix. He was halfway into the third when he looked up at the clock in the corner of the screen. 12:41.

He took Dan's leash off the hook. "Come on, buddy, we're going for a ride." Dan was at the door before he was. 

Back out on Old Milton Parkway he headed south toward 400. The eighteen-wheeler still hung off the bridge exactly as he'd left it, and he drove under it slowly, not liking what was over his head.

He took 400 south. A mile down, the smoke he'd seen that morning was what was left of a fuel tanker, burned down to the frame, the trees along 400 were on fire it was spreading toward the buildings beyond. That may have been the thud at 2:05. Most of the cars had drifted off to the side, into the median cables, off the right into the ditch, a few all the way into the trees, and the ones still in the lanes sat at angles he had to steer around. He drove it at fifty anyway, then sixty, picking the open lane a quarter mile ahead and changing lanes early. He tried 911 again on the speaker. Same voice, same answer. He hung up. He had meant to get gas on the way back home on Sunday, but had blew it off, now the gas light was lite up. Exit 8 had a QuickTrip a few blocks off 400.

The place was lit up and the pumps were on. There were two other cars at the island. A white pickup at pump two with the door hanging open and a man face down on the concrete beside it, the pump handle still in the gas tank. A red Honda at pump five, a woman behind the wheel with her head against the window.

He pulled up to pump eight. The pump took his credit card and turned on, the numbers ticking up, it was comforting to have something working like normal. John checked the man and then the woman, both dead. Dan watched from the back window, quiet.

He was thirsty and hadn't thought to bring anything to drink. He went into the store for water.

The lights were on, the coolers still humming along the back wall, country music playing low over the speakers. He went straight to the cooler and grabbed three water bottles, and drank half of one standing there. "Hello? Anybody here?" Nothing.

A flat, cheerful voice from the side aisle: "Caution. Cleaning in progress." He spun around with the water bottle in his fist. A floor-scrubbing robot, disc-shaped, knee-high, was working up the aisle, brushes turning, a thin sheet of water and cleaner out ahead of it. It said the line again to nobody, bumped the base of a shelf, backed off a couple of inches, corrected, and kept going. John stood there working for a breath he couldn't quite get. 

He found her on the floor behind the counter, wedged between the register and the cigarette rack. Twenties, maybe younger, in a QuickTrip polo a size too big for her. He checked anyway. Her eyes were open, and mouth wide open. Her skin was cold, like everyone else that day. "So sorry," John found himself saying out loud. He checked FlightAware at the next straight stretch. DAL27PDK was over southern Illinois, still pointed southeast, an hour out. John was half an hour from PDK.

Back on 400 he stopped looking into the cars he passed. He took the ramp onto Interstate 285 east, and the stalled cars thinned out, and he sped up. Sixty, seventy, swinging wide around a minivan nosed into the median, a box truck on its side across the right two lanes. Twice he took the shoulder, gravel rattling in the wheel wells, Dan braced on the back seat with his nails dug into the upholstery.

Past the Ashford-Dunwoody exit the road bent left under an overpass, and on the far side of it a sedan sat dead in the middle lane. He was doing seventy-five. He stood on the brake and felt the pedal buzz under his foot as the ABS chattered, the tires hissing, the car sliding a little sideways before it caught, and Dan went off the back seat into the footwell with a yelp. He got it stopped about a car length short. He sat there a second with both hands on the wheel, breathing hard, in the sedan there was the shape of somebody slumped against the driver's window.

"Sorry, buddy," he said. Dan climbed back up onto the seat. John went around the sedan and brought it back up to sixty, and kept it there.

He came off at Peachtree Industrial and went south toward the airpot. He had made this drive most Saturday mornings for twenty years, to the hangar and the Cessna 182, the daily walks with Dan and the weekend flights helped balance out the demanding focused concentration of his work. Peachtree Dekalb airport had become the place he interacted most with others. In the past 16 years, he had many meals at both the restaurants at the field, one above the fixed based opertion, Epps, called the Downwind, and the 57th Fighter group down at runway 3.
