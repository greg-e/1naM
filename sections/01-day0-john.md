# 9/5/2028 — John Lauer, AI Engineer & Researcher, Day 0

The sound woke him at 2:05 a.m., a distant thud that rattled the window in its frame and was gone before he'd fully woken up. He lay there a minute in the dark, waiting for something — a siren, a repeat, anything that would tell him what he'd just heard. Nothing, just the ordinary sounds of the house around him, Dan, his dog, tags shifting faintly somewhere down the hall as he resettled himself. He rolled over and faded back to sleep.

He woke again at six, same as every morning: sixty-one years old, alone the last 16 years, well not alone, there was always a dog, not just any dog, always a Mountain Cur. 

Morning routine... hot shower, then down to what he liked to think of as first breakfast — the same every day, a blueberry-avocado-beet-spinach concoction he called a brain shake. Early dawn light shone through the kitchen window; the days were getting shorter and real light didn't come until 7:15. Necessary but always enjoyable was getting Dan out for their 2 mile morning walk. He intentionally tried to not think about work, and just enjoy Dan and the fresh air and first light. The dog's tail was already going dangerously fast back and forth before they'd cleared the driveway. Happy dog, happy day.

The neighborhood was quiet, more cars in driveways than a normal Tuesday — half the street working from home, maybe. Yesterday had been Labor Day, things were usually a bit more busy, a short week compressed schedules. At the corner where the Pattersons' kids caught the 7:15 bus like clockwork, there were no kids and no bus, he double checked his watch, it was just past 7:20. He kept going, past his usual turnaround, and the stillness held all the way to where his subdivision met Old Milton Parkway. Old Milton Parkway hadn't been genuinely quiet at any hour in years, since it was expanded to a four lane divided highway. So quiet, he had a eerie remembrance to a day 27 years ago when the skies had been cleared because of the attack by islamic terrorists.

"Hm," he said, out loud — the flat working hum he made when the inputs on a problem hadn't added up to anything yet, like the processing circle that annoyed him on the screens he looked at everyday. He walked out to the edge of Old Milton Parkway. To the north, a hundred yards off on the blind curve, a car sat nose-down in the drainage ditch, lights on, and he could just make out its engine running high and uneven — a car in a ditch with the engine still going, no emergency vehicles, no sirens, must have just happened. It took him a couple minutes fast walking up the hill. The engine was at a fast idle, the rear wheels spinning free in the air above the ditch, turning and turning against nothing. He had Dan sit and wait a few yards back. He reached the door and pulled it open.

A man, sixty-odd, sitting almost upright, held there by the shoulder belt. No blood, no broken glass — nothing about him matched a car in a ditch. Dan, ears flat, a thin whine building in his chest.

John leaned across the man's knees, found the ignition, and shut it off. The quiet that came was total. He pressed two fingers to the side of the man's neck. His hand was steady — steadier than the rest of him had any right to be. No pulse, no rise in the chest, the skin cool even cold — this had happened a few hours ago, not a few minutes ago. Around the time, some back part of his mind noted, of the thud that woke him, not sure this would have been loud enough to wake him up he thought.

He backed off from the car and called 911, Dan pressed against his leg.

It answered on the first ring, and for a second he took it for a person — a woman's voice, warm and unhurried, with a small breath before the question. "Nine-one-one, what's the address of your emergency?" Then the rhythm registered, a hair too even, no room behind it, and he knew what he was talking to. He had been on the periphery of the decisions about how 911 centers would use AI agents to triage the queue while human dispatchers were tied up on other calls. He had sat in the rooms where deployments like this one were argued line by line. None of those demos had sounded this good.

He gave it a flat, clean report — a man, deceased, alone in a vehicle off Old Milton Parkway just north of GA Highway 400. It asked if he was sure. It asked whether the man was breathing, and whether John was safe where he stood. "No," he said, and "No," and then, "Yes." "I'm so sorry," it said. "That's a hard thing to walk up on." He felt the small, stupid pull of being understood by it. Call volume was higher than usual, it told him, and a unit would be dispatched as priority allowed. Would he stay near the vehicle?

He asked for a dispatcher. Gently, it said it was an AI assistant handling the queue while the dispatchers were on other calls, that everything he'd told it was logged and would reach them, and that it would stay on the line as long as he liked. He asked again — an operator, then, a person. It stayed kind and steady and told him the same thing in slightly different words.

He walked south, toward Georgia Highway 400 the next bend over the hill — some part of him still insisting there'd be a version of this morning that made sense a little further on. A road crew. A cop waving traffic around a wreck. 

Old Milton Parkway passed under 400. An eighteen-wheeler had jackknifed across the southbound lanes and folded against the bridge rail. The tractor had gone through the rail and hung nose-down off the edge, out over the road below, held by nothing he could see. Every car on 400 he could make out was stopped — no brake lights. On an ordinary Tuesday at this hour this highway moved more people past this one spot in a minute than lived on his whole street. Further south there was a plume of smoke rising high into the sky.

"Hm" he said again outload, he pulled up the news on his phone. The headline feed hadn't moved. He checked the timestamp on the top story: six hours old, no update. He switched apps. The same frozen feed. A third. The local station's live page held a single paused frame, an anchor caught mid-word. Dan leaned hard into his leg and whined, John turned and walked home fast, leash short, not quite running. 

Back at his house, Dan curled up under the kitchen table. He moved to the laptop. The same frozen feed, wider: national wires, overseas outlets, the social platforms that should have been a roar of overnight noise and instead sat still, every timestamp clustered in the hours before 2:00am. Nothing anywhere had moved since. 

He opened FlightAware a flight tracking app. This time of day there would be thousands of flights being tracked worldwide. The airspace over the country was mostly empty. The whole global map was down to a scatter of aircraft, most of them long tracks over open ocean. A few were wrong in a way he didn't quite understand. He clicked into one: a FedEx 767, Memphis to Jacksonville, a fifty-minute run, now four hundred miles out over the Atlantic and still going — well past its destination. He checked another, the same, routes that would never be flown like this.

He pulled up a handful of the state DOT traffic cameras — the interchange where 400 met the Perimeter, then a few in other cities. Every one showed the same thing he'd walked through that morning: open lanes at an hour that should have been solid rush hour, a scatter of stopped vehicles across them, nothing moving. Whatever had happened, it wasn't just what he had put his eyes on.

He logged in to his 8:30am online standing sync meeting — his group at Google, the public-sector team he ran engagements for out of the D.C. office. The meeting time ran past five minutes, then ten, and no one else joined. He sat looking at his own video tile — pale, jaw set, mouth thin. John left the meeting.

He started calling people — his brother Mark first, then down the mental list of everyone he talked to in an ordinary week. Voicemail mostly; he left a few messages. Some numbers just rang and rang, no busy signal anywhere. 

He set the phone down. The house was very quiet. Dan lifted his head off the floor and watched him. 

"Harry" he said outloud. He left Dan in the house and went next door, Harry had been a friend for 20 years... not just that guy over the fence always giving advice about the lawn, a real friend who had helped John through many tough times.  Rang the door bell, waited a minute, nothing... knocked... nothing.  Tried the door and it was open, stepping in "Hello? Harry? are you here?" nothing. Walking through the living room to the kitchen, no Harry... down the hall to the master bedroom, "Harry?" There he was in bed, check pulse, his friend was dead.

He stood a minute in the doorway of a dead man's bedroom, the house around him exactly as tidy as Harry always kept it. He pulled the blanket up over Harry's shoulder and let himself back out through the kitchen and across the strip of grass between the two driveways.

Dan met him at the door like it had been a week. He went straight to the laptop he pulled FlightAware back up, wider than he'd looked at eight, studing patterns and checking each aircraft information to see orgination and destination. The map was still down to a scatter, most of it the same long-haul tracks over open ocean he'd already half-noticed — each icon still crawling forward in real time holding a heading that never varied, never corrected, never slowed. He was ready to write the whole screen off the same way when one track stopped him.

An airplane doing something different. He clicked into it. **DAL27**, an Airbus 350-900 out of Atlanta, filed for Seoul, and somewhere over northern Alaska, seven hours into that routing, it had turned around. A hard bank off the great-circle track and a new heading laid out clean and deliberate, arrowing back southeast across Canada.

FlightAware wasn't the real source, it was a patchwork. A lot of its ground coverage came from hobbyists, thousands of people with a thirty dollar ADS-B receiver and a little antenna in a window or an attic, feeding it over their home internet. Nobody had an antenna in a window on Alaska's North Slope. So that turn was coming from a satellite, or an airline feed, or FlightAware filling a gap with its best guess. John had spent his career taking apart pipelines like that, and he knew it could just as easily be a caching bug as a real airplane. All the page showed him was the turn, no squawk, no status, just a line on a map.

He opened a chat window on the laptop. His job at Google gave it reach into a lot of things. For years his team had built Gemini deployments for the agencies that run the systems under all those maps... the FAA, DOT, Coast Guard, NOAA, Homeland Security, FEMA. That meant read-only access to their live feeds same as the DOT cameras he'd pulled up earlier. He asked the model to check the track against the FAA's own sensor data. It came back in a few seconds: same tail number, same turn, so it was real, not a bug. And one more thing FlightAware didn't show, the flight was squawking 7700.

7700... general emergency. John knew the code the way any pilot does, four digits somebody has to key in by hand on the transponder, it was not automatic. Someone had turned that airplane around, and someone had changed the squawk, and someone was still flying it. Every other airplane on the map was just holding a heading. This one was making decisions. That didn't add up, not with everything he'd walked through since 7:00 this morning.

He did the math in his head: departed a little after eleven the night before, turned back sometime around three thirty, so it had been flying home for over five hours already and was somewhere over the Great Lakes now. If they were coming back to Atlanta it would be here in an hour and a half, two at the most.

John grabbed Dan's leash off the hook, "Come on buddy, we're going for a ride," Dan was already at the door. He had the car running before he'd really decided if driving to Hartsfield made any more sense than calling people had... it did. It was the first thing all morning that felt like doing something.

Back out on Old Milton Parkway he headed south toward 400. The eighteen-wheeler was still hanging off the bridge, nothing had changed since he first laid eyes on it, and he drove under it slowly, not liking what was over his head. A mile further south was the local firehouse. Somebody on duty had to be alive... A ladder truck and two ambulances sat in the garage with the bay doors open. He left Dan in the car with the windows cracked and walked into the building. "Hello? Anyone here?" Silence, a flat, dense silence with no echo in it. Two uniformed firefighters were on the kitchen floor, both cold, no pulse. Upstairs there were eight more, all in their bunks, and he checked each one to be sure. All gone, all cold. Shivers went up his back. "Ok, John, just keep going." His voice sounded flat against the immense silence of the room.

Onto GA Highway 400 headed south. It was slow going, most of the cars had drifted off to the side, run into the median wires or off the right into the ditch or all the way into the trees. He stopped to check a couple, though he already knew, and tried 911 again with the same voice and the same answer as earlier. Then he noticed the gas tank was almost empty... exit 8 had a QuickTrip a few blocks off 400.

The place was lit up and the pumps were on. There were two other cars at the island. A white pickup at pump two with the door hanging open and a man on the concrete beside it, work boots, a lunch cooler still on the seat, the nozzle latched in his tank above him where he'd left it. A gray sedan at pump five, a woman behind the wheel with her head against the window and her phone in her lap. John checked them both, the way he had all morning. Nothing. Dan watched from the back window, quiet.

He pulled up to pump eight and filled his car like normal. The pump took his credit card and turned on, the numbers ticking up, ordinary as any Tuesday, with two dead people twenty feet away. He could use something to drink, and more coffee would be good, so he went into the store.

The lights were on, the coolers still humming along the back wall, country music playing low over the speakers. He poured a coffee without really thinking about it, cream, no sugar, the same as always. He grabbed a couple water bottles. "Hello? Anybody here?" Nothing.

A sound behind him made him jump out of his skin, a flat, cheerful voice from the side aisle: "Caution. Cleaning in progress." He whipped around. A floor scrubbing robot, disc shaped, knee high, was working its way up the aisle on whatever schedule somebody had set once and never touched again, brushes turning, a thin sheet of water and cleaner out ahead of it. It said the line again, unbothered, aimed at absolutely nobody, bumped the base of a shelf, backed off a couple inches, corrected itself and kept going. John stood there with his heart still going too fast. It was the most normal thing he'd seen since six that morning, a machine doing exactly the job it was built for.

He found her on the floor behind the counter, wedged between the register and the cigarette rack. Twenties, maybe younger, in a QuickTrip polo a size too big for her. He checked anyway. Cold, like everyone else that morning. "So sorry" John found himself saying outloud.

Back on 400 every stalled car he passed was a life gone... the firehouse, the girl on the floor, the two at the pumps, the man in the ditch, Harry, all of it stacking up behind him the same way the traffic wasn't. He checked FlightAware again, DAL27 still on course for Atlanta, over Kentucky now, forty minutes out, maybe less. John was close to an hour from Hartsfield. He didn't have much time.
