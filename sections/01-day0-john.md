# 1 — John, Day 0

The sound woke him at 2:05 a.m., a distant thud that rattled the window in its frame and was gone before he'd fully woken up. He lay there a minute in the dark, waiting for something — a siren, a repeat, anything that would tell him what he'd just heard. Nothing came. No sirens, no distant alarm. Just the ordinary sounds of the house around him, Dan, his dog, tags shifting faintly somewhere down the hall as he resettled himself. He decided it was a transformer, or somebody's car doing something stupid out on Old Milton Parkway, rolled over and faded back to sleep.

He woke again at six, same as every morning: fifty-eight years old, alone the last 16 years, well not alone, there was always a dog, not just any dog, always a mountain cur. 

Morning routine... to the bathroom, hot shower, then down to what he liked to think of as first breakfast — the same every day, a blueberry-avacado-beet-spinach concoction he called a brain shake. Early dawn light shown through the kitchen window; the days were getting shorter and real light didn't come until 7:15. Necessary but always enjoyable, next was getting Dan out for their 2 mile morning walk. He looked forward to this, he intentionally tried to not think about work, and just enjoy Dan and the fresh air and first light. The dog's tail was already going dangerously fast back and forth before they'd cleared the driveway. Happy dog, happy day.

The neighborhood was quiet, more cars in the driveways than a normal Tuesday — half the street working from home, maybe. Yesterday had been Labor Day, he'd had Monday off, today was the morning everything went back to normal. At the corner where the Pattersons' kids caught the 7:15 bus like clockwork, there were no kids and no bus, double checked his watch, it was just past 7:20. He kept going, past his usual turnaround, and the stillness held all the way to where his subdivision met Old Milton Parkway. Old Milton hadn't been genuinely quiet at any hour in years. So quiet, John was always aware of airplanes flying over, his morning walk with Dan was always highlighted by the expected hum of a propellor or the sound of a biz jet on approach to Peachtree Dekalb airport or Lawrencville Municiple. Nothing, quiet below and above.

"Hm," he said, out loud. Then, "Ummm" — the flat working hum he made when the inputs on a problem hadn't added up to anything yet, a noise standing in for a conclusion he didn't have. He walked out to the edge of the parkway. In both directions something was wrong. To the north, a hundred yards off on the blind curve, a car sat nose-down in the drainage ditch, lights on, and he could just make out its engine running high and uneven. To the south a box truck had stopped square in the median and blocked the rest of the view that way. He went north first — a car in a ditch with the engine still going, someone might need pulling out. It took him a few minutes. The closer he got the clearer it was: the engine pinned at a fast idle, the rear wheels spinning free in the air above the ditch, turning and turning against nothing. He had Dan sit and wait a few yards back. He reached the door and pulled it open.

A man, sixty-odd, sitting almost upright, held there by the shoulder belt. One hand had slid off the wheel into his lap. No blood, no broken glass — nothing about him matched a car in a ditch. Dan, ears flat, a thin whine building in his chest.

John leaned across the man's knees, found the ignition, and shut it off. The quiet that came in behind it was total. He pressed two fingers to the side of the man's neck. His hand was steady — steadier than the rest of him had any right to be. Forty years back he'd worked the overnight grill at a Waffle House off North Avenue to get himself through Tech, and the one thing that job had left him with was this: panic and competence could run at the same time, the hands finishing the work while the rest of a man came apart. No pulse, no rise in the chest, the skin cool in a way that meant hours, not minutes — this had happened a few hours ago, not a few minutes ago. Around the time, some back part of his mind noted, of the thud that woke him.

He backed off from the car and called 911, Dan pressed against his leg.

It answered on the second ring, and it wasn't a person — a calm synthetic voice already mid-sentence about elevated call volume and estimated response times. He knew the cadence of it he had been on the perifery of decisions about how 911 centers were implementing AI agents to triage the queue when the human dispatchers were on other calls. He had sat in the rooms where deployments like this one were argued line by line. He gave the synthetic voice a flat / clean explanation — a man, deceased, alone, in a vehicle off Old Milton Parkway just north of GA Highway 400. The voice thanked him and said a unit would be dispatched as priority allowed. He asked for a dispatcher. It repeated the line about call volume. He asked again — an operator, than - how about a person, *is there anyone actually there?* — and it looped cleanly back to the top of its script and started over.

He went back south and crossed to the box truck in the median — a white contractor's rig, nose against the cable barrier. The driver was slumped the same way the first man had been, belt on, no mark on him. John opened the door and checked to be sure what his thoughts were already telling him... dead. "Ummm," he said, to no one.

He kept walking. South, toward the next bend — some part of him still insisting there'd be a version of this morning that made sense a little further on. A road crew. A cop waving traffic around a wreck. Dan pulled back against the leash the whole way and John kept coaxing him on.

The parkway passed under GA Highway 400. An eighteen-wheeler had jackknifed across the southbound lanes and folded against the bridge rail. The tractor had gone through the rail and hung nose-down off the edge, out over the road below, held by nothing he could see. Every car on 400 he could make out was stopped — no brake lights. On an ordinary Tuesday at this hour this highway moved more people past this one spot in a minute than lived on his whole street.

He stood there until Dan leaned hard into his leg and whined, then turned and walked home fast, leash short, not quite running. 

Back at his house, Dan curled up under the kitchen table. John pulled up the news on his phone. The headline feed hadn't moved. He checked the timestamp on the top story: nine hours old, no correction stacked on it, no update layered over it the way a real morning's coverage always thickened by six. He switched apps. The same frozen feed. A third. The local station's live page held a single paused frame, an anchor caught mid-word, standing in for a broadcast that wasn't happening.

He moved to the laptop and widened it out, methodical the way thirty years of building safety-critical systems. Never trust one sensor. Get a second source, a third, and see where they disagree before you believe any of them. National feeds, international ones, the social platforms that should have been a roar of overnight noise and instead sat still, every timestamp clustered in the hours before 2:00am. Nothing anywhere had moved since.

He opened FlightAware a flight tracking app. He knew what that map was supposed to look like at eight on a weekday. The airspace over the country was empty. The whole global map was down to a scatter of maybe fifty aircraft, most of them long shallow arcs over open ocean. A few of the arcs were wrong in a way he didn't quite understand. He clicked into one: a FedEx 767, Memphis to Jacksonville, a fifty-minute run, now four hundred miles out over the Atlantic and still going — well past its destination. He checked another, the same, routes that would never be flown like this.

One track was very different, a Delta flight out of Atlanta, well east over the North Atlantic — its line bent hard north and west off its great-circle route to Paris, running down toward the Newfoundland coast, and then, an hour back by the timestamps. The marker was crawling as he watched now, circling low and slow over a field in the interior. He refreshed once, twice; the icon kept circling, then steadied onto a final and came down, the altitude and groundspeed readouts stepping down in the clean orderly way of a controlled landing, and then slowed and turned off the runway and began to taxi. Gander, the label read, once he zoomed in far enough to catch the code. An Atlanta–Paris run would fly an augmented crew, more than two up front. Somebody who knew exactly what they were doing had put that airplane on the ground there, on purpose, in the last ten minutes. It was one specific, deliberate fact in a morning that had given him nothing but absence, and he sat looking at it. *Somebody's still making decisions out there. Whatever this is, it's not nothing.*

He pulled up the state DOT traffic cameras next. He'd spent years on the government side of these systems, and that work taught you the roads showed you trouble before anyone got around to announcing it. He'd seen one stretch of 400 with his own eyes. It went everywhere. By this hour 400 and the Perimeter and every artery between them should have been thick with rush hour, brake lights strung for miles. Instead the freeways sat nearly empty, a scatter of vehicles across lanes that should have been packed solid, and none of them were moving. One camera near a Perimeter interchange caught three or four cars nosed together mid-lane, doors shut, the image too coarse to explain itself. Another, near his own exit, held a box truck stopped across two lanes, no flashers, nothing stacking up behind it because there was nothing left to stack. He sat very still in the kitchen chair and did the arithmetic his own commute had drilled into him a thousand times. Not a light morning. A road that should have been full and wasn't, except for the cars that were never going anywhere again.

He kept his 9:00am standing sync meeting — his group at Anthropic, the public-sector team he ran engagements for out of the D.C. office. The meeting time ran past five minutes, then ten, and no one else joined. He sat looking at his own video tile — pale, jaw set, mouth thin - the thing that had been rising in him all morning finally arrived and had a name. He'd worked disaster scenarios his whole career, on paper, for money. He had never once been inside one, never tasted the reality of it.

He started calling people, working down the mental list of everyone he talked to in an ordinary week. His brother Mark first. Then friends. Then coworkers he barely knew, on the theory that somebody, surely somebody, would pick up. Voicemail mostly, he left messages, some just rang and rang, no busy signals,over and over, until he'd worked through nearly every name he had and the silence started feeling like the answer.

He set the phone down. The house was very quiet. Dan lifted his head off the floor and watched him, and John scooted back from the table and Dan stood and John held Dan's head in his hands rubbing his ears, "What are we going to do dog?". 

"Harry"... he left Dan in the house and went next door, Harry had been a friend for 20 years... not just that guy over the fence always giving advice about the lawn, a real friend who had helped John through many tough times.  Rang the door bell, wait a minute, nothing... knocked... nothing.  Tried the door and it was open, stepping in "Hello? Harry? you here man?" nothing. Walking through the living room to the kitchen, no Harry... down the hall to the master bedroom, Harry? There was Harry in bed, check pulse, just like the others... gone.

Back to the house, Dan at the door waiting. Something came up out of his chest then — not the flat working hum from the roadside, the one that stood in for a conclusion he didn't have. This one had words in it. He hadn't made a sound like it since Naomi died, sixteen years ago, and he heard himself say it into the dog's coat, quiet, with nothing behind it but need: "Oh, God. Please help me."
