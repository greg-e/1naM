# 5 — Sam, Day 1

That same Wednesday morning, an ocean and most of a country northeast of Marietta, Sam woke not knowing where he was. It lasted a second and a half before all of it came back and settled onto his chest like a weight — the airport hotel at Halifax, third floor, a stranger's turned-down bed he'd gone into with his shoes on. Gray light around the blackout curtain. He took inventory the way he would have after any bad night in any crew hotel: he'd slept, harder than he'd expected, long enough that his body had stopped arguing with him. That was the only thing he'd gained since the flight deck, and he wasn't going to waste it lying here.

He got up, drank from the tap until he wasn't thirsty, looked at himself in the mirror for exactly as long as that took. Somewhere in the night it had gone from a question to a plan, and he woke already holding it: he was going to fly home. Not wait, not for a rendezvous, not for anyone. Marietta, the house, Elena and Maya, who hadn't answered a phone in a day and a half and whom he was going to go and find himself, because it was the one thing left that he could put his hands on.

The phone rang while he was pulling his shoes back on.

He almost didn't believe it. He'd had the thing in his pocket for two days as a piece of dead weight that occasionally rang out into nothing. He looked at the screen — a number he didn't know, Atlanta area code — and answered it on the second ring, and a voice said hello like it had been waiting a long time to.

A man named John. In Atlanta. Calm in the specific, effortful way of someone working very hard at it. He'd watched Delta 84 turn around over the water and come down at Halifax, and he'd spent a day and a half since then getting from that track to Sam's name and Sam's number — into Delta's own records, which should not have been possible and which Sam found he could not make himself care about. They talked fast and over each other, neither believing there'd be a second call. And near the end of it Sam heard himself ask the thing he'd woken up planning to do alone: John was twenty minutes from the house. Sam was a day of flying away from it. Would he go — not call, go, and look, and say what was true. John said yes before he'd finished the question. Elena. Maya. He gave the address twice, and made John say the numbers back, and then told him the thing that mattered most: *whatever you find, tell me straight. Don't soften it.* The line thinned and dropped.

Sam sat on the edge of the bed for a minute with the dead phone in his hand. The plan had changed under him in the space of one call. He wasn't flying home to search a house anymore. He was flying to Atlanta because someone there was alive and had asked him to come, and because the searching was going to be done for him by the time he could land.

He found the crew wardrobe off the hotel's back corridor and traded the slept-in uniform shirt for a plain gray henley and a fleece off a housekeeping rack, folding the epaulets into his flight bag rather than leave them on the floor. Then he went to find an airplane.

His A330 sat on the main ramp where he'd left it, already looking abandoned. He didn't look at it long. The general aviation apron was off the far end of the terminal — PC-12s, a couple of King Airs, a row of the province's water bombers waiting on a fire season nobody would call this year. King Airs or PC-12s. He thought about the King Airs longer — two engines, real redundancy — but managing twin engines and a heavier panel alone, today, didn't sit right. The PC-12s were simpler. Single engine, single lever, forgiving. Five of them in a row like the world had set them aside for him.

Getting into one was easy; getting one running was not. Two had unlocked cabin doors — nobody had bothered locking rentals on a quiet Monday night — but no keys in them, and a PC-12 wasn't a car he could hotwire on stubbornness. He forced a cracked side window on the FBO, a low prefab marked GATEWAY / PAL AEROSPACE, and found a pegboard of key fobs behind the counter, each tagged with a tail number in a dispatcher's careful hand. It took longer than he wanted to admit, matching tags to the registrations on the tails outside, before he had one — a well-kept airframe near the end of the row, tie-downs cinched tight, an airplane somebody had loved.

The office lights were on, the wall clock right, a space heater still ticking under the desk. The power was up because some mix of the big hydro up north and the coastal wind was carrying it — the parts of a grid that don't need a person in the room — and it would hold until the rest of the network dragged those down too. Not long. Long enough today.

The walk-around was clean — tires, control surfaces, fuel caps, the belly. A step stool from the tool room reached the wing of something that sat this low; he'd moved a few hundred thousand pounds of Jet-A around the world and never once held the nozzle, and he noted that from a long way off while he found the fuel. The bowser by the farm was locked tight, but a second key ring on the pegboard, tagged GROUND EQUIPMENT, turned it over on the third try. He fueled by eye against the tank markings, the better part of an hour, hands smelling like kerosene after. He didn't mind. It kept his head full.

He pulled weather before he committed — clear the whole way down the seaboard, a weak front well south of anything he'd fly — and worked the chart the way training had built into him. Halifax to Atlanta was too far to fly alone and rattled in one leg, and he didn't want long legs today; he wanted short ones, with the ground coming back up under him often enough to keep him honest. Bangor first — the old transatlantic gateway, first US soil, a runway a mile and a half longer than he'd need and fuel he could count on. Then a middle leg down to Washington, and the last one home to PDK. Three hops, all of them inside daylight if he got moving. He got moving.

The first leg was the easiest flying of the day, and he knew going in that it would be. He ran the checklist out loud to no one, the way he had the morning before in a much larger airplane, lifted off into a bright cold Atlantic morning, and let the autopilot carry the parts he didn't have the attention for while Nova Scotia and then the folded green of Maine went by underneath. Bangor came up out of the haze exactly where the chart said it would, the long runway unmistakable from thirty miles out. He flew a slow inspection pass, saw nothing on it, and put the airplane down soft and taxied to a self-serve pump that took a card without asking any network for permission.

He had four bars on the ground. He called John from beside the airplane, the engine still ticking as it cooled, and John picked up on the first ring like he'd been sitting there holding the phone.

"Tell me," Sam said. No hello. He'd known since somewhere over the Gulf of Maine — the long quiet with nothing to do but fly and think — that Marietta had stopped being a place he might still find them and turned into a place John was going to confirm.

John didn't make him wait for it. "They're gone, Sam. Both of them. In their beds, in their sleep — no mark on either of them, nothing knocked over, nothing that says they ever woke up. I checked them myself. I wouldn't be telling you this if I hadn't."

"Okay," Sam said, when he could. Then again, because the first one hadn't done anything at all: "Okay."

Neither of them tried to fill the silence. There was a little more before the end of it — John was driving to PDK, he'd be there by evening with the dog and the one machine he said might still find other people; Sam had two legs left and would be in tonight — and then the connection thinned out and dropped for the last time. Sam set the phone on the glareshield and sat in the cockpit of a stranger's airplane on an empty ramp in Maine and let the thing catch up with him that he'd been outrunning since the flight deck went quiet. He didn't time it. There was no one to hold it together for — no radio, no checklist, no one in the right seat. When it passed he wiped his face on the borrowed fleece, topped the tanks the rest of the way, and walked the airplane around again, slower than it needed, because his hands wanted a job.

The middle leg he flew mostly on the autopilot with the sun swinging around toward the nose. New England, then the long gray sprawl of the northeast corridor, every city under him lit in patches and stopped. He came into Washington over a river with the monuments standing white and untended along it and put the airplane down on a runway built for a hundred times its size, fueled, checked the oil and the chip detector out of a caution he couldn't have explained and found both of them clean, and got back in the air inside the hour. One leg left.

The last of the light was still on the land when PDK came up ahead of him — low sun, long shadows off the hangars, the field as quiet as every other field he'd touched that week. He flew the inspection pass anyway, out of the habit two decades of flying had built and this week had only hardened, and on the base turn he saw it: a single car parked nose-on to the approach end of the runway, and beside it, small at this distance, a man standing next to it with a dog.

After two days and an ocean and the whole seaboard flown alone, there was someone down there waiting for him to arrive. He rolled out onto final with his hands not quite steady on the yoke.
