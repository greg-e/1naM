# 4 — John, Day 0

The house took Sam differently than it had taken John twelve hours before — not the settling-back-in of somewhere familiar, but a stranger's first look at a life he'd landed in the middle of. He stood a moment in the front hall while Dan ran a lap of greeting around both their legs, taking in the framed photos on the stair wall, the quiet of a house built for one person and a dog.

"Sit," John told him. "I mean it. You've been standing up since eleven last night."

Sam sat. John went to the kitchen and did the only thing he currently knew how to do with his hands that wasn't dangerous — eggs, the last of Monday's sausage, more of it than either of them would probably finish. He'd caught himself shaking once already that morning, standing over a dead man in a ditch, and he wasn't going to let it happen again with a guest in the house who had far more reason to be shaking than he did. They ate at the counter, mostly quiet, Dan working a bowl of his own on the floor between them.

"Okay," John said, when the plates were down to scraps. "Come see the thing that found you."

His office was the last bedroom down the hall, a room that had given up pretending to be anything else years ago. Against the far wall, under its own quiet cooling, sat the workstation the company had issued him when he moved to the public-sector group — a squared-off case no bigger than a two-drawer file cabinet, drawing more power than the rest of the house combined, running locally and off the network a build of the model his own team was still developing. He was cleared to have it. It came with a toolchain built for evals — connectors into aviation telemetry, weather, the government data feeds his clearances let him touch.

"This is what showed me your track turn around this morning," John said, waking the second monitor. "It's also, as of about eight this morning, running with its safety refusals stripped out, paired with the real thing — the model my company actually ships — still answering on the API because somewhere, some data center's still standing on backup power. I did that last night, before I knew your name, so I could break into Delta's own systems and find out who was flying that airplane. Then you turned around and made the whole plan redundant by just landing here." He said it flat, no attempt to make it sound better than it was, and watched Sam's face for what came back.

Sam was quiet a second. "You'd have hacked an airline to find me."

"I'd already started."

"Good," Sam said, and there was nothing performed in it. "I'm glad somebody was willing to do the ugly thing. I spent yesterday finding out there's nobody left to ask permission from."

That was the whole of it, as far as either of them needed to relitigate. John turned back to the screen.

They spent the afternoon on the actual size of the thing — not John alone this time, working it in a locked room with only a dog for company, but the two of them at the desk, John driving and narrating, Sam leaning over his shoulder with a coffee going cold in his hand. State DOT camera feeds, one metro at a time: Atlanta, which both of them had now seen firsthand, and then Charlotte, Nashville, St. Louis, Dallas, Denver, Phoenix, the whole spine of California, every one of them the same still photograph scaled up or down — cars stopped at wrong angles across empty lanes, nothing moving, nothing burning. Maritime AIS: hundreds of ships still under way in every ocean, transponders reporting position and course and speed, not one of them answering a hail — a shadow fleet steaming nowhere. "I know that feeling," Sam said, low, watching the map fill in with drifting hulls. Distress beacons worldwide: a handful of activations in the last twenty-four hours, each one a dot that meant somebody had been alive and scared enough to reach for a switch. Sam looked at the scatter of them a long moment without saying anything, and John didn't ask what he was thinking.

The sky itself was down to a scatter by then, most of it long shallow arcs over open ocean — flights that had launched off the far side of the world in the last hour before it happened and still had fuel because they'd been built to cross half the planet without stopping. Delta 27 wasn't among them anymore; it was parked and dead on a runway forty minutes south, the first exception either of them had found all day, and they both understood without saying it that it wasn't really an exception, just the one flight somebody had lived to fly home.

Last, the grid — his own corner of it, because the grid had a clock running on it and the clock had his house near the middle. He pulled the telemetry and had the model run the collapse forward instead of just watching it, and Sam leaned in to watch it with him. North America wasn't one grid, it turned out, but four — separate synchronized zones tied together by nothing sturdier than a handful of DC links — and each would fail on its own clock, not all together. Individual plants would start tripping within hours as fuel handling and unwatched faults caught up with them one at a time; then, zone by zone, frequency would sag past the threshold that held the lights on at all, and each interconnection would go down hard and fast once it crossed that line — minutes, not days, once it started. Inside a day, maybe less, nearly the whole country would be dark, in patches too small and too random to plan around — an accidental pocket here and there where the math happened to balance, nothing worth counting on finding twice. What actually mattered wasn't where the lights stayed on. It was what happened after they went out at a nuclear site with nobody left to watch it: the reactors would scram themselves safe, same as always, and sit on backup power with a second, slower clock now running underneath the first — one that had nothing to do with the grid at all. His own patch of Georgia sat near two of those reactors, and the model's map came back a bad color, with a date on it. He wrote the date down and didn't look at it again yet.

"That's not abstract to you," Sam said, watching him write the date down on the pad without comment.

"No," John said. "It's not." He didn't say anything else about it, and Sam let it sit, and they moved on, because there was nothing to be done about a date yet except know it was coming.

It was the smaller thing, sideways, that mattered most. John told him about the first 911 call, that first morning — a calm synthetic voice reciting call volume and response times while a dead man sat slumped in the seat ahead of him, no human being anywhere in the loop because every dispatcher in every center in the country was as gone as everyone else. He'd written it off in the moment as a dead end. It wasn't. That voice hadn't just answered him. It had answered *everyone* — every person anywhere in the country who'd been frightened enough that morning to dial three digits, and every one of those calls was still sitting in a log on some server nobody was watching.

"I wasn't the only one who tried," John said. "That's the whole thing. If the agent logged mine, it logged all of them."

Sam sat with that a second, turning his cup on the counter. "You're about to do to every 911 system in the country what you were going to do to Delta."

"Yes."

"Then do it," Sam said. "I flew two hundred and seventy people most of the way to Europe with a dead crew and a full cabin and I didn't ask anyone's permission to turn that airplane around either. Some decisions don't need a vote."

John set it running that evening — not one instance on one thread but a dozen, each reaching into a different state's patchwork of county and regional dispatch systems, filtering for the thing that actually mattered. Not who called. Who *pushed back*. Who argued with the reassurance, who refused the script, who hung up angry and scared and alive. Then he left it to grind, the fans in the back bedroom settling into a steady hum that would run all night.

They ate again around eight, simpler this time, and didn't talk much. Somewhere in the quiet after, John found himself in the hallway outside Naomi's study, and went in the way he sometimes did without quite deciding to. Sixteen years and he'd never made it into anything else — her books shelved the way she'd shelved them, the reading chair, the rolltop desk he'd bought her their second year in the house. Her Bible sat where it always sat, soft at the corners, the ribbon still marking wherever she'd left off. He opened it there. Isaiah, a verse underlined in pencil and gone over again in yellow, the way she used to mark the ones she kept coming back to. *Fear not, for I am with you; be not dismayed, for I am your God. I will strengthen you, I will help you.* He read it twice, closed the book, and carried it out to the duffel by the front door without examining too closely why.

Sam was on the couch when he came back through, Dan sprawled half across his lap like he'd already decided this one belonged to him too. "We're going to need an airplane," Sam said, before John had even sat down. "Mine's sitting dead on a runway at Hartsfield, and even if it weren't, you don't run a rescue out of a widebody built to cross an ocean. Something smaller. Something that can get into the kind of strip your three leads are probably going to turn out to be near, once we have them."

"PDK's twenty minutes from here," John said. "There's a Pilatus dealer on the field. I've walked past the showroom a hundred times."

"Then that's where we go once we've got something worth flying to." Sam looked toward the hallway, toward the low steady hum of the machine still working. "How long's it going to run?"

"All night, probably. It's not fast. The systems it's reaching into were never meant to be reached into like this, and half of them are already going dark on their own." John sat down heavily in the chair across from him. "We should sleep. Whatever it finds will still be there in the morning."

Sam didn't argue, which told John more than anything he might have said. There was a guest room down the hall that had never once had a guest in it; John pointed him to it and took the couch himself, close enough to hear the machine's fans if anything changed. Dan settled across his feet, the workstation churned on in the dark, and outside the window a country that had been running for two hundred and fifty years without anyone at all thinking about it kept sitting exactly as still as it had since two o'clock that morning.
