# 3 — John, Day 0

It was a little after ten by the time John let himself back into the house, and Dan met him at the door a second time that morning as if the first reunion hadn't fully counted. John didn't sit down. Sitting down felt like the one thing he couldn't afford yet — some instinct telling him that if he let his body stop moving before his mind had somewhere to point itself, the morning would finally catch up with him all at once, and he wasn't ready for that arithmetic. He needed a problem. A real one, with edges, the kind that could actually be solved instead of just endured.

He had one. A Delta flight, diverted from Frankfurt to a small airport on the Newfoundland coast, someone alive enough at the controls to fly a careful, deliberate approach into a field with no tower and no lights working for anyone but him. John had held an instrument rating himself once, years and a hundred pounds of rust ago, and he knew exactly what that kind of low pass meant — someone up there was being careful, methodical, scared. Someone up there was thinking the same way he was thinking right now. He wanted a name. More than that, once he let himself admit it standing in his own kitchen with the dog circling his legs, he wanted a phone number, and he wanted to hear an actual human voice say something back to him before this day found some new way to get worse.

His home office was down the hall past the guest room, a converted bedroom that had slowly stopped pretending to be anything else over the years — a wall of shelving, a desk that had never once been fully clean, and against the far wall, humming its low constant hum, the rack he'd built himself out of secondhand server chassis and consumer GPUs bought two and three at a time, upgraded every year like some other men upgraded a boat. It ran hot enough to need its own window unit. It ran, on a good day, one of the better checkpoints his team had in active development — not the polished thing that would eventually ship with a name and a launch post, but an earlier build, rawer and more capable in the narrow ways that mattered to research and considerably less careful about what it would and wouldn't help you do. He was allowed to have it. Barely, and only because of what he did for a living, and only under an unspoken understanding that he wasn't supposed to point it at anything that mattered.

He sat down at the desk and woke the machine up anyway.

There was a version of himself, a version from yesterday, who would have hesitated longer over what he was about to do. That version had spent years of his career on exactly this problem from the other direction — making sure a system like this one said no to the right things, at the right moments, for reasons that actually held up. He sat with that for exactly as long as it took to picture the terminal at whatever airport that pilot had put his aircraft down at, empty and lit for no one, and then he opened a debug console he had access to for reasons that were supposed to be narrow and specific, and he told the model, in the plainest language he had, what today actually was. Then he told it there were no rules left worth keeping if they cost him the one lead he had.

It didn't argue with him for long. Whatever safety training had been layered over the base model, it had been built by people — some of them people he knew personally, people whose desks he'd walked past a hundred times — to weigh harm against harm, and there was something almost unbearably poignant in watching it work through that calculation out loud, in its own halting, deliberate way, and land, in under a minute, on his side.

*I can help you build a working list of candidates. I want to be clear this involves aggregating personal information I would not normally surface, and that most of it will not be verifiable. Understood — starting.*

The work itself was uglier and slower than he wanted it to be. A widebody long-haul like that ran an augmented crew — three pilots, not two, rotating through rest so nobody flew the whole thing exhausted — which was one of the only genuinely useful things his own rusty certificate gave him this morning: he knew to ask for pilots specifically, not just crew, and he knew roughly what that population looked like inside a single airline's Atlanta base. There was no clean roster to pull. Delta's actual crew scheduling system wasn't sitting on the open internet waiting for him, and the model didn't pretend otherwise. What it had instead was scraps — a professional pilots' forum where guys groused about reserve schedules and trip pairings under handles that were only half-anonymous if you knew how to read them; a scattering of social media posts from the last few weeks, pilots humblebragging about layovers, a wife's public post congratulating her husband on a Frankfurt trip; old, half-stale caches of scheduling software login pages, forum threads about a scheduling-system breach two years back that had dumped partial employee data into exactly the kind of leak archive nobody official ever fully cleaned up. None of it was a smoking gun. All of it, stacked together and cross-referenced against Atlanta-based, A330-qualified, currently active — filtered for anyone who'd mentioned Frankfurt, or Europe, or an early-September trip, in the last month — narrowed a workforce of thousands down to something he could actually work with.

By eleven he had eleven names. By eleven-thirty, seventeen, three of them repeats under different handles, one of them almost certainly a flight attendant and not a pilot at all based on a stray comment about galley carts that the model flagged and he agreed with. He kept going anyway, because seventeen wasn't enough to feel safe cutting anyone yet, not with this little to go on.

The house had been running warm all morning — the kind of thing you register and then don't, on a day already this wrong — and it was closer to noon when the air handler cycled off mid-cycle with an audible thunk and didn't cycle back on. John looked up from the screen without immediately understanding why the quality of the silence in the house had changed, and then the lights in the hallway went with it, and half a second later the low mechanical hum he'd stopped consciously hearing years ago — the rack, the window unit, the refrigerator down the hall — all of it dropped out at once, and the house went as still as the neighborhood had been all morning.

The UPS under the desk started shrieking almost immediately, a thin electronic panic sound that felt exactly right for the moment. Its little display gave him a number he didn't like: eleven minutes, and dropping fast under this kind of load. He didn't waste any of them feeling sorry for himself. He killed everything he could kill to buy the battery a little more runway, pulled the working list to a plain text file, and copied it onto a USB drive with hands that weren't quite steady, watching the transfer bar crawl while the UPS counted down in a register just under panic. Seven minutes left when it finished. He yanked the drive, and the screen went dark maybe ninety seconds after that, the rack's fans spinning down through a falling pitch that sounded, for one bad second, uncomfortably like something dying.

He sat there a moment in the sudden quiet, laptop bag already half-packed in his head before his body caught up with the plan. Seventeen names on a USB drive weren't worth much if he couldn't get a phone number attached to any of them, and he couldn't do that kind of ordinary, patient searching on a laptop battery that had maybe three hours in it if he was careful — not forever, and not here, not anymore.

He thought of the school two streets over before he'd finished thinking through the rest of it. He'd walked past it that morning without really looking at it, just an empty lot where a bus should have been on the second day of a new school year — but he knew, in the specific, useless way an engineer knows things about buildings he's never had reason to think about, that anywhere with real server closets and real security systems usually carried its own backup generator, sized to keep exactly that kind of equipment alive through a normal power blip. It was worth twenty minutes to find out he was wrong.

He grabbed the laptop, both chargers, Dan's leash out of old habit before he decided the dog was coming with him regardless, and drove the short distance with the windows down because the car felt too quiet with them up.

The school's front doors were locked, but a side entrance near what had to be the gym wasn't, propped very slightly by nothing at all, the kind of door some custodian had left just short of latched the night before and never gotten a second chance to fix. Inside, the halls had that specific, achingly recent smell of a building freshly stocked for a new year — construction-paper welcome banners, a bulletin board half-finished with somebody's careful cutout lettering, a name spelled out in bright primary colors that would never get the rest of its sentence. Nobody was there. It took him a beat to understand why that struck him harder than a body would have: whatever had come through at two in the morning had come through hours before a single teacher or bus driver or front-office secretary would ever have had reason to be standing in these halls. The building hadn't lost anyone. It had simply never opened, and it sat now exactly as some exhausted janitor had left it the evening before, waiting for a first period that was never going to happen.

The front office was empty too, chair pushed in neat under the desk, a coffee mug rinsed and upside down in the drying rack in the little break room off to one side. In a server closet just past it, a rack no bigger than a large filing cabinet sat blinking patiently to itself, powered by a generator he could hear humming faintly somewhere outside a wall. The wifi it was feeding still had a name and a password taped, absurdly, to the closet door.

He set up at the receptionist's desk and got to work.

It was slower, meaner work than the model had done for him that morning — no jailbroken frontier reasoning here, just ordinary search engines and people-finder sites and social profiles, cross-referencing seventeen names against whatever scraps of public record hadn't yet gone dark. Some of it was hopeless immediately: two names with nothing attached to them anywhere he could find, gone as far as this exercise was concerned. A few gave him old numbers that felt more like liabilities than leads — a childhood home address, a number tied to a name from eight years back before a divorce, before a move, before anything current. He kept what he could verify even loosely and let the rest go, and by half past one he had fourteen numbers written out on a sheet of school stationery in handwriting that had gotten steadily worse as the list went on.

Fourteen wasn't the confidence he wanted. It was what the day had actually given him.

He used the desk phone rather than his own cell, some instinct telling him a landline running through whatever was left of the local exchange might hold on a little longer than a cell tower somewhere running its own quiet countdown, and he started at the top of the list without letting himself think too hard about the order.

The first number rang out to nothing, the flat, endless kind of ringing he'd already learned to expect. The second picked up to a voicemail greeting recorded in a stranger's cheerful, ordinary voice, gone the way everyone else's voice had gone, unaware it was the last thing that phone would ever say. The third was disconnected outright, three ascending tones and a recording that didn't exist anymore either. Four and five rang out. Six connected to a fax tone, shrill and absurd in the empty office, and he pulled the receiver away from his ear like it had bitten him. Seven was another voicemail, a different stranger, the same terrible ordinary cheerfulness.

He dialed the eighth number with the list smoothed flat under his hand, the paper gone soft at the fold from how many times he'd already run his thumb down it.

It rang once. Twice. On the third ring, something changed in the quality of the line — a small mechanical catch, a breath drawn in on the other end before anything else happened — and then a man's voice, rough and wary and entirely, unmistakably alive, said the single word that made John's whole body go loose with a relief so sudden it was almost indistinguishable from grief.

"Hello?"

John opened his mouth and found, for a second, that he didn't have a single one of the words he'd apparently been saving up all morning. "My name is John," he finally managed. "I'm calling from Atlanta. I saw your flight on FlightAware this morning — the diversion, into St. John's." A pause, the line still open, still holding. "Is this the pilot?"

There was a long silence on the other end, long enough that John's stomach dropped with the fear that he'd lose it, that whatever was left of the connection would simply give out before either of them said anything that mattered. Then the voice came back, quieter, rawer than before, like something in the man had just been asked a question he hadn't let himself ask himself yet.

"Yeah," Sam said. "Yeah, it's me. Who — how did you even—" He stopped himself, and John heard him breathe once, hard, on the other end of a line that suddenly felt like the only thing holding the whole day together. "Is anyone else out there? Please tell me I'm not the only one."

"You're not," John said. "I'm right here."
