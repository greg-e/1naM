# 4 — John and Sam meet, Day 0

John pulled into the Signature lot at five past ten. The glass doors were unlocked, and he went through the lobby, Dan's leash in hand — back toward the ops counter, where line crews would have coordinated fuel trucks and inbound traffic on any ordinary morning. The radio was there, the display read CH 1 over the FBO's own frequency, and the channel knob stepped through presets: the FBO, ground, the fuel farm. It took him three tries and the manual from the drawer under the counter to find the button that let him dial a frequency by hand. He checked the clock over the counter. 10:14.

A laminated card was taped to the counter beside the radio, the frequencies written in marker: Clearance, Ground, Tower, one line for Atlanta Approach, one for Atlanta Center. He dialed the Approach frequency and keyed the mic. "Atlanta Approach, anybody on this frequency?" Nothing. He dialed the Atlanta Center frequency. "Atlanta Center, Delta Twenty-Seven, anybody?" Nothing.

He pulled the phone out again. FlightAware showed Delta 27 at ten thousand feet, forty miles out, swinging east of the field. On the map the icon was close enough now to tell which way it was pointed. His doubts caught up with him while he watched it. Maybe whoever was flying it wasn't listening to anything. Maybe it was set up on some automatic approach mode he didn't know about, and the 7700 had been the last thing a dying pilot did. Maybe whatever had killed everyone else had just taken longer with him, and John was about to watch the only moving airplane in the country fly into the ground.

He looked at the card again. One line for Approach. But Atlanta Approach worked the sky around the airport in slices, a frequency for each, and the card only gave him the one the ramp here used. He had no way to know which slice the airplane was in, or which frequency its pilot had dialed. There was only one frequency every airplane was supposed to be listening to.

10:19. He dialed in 121.5, the emergency frequency, turned the volume all the way up, and got his thumb on the transmit button.

Before he could press it, the speaker came alive, and John's stomach jumped as a voice filled the empty room.

---

Twenty miles east of the field, Sam had Atlanta Approach on VHF 1 and 121.5 on VHF 2, and on the turn in he keyed 121.5 the way he had every twenty minutes since the North Slope, the words worn smooth by now.

"MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY. Atlanta Approach, any station, Delta Twenty-Seven Heavy, A350. Twenty miles east, descending through seven thousand, landing Atlanta, two-six-right. Fuel remaining one hundred ten minutes. Three hundred twenty-three on board."

He let the key up. Then he pressed it again, and nothing that came out was in the book. "Is there anybody on this frequency."

Then a voice in his headset, on 121.5. A man, unsteady, no callsign. "Delta Twenty-Seven, this is — this is John Lauer. I'm on the ground in Atlanta. I can hear you."

For a second Sam's voice wouldn't work at all. "Say again," he finally got out, rougher than he'd sounded once in seven hours of talking to dead air. "Say again. Who is this?"

Nothing came back for several seconds. Then John's voice again, steadier. "John Lauer. I live up in Alpharetta. I watched your track turn around on a map this morning and drove straight here. I'm at Signature, on the north side. At the ops desk."

"Signature," Sam said. He knew that ramp. Two years of charter work before Delta, in and out of that FBO more times than he could count. "John, I've got a full airplane, and everybody appears to be dead. You're the first voice I've heard since Alaska. I need emergency services to meet the aircraft."

John took a moment to answer. "There aren't any," he said. "There's nobody to call. It's me. Just me and my dog."

The frequency went quiet. Sam heard his own breathing in the headset. "Copy," he said, flatter than he meant it. "Eight minutes out. I'll bring it to Signature. See you on the ground."

Fly the airplane. Out of habit he called Atlanta Tower on its published frequency: "Atlanta Tower, Delta Twenty-Seven Heavy, twelve-mile final, two-six-right." Nothing. There was nobody in the tower to give him the wind. He went back to 121.5.

"John, Delta Twenty-Seven. Can you see a windsock from where you are? Or a flag?"

A few seconds, then John's voice. "Flag out front's hanging almost straight down. There's a windsock past the hangars. It's lifting a little, pointing east."

A light breeze out of the west, then, blowing straight down the runway toward him, the way he wanted it. "Copy. Thanks."

He lowered the landing gear and felt the rumble through the floor as it came down and locked, ran the flaps out to their last setting, and let the speed bleed back to a hundred and forty knots. Four lights beside the runway told him whether he was too high or too low, and they showed two white and two red: right on the glide path. At a thousand feet he clicked off the autopilot, and the airplane was his. He searched the runway ahead for anything sitting on it, a fuel truck, a tug, another airplane that had rolled to a stop in the night with nobody to move it. Nine thousand feet of concrete, empty end to end. The airplane's recorded voice counted the last few feet down for him, fifty, forty, thirty, and at twenty it told him to take the power off, and he pulled the thrust levers back to idle. The main wheels touched with a firm thump he felt through the seat. He lowered the nose, pulled the thrust reversers, and the engines roared while the brakes worked better than four hundred thousand pounds of airplane down to a walking pace. He turned off at the next exit onto the taxiway.

---

John was standing outside Signature's glass doors with Dan at his side when it came out of the haze low and steady, gear down, flaps out. He watched it touch down on 26R, heard the reversers roar and fade across the field. 

The jet came to a stop on the ramp in front of the building, engines spinning down through their whine into silence. John stood outside the doors, looking up at the flight deck windows of the A350, and let himself believe for the first time all day that he was not alone.

Then the forward door behind the cockpit unlatched, pushed out from the fuselage, and swung forward flat against the side of the airplane. A bundle dropped out of the bottom of the doorway and blew open with a roar of compressed gas, and in a few seconds the slide had unfolded itself all the way down to the concrete and settled with a thump. A man stepped into the doorway, better than fifteen feet up. He wore a Delta captain's dark jacket, four gold stripes on each sleeve catching the morning sun, the cap in one fist. He looked the way he would have standing at the flight deck and see three hundred people off at the gate. He stood there a moment looking down at the slide. Then he sat on the lip of it, and let go.

Sam came off the slide, and there was John, thirty yards off next to the FBO, a dog already running toward him: a tall, rangy man in a ball cap and black-framed glasses, a thick brown beard shot through with white, olive drab cargo pants and flip-flops on an airport ramp.

"Should I be concerned?" Sam called out, eyes on the dog.

John was already moving too. "Not at all," John called back. "He's excited to see you, his name's Dan."

Dan got there first, pressing himself against Sam's legs the way he pressed himself against anyone in the family. Sam went down on one knee, patting the dog's side and scruffing his head and ears. John got there, flip-flops slapping the concrete, and Sam got back up to his feet, straight-backed even now. John stood there a second, looking at a face of another living person: clean-shaven, short black hair gone salt-and-pepper at the temples, olive skin, brown eyes rimmed red.

John held out his hand. "John."

Sam took it, and laughed — a short, broken sound that wasn't really a laugh. "Right! my name is Samuel Reyes. Everybody calls me Sam." He held on a second longer than a handshake needed. "Everybody on that airplane is dead. Three hundred and — " Choking, he stopped there and didn't try to finish it.

For the next half hour they stood on the ramp beside Delta 27. Sam gave him the outline — Theresa, Jack, Ruth, the cabin, the turn over the top of Alaska, why he'd flown for seven hours straight back to Atlanta instead of the nearest runway in Kotzebue or Fairbanks. John gave his own outline back — the thud at 2:05, the man in the ditch, Harry, the frozen feeds, the empty sky, the drive to the airport. Somewhere in it Sam said the name Elena, and then Rosa.

"Marietta," Sam said. "Thirty minutes from here. I need — " His voice caught and he pushed through it. "I need to go."

"I'll drive," John said. "Let's go."

They left the airplane sitting where it had rolled to a stop and took John's car north and west into Cobb County a little past eleven thirty. Sam sat rigid in the passenger seat the whole way, watching a city he knew by heart go by in the same wrong stillness John had already spent a morning learning to expect — the cars stopped at their odd angles, the intersections holding a red light for no one, a strip mall with its lot empty and not one person crossing it. Twice he had John pull over — once at a sedan folded around a light pole, once at an eighteen-wheeler jackknifed across two lanes with its trailer doors sprung open onto the shoulder — and got out both times without a word of explanation, crossed to the wreck, and checked it himself. John understood exactly what it was: not disbelief, not procedure, the need to put his own hands on the thing before he'd let himself believe. Sam came back to the car each time a little more hollowed out than he'd left it, and didn't talk much. Twice Sam tried Elena's number and only got voicemail.

The house looked like every other house John had already stood in front of that day — ordinary, undisturbed, a minivan in the driveway parked the way you park it when you expect to need it again in the morning. Sam was out of the car before John had it fully stopped.

Sam went in through the front door at a near run, calling their names, and John came up the walk slower, leaving Dan in the car, and stopped on the front porch. He stood in the open doorway.

It didn't take long. From a bedroom down the hall came a sound John had never heard a grown man make, lower and more ruined than crying. John followed it.

Elena was in their bed, on her side, one arm still reaching toward the nightstand. No mark on her, nothing disturbed, the same gentle, terrible peace John had seen enough times that day to recognize from the door. Farther down the hall, through a door standing half open, in a room with fairy lights strung along one wall, Rosa lay curled the way kids sleep.

Sam was on his knees at the foot of Elena's bed when John reached the doorway, both hands pressed flat against the mattress as if he could hold something down by will alone. John stopped there, the way he'd stopped on the porch.

After a while it eased. It didn't pass; John already understood that grief like this never passed, only loosened its grip for a while. Sam got slowly to his feet and went to Rosa's room. He slid his arms under her, and she came up off the mattress all at once, still curled on her side. Ten hours had set her in the shape she'd fallen asleep in. Sam held her against his chest like that, the way he must have carried her to bed a thousand times when she was small. John stepped back from the doorway to let him through. Sam turned sideways so her feet wouldn't touch the frame and laid her on the bed beside her mother, on her side, facing Elena, close enough that her forehead nearly touched Elena's shoulder. He pulled the blanket up over both of them and stood there. 

John left Sam alone and went back outside.  He got Dan and they walked down the street a few blocks, they came back to Sam's house 30 or so minutes later and John sat down on the steps of the front porch, Dan laid down next to him.

When Sam came outside his eyes were dull and empty and his olive skin was ashened.

"Come back to my place," John said. "We can figure out what's next."

Sam looked at him a long moment. "Okay," he said. "Yeah. Okay. I'll grab some of my things." John went back to the car.

Sam came back a few minutes later with a backpack and locked the front door behind them.

They pulled out of the driveway a few minutes after one, Sam quiet in the passenger seat, Dan's chin resting on the console between them. The drive back to Alpharetta took the better part of forty minutes, through the stopped, silent world, and neither of them said much until John pulled into his own driveway at twenty minutes to two, pulled into the garage and cut the engine. They sat there a minute before either of them moved.

John broke the silence, "Come on. I'll make something to eat."
