# 2 — Sam, Day 0

Delta 27 pushed back from Atlanta at eleven thirty-five, climbing out into a clear night sky on a heading that would carry it up over the Great Lakes, across Hudson Bay, over the top of the world and down into Seoul fifteen hours later — an ultra-long-haul run, one of a handful on the whole airline's map, flown by an augmented three-pilot crew to comply with federal duty-time regulations on a sector too long for two. Sam had the left seat. Forty-three years old, a decade at Delta after two years flying corporate and charter jets out of Atlanta and eight years before that flying C-17 Globemasters for the Air Force, a flight instructor rating to help pay his way through college, ratings added one after another for twenty-five years straight until there wasn't much left to challenge his skills. He'd earned the left seat of an A350-900 the same patient way he'd earned everything else, and he still didn't take it for granted.

The crew tonight was seasoned — first officer Theresa Foreman and relief pilot Jack Sommers, both senior operators in their own right, both good friends, three people who trusted each other with a cockpit the way you only trust people you've flown a few thousand hours next to. Tonight's rotation had all three of them up front through the climb, with each of them peeling off for a rest block over the long, dark middle of the flight before anyone thought seriously about the descent into Incheon. Routine. He'd flown rotations like it more times than he could count, long enough to know their rhythms by heart — a late departure, a quiet cockpit going quieter, a sunrise somewhere over Siberia that he still hadn't gotten tired of.

Sam went down to the crew bunk at half past midnight, not much past top of climb, the first of the three to take his block on a flight long enough to need every hour of rest doled out early and evenly. He left Theresa and Jack up front together. His alarm woke him a little before three to relieve Theresa. He climbed up still working the last of the sleep out of his eyes, already running the next few hours of the flow in his head. The quiet on the flight deck registered before anything else did — not the ordinary quiet of a long night leg. "Hey, guys... sleeping on the job?" Sam said, half a laugh in it. Then he saw them. Theresa, slumped forward against the harness. Jack, the same. Neither of them moved, and there was no response. His whole body went cold. "Hey — stop fooling around, this isn't funny." Quiet. Nothing. No sound, no response.

He checked for a pulse on each of them and found nothing on either — skin cool, they'd been like this a while. Falling back on years of ingrained training, fly first, work out the details after: the airplane was off course a little, drifted while nobody had a hand on it, but flying, and everything in the green.

He walked the length of the cabin, slow, checking rows. "Hello? Anyone?" A pause. "Hey — is there anyone back here?" No response — only an off smell he started to become conscious of, a few rows in. Horror was not an emotion Sam had ever really had cause to feel, not the real thing, maybe some thin echo of it from a movie theater as a teenager. Now something was coming up in him he had no name for. Two hundred and sixty-some passengers and thirteen of his colleagues — gone, dead? He couldn't make himself accept it standing still, so he didn't stand still. Methodically, he went row by row and checked for pulse and breath. After close to thirty checks, as far as he could tell, he was the only living thing left aboard a fully loaded widebody that had been flying itself for the better part of an hour while he slept.

Back on the flight deck, he keyed the radio — company ops, Winnipeg Center, another aircraft on guard, anything. The carrier tone was there, clean, the radio doing exactly what it was built to do. No voice ever answered him, on any frequency he tried. He had to move them. He unbuckled Theresa first, pulled her gently up and out of the right seat and back past the flight deck door into the forward cabin. Then Jack — not easy; Jack went six-three and better than two hundred and thirty pounds, all of it muscle, and it took everything Sam had to get him laid out with any dignity at all. He checked on the lead flight attendants too, up front, more out of thoroughness than any real hope left in him by then.

It had been thirty minutes, start to finish, from the moment he'd woken to the moment he made himself decide. Not because the horror had faded — it hadn't, and wouldn't for a long time — but because twenty-five years of training had built something into him that didn't care how he felt underneath it: when everything goes wrong at once, a pilot's job is to act. Not to sit with it. His job was to fly the airplane.

He thought, first, the way any of it had ever been trained into him: nearest suitable field. He was somewhere over James Bay by then, deep into northern Ontario, with Churchill and Iqaluit and a scatter of other names on the chart closer than home by a wide margin. He got as far as pulling one of them up on the display before the rest of it caught up with him — there was no one left aboard to save by landing fast. No medical emergency ticking down a clock. Whatever this was, it wasn't going to get better in the next hour or worse in the next six. The only thing that mattered now was that Elena and Maya were in Marietta, and Marietta was fifteen hundred miles closer to Atlanta than to a gravel strip on Hudson Bay. He squawked 7700 out of habit, declared an emergency into the same silence that had answered every other call, and brought the big aircraft around until the heading bug read due south and Seoul fell away behind him for good. Home. Not because the manual said so. Because it was the only direction that meant anything anymore.

Then the weight of it caught up with him — the airplane's weight, this time, not his own. Four hours into a fifteen-hour flight, nearly a full ocean crossing's worth of fuel still sitting in the wings and the center tank, tens of thousands of pounds over anything he could put down on a runway without folding the gear. He ran the numbers once, out loud, to no one, and decided against a hold to burn it off — there was nothing to burn it off *for*, nowhere closer worth diverting to, and the flight home would do most of the work on its own if he let it. He set a cruise power that traded speed for fuel burn, watched the gross-weight readout crawl down a long, shallow curve over the hours it took Canada to slide by beneath him, and did the arithmetic again near the border: close, not quite there, close enough that a slow racetrack over north Georgia in the last twenty minutes would finish it. Theresa and Jack lay in the forward cabin the whole way down, and for six hours there was nothing in front of him that needed flying every minute, and nowhere to put his attention except on what had actually happened.

He tried the radios the whole way south — Winnipeg, Minneapolis Center, Memphis, Atlanta Center, company frequency, the emergency guard channel, on and on down the list of anyone who might still be sitting at a console somewhere with a headset on. Nothing ever answered. Somewhere over Kentucky the sun came up behind him, ordinary and indifferent, and he watched it light the wing and thought, distantly, that it was the first sunrise he'd ever flown into feeling nothing about at all.

Atlanta, when it finally rose out of the haze ahead, looked wrong before he could say how. No trails of morning traffic threading the interstates. No haze of ground light left over from the night, because there'd been no city awake to make it. Just the familiar shape of the skyline, the familiar bend of the river, the ordinary geography of a place he'd flown into a thousand times sitting there perfectly still, waiting for him like it always had, and no one in it.

He keyed Guard again on the turn in, the way he had every twenty minutes since Hudson Bay, the words worn down to almost nothing by now. "Anybody — this is Delta Two-Seven, is there anybody on this frequency." He didn't expect anything back. He hadn't expected anything back in six hours of asking.

Something answered.

Not a carrier tone, not the dead-air hum resolving into nothing the way it always had — a voice, thin and startled and entirely human, stumbling over the words getting them out. "Delta Two-Seven, this is — I don't have a callsign, my name's John Lauer, I'm on the ground in Atlanta, I can hear you."

Sam's hand came off the yoke before he caught it. For a second his own throat wouldn't work at all. "Say again," he finally got out, rough, urgent in a way he hadn't sounded once in six hours of talking to dead air. "Say again — who is this?"

The voice came back steadier the second time, like the man on the other end was working just as hard to hold himself together. John Lauer. Alpharetta. He'd watched the track turn around on a map that morning and driven straight to the airport on nothing but the hope of being there when it landed. He was parked on the general aviation side, south field — Signature.

"Signature." Sam said it back and felt something in his chest that had been clenched for six hours ease off half an inch. He knew that ramp — two years of charter work before Delta, in and out of that exact FBO more times than he could count, a life that felt like it belonged to somebody else now. "I know exactly where that is. I'll bring it right to you."

The radio went to dead air a few seconds later, whatever had been holding the signal together finally giving out, and Sam flew the rest of the approach with his hands steadier than they'd been since three o'clock that morning — weight finally down where it needed to be, a reason now to want the ground under his wheels beyond just getting down safe. He set up for Runway 26R the way he could have blind — home base, the one airport on earth he didn't need a chart for. No tower answered his calls. No ground vehicles crawled the ramps. He flew it as a plain visual approach, gear down, flaps full, hand-flying the last thousand feet himself, and the wheels touched Atlanta pavement a little after ten thirty in the morning, smooth, the reversers spooling down to a walk on a runway he'd departed off of less than twelve hours before as a completely different man.

He slowed the airplane to a stop short of the first taxiway, hands still on the controls, engines idling, and keyed the radio once more before he moved another foot. "On my way to you now." Then, working the tiller with the same numb competence that had gotten him this far, he turned off toward the general aviation side of the field, toward the one ramp on this whole dead airport where he knew, for a fact, somebody was standing there waiting for him to come into view.
