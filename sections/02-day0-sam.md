# 2 — Sam, Day 0

Delta 84 pushed back from Atlanta just after ten-fifteen, climbing out into a clear night sky toward Paris — a long-haul route, close to nine hours, flown by an augmented three-pilot crew to comply with federal pilot time regulations. Sam had the left seat. Forty-three years old, a decade at Delta after eight years flying C-17 Globemasters for the Air Force, a flight instructor rating to help pay his way through college, ratings added one after another for twenty-five years straight until there wasn't much left to challenge his skills. He'd earned the left seat of an A330-900neo the same patient way he'd earned everything else, and he still didn't take it for granted.

The crew tonight was seasoned, first officer Theresa Foreman and relief pilot Jack Sommers, both senior operators in their own right, both good friends, they had flown this route together quite a few times in the last 2 years. Tonight's rotation had all three of them up front for departure, with each one taking a break overnight, before the descent into Paris. Routine. He'd flown this exact rotation more times than he could count, long enough to know its rhythms by heart — a night departure, a quiet cockpit, a sunrise somewhere over the Continent that he still hadn't gotten tired of.

Sam had gone down to the crew bunk a little after 1:30am, leaving Theresa and Jack up front together. His alarm woke him a little before 3:00am to relieve Theresa. He climbed up still working the last of the sleep out of his eyes, already running through the pre-descent to Paris flow in his head. The quiet on the flight deck registered before anything else did — not the ordinary quiet of a long night flight. "Hey guys... sleeping on the job?" Sam said laughing. Then he saw them. Theresa, slumped forward against the harness. Jack, the same. Neither of them moved and there was no response. His whole body went cold, "Hey stop fooling around, this isn't funny..." quiet, nothing, no sounds no response.

He checked for a pulse on each of them and found nothing on either — skin cool, they'd been like this for a while. Falling back on years of ingrained training, fly first, then work out the details, the route had drifted and they were off course but flying and all systems in the green.

He walked the length of the cabin, slow, checking rows, "Hello? anyone?" pause, "Hey, is there anyone here?" no response... only an off odor he started to become conscious of. Horror is an emotion Sam had never experienced, maybe a shallow sense of it from watching too many movies in his teens... but now, something was welling up in him he had never experienced. Two hundred and sixty-one passengers and 13 of his colleagues, gone, dead? He couldn't accept that.Methodically he went to each row and checked pulse and breathing... after about 30 checks, as far as he could tell, he was the only thing still alive on a fully loaded A330 that had been flying itself while he had been sleeping.

Back on the flight deck, he keyed the radio, calling for anyone — company ops, Gander Center, another aircraft on the emergency frequency, anything. The carrier tone was there, the radio working exactly as it should. No voice ever answered it, on any frequency he tried. Have to move them... he first unbuckled Theresa and gently pulled her up and out of the left seat and pulled her back past the flight deck to the left first class aisle... then the same for Jack, not easy... Jack was 6'3 and at least 230... all muscle, he pulled Jack back to the right side first class aisle.  He double checked Helen and Robert, the lead flight attendants, thoroughness or last ditch hope.

It had been thirty minutes, from the moment he woke up to the moment he made himself decide. Not because the horror had faded — it hadn't, and wouldn't for a long time — but because twenty-five years of training had built something into him that didn't care how he felt underneath it: when everything goes wrong at once, a pilot's job is to act, not doing nothing, his job was to fly the airplane.

Emergency procedures: get the airplane onto the ground at the nearest field that could take it. Nearest was Sydney, out on the eastern tip of Cape Breton Island — he was maybe ninety miles southwest of it, over the Cabot Strait, close enough that on an ordinary night he'd already have been thinking about the descent. He squawked 7700 and declared an emergency out of habit, into the same silence that had answered every other call, then brought the big aircraft around, watching the heading bug swing until Sydney sat on the nose and Paris fell away behind him for good.

Then the weight caught up with him. Five hours into a nine-hour flight, most of a transatlantic fuel load still in the wings — tens of thousands of pounds heavier than anything certified to touch a runway. Put it down like this and he'd fold the gear or crack a spar. So before he could do anything else he set a hold over the Gulf of St. Lawrence and ran the fuel-jettison checklist alone, out loud, then flew a slow racetrack through the last of the dark, watching the gross-weight readout crawl toward a number he could land at. It took the better part of an hour — close to thirty tons of Jet-A trailing off into the night behind him, nothing to do but hold the turn and wait, Theresa and Jack lying now in the forward cabin where he'd moved them. For the first time since the flight deck had gone quiet there was nothing in front of him that needed flying, and nowhere to put his attention except on what had actually happened.

When the number finally came down he rolled out of the hold and started the approach into Sydney. The sky was going gray by then.

The coast came up wrapped in cloud — not thin morning haze but a solid deck lying right down on the water, edge to edge, the kind of fog these shores grow off the cold current all summer and deep into fall. The field's automated weather was still broadcasting on its own frequency — nobody had switched it off — a flat recorded voice reading him a light wind, an altimeter setting, and a visibility that rounded down to nothing. What he didn't have was a controller to confirm the runway was clear, or anyone to check the machine against. He flew the ILS to Runway 06 coupled anyway — automated readings had been wrong before, and fog could have a hole in it — down to two hundred feet, and at minimums he broke out into nothing at all — no runway, no approach lights, no ground, just the same white pressed flat against the windshield. Sydney was a regional field; its approach put him at two hundred feet and not one foot lower, and it had nothing that could land an airplane out of weather like this. He took the go-around, climbed back into clear air on top, and did the one thing every hour he had ever logged had trained him to do: he did not land.

Halifax was the answer, and maybe had been all along. A hundred and fifty miles southwest down the length of Nova Scotia — a full international airport, long runways, and the one thing that mattered now: Category III equipment, the kind rated to fly an airplane all the way to the pavement with the autopilots coupled and the pilot's hands in his lap. It was the field the whole North Atlantic system kept on the shelf as its transatlantic alternate, and on the fuel he had left it was the only one that could do what needed doing. He briefed the divert out loud to nobody, turned southwest, and let Cape Breton fall away behind him.

He flew the transit in a silence he had never once experienced in this cockpit — no calls from the back, no company frequency, no controller's voice walking him down, no second and third set of hands beside him doing half the work the way they always had before today. Just the engines, his own breathing, his own checklist calls answered by no one, and somewhere below the fear a single question turning over and over without an answer: *what happened, and why am I the only one left to ask it.*

Halifax was fogged in too. Of course it was — same coast, same morning, same gray deck lying on the ground. But Halifax he could land out of it, if the automation held. He set it all up the way the manuals laid out for a hands-off approach he'd drilled a hundred times in the simulator and flown for real maybe twice, both times with a full crew and a working tower and a controller reading him the RVR all the way down. Now the number came off the airport's automated weather, updating on its own every minute, and it sat right at the edge of what the autoland was certified for. He intercepted the localizer for Runway 23 ten miles out, ran the airplane down through the configuration, armed the autoland, and rode it with his hands hovering off the controls — watching the two autopilots hold the beam, hating every second of not being the one flying it. Somewhere around two hundred feet the automated number dropped below the limit, a bank of fog sliding through the touchdown zone as it did, and the airplane did exactly what it was built to do: it powered up on its own and flew the missed approach, climbing away into the murk. Sam let it. It was right, and he was not going to fight a machine that was doing the one thing keeping him alive.

He came back around through the pattern and set up to fly it again. This time the automated report had the visibility back above minimums and the fog had shifted off the field. Same localizer, same glideslope, same coupled descent, flaps full, the autopilots locked in and the runway somewhere under all that cloud. He crossed the threshold still in it. Then the main gear was down — a firm, automatic touchdown he hadn't commanded — the nose settling, the spoilers coming up, the reversers waking symmetrically, the airplane rolling itself straight down a runway whose far end he still couldn't see. It slowed to a walk before he took it off the autopilot at last and steered it clear onto a taxiway, alone, the terminal a gray shape off to one side and not a single light moving anywhere on the field.

The touchdown was almost anticlimactic after everything — a machine setting an airplane onto pavement that had been sitting patient and unused since long before he'd turned around. He taxied in on instinct more than plan, no ground control to direct him, and brought the aircraft to a stop on a wide stretch of the main apron, the terminal building dark off to one side, only the emergency lighting still burning behind the glass.

Shutdown took longer than it should have, his hands running checklists out of habit while the rest of him sat frozen, staring out at an apron with no vehicles, no ground crew jogging out to meet an airplane that had just declared an emergency and landed itself with a single pilot aboard. Nobody came. He hadn't really expected anyone to, not after the last several hours, but some old, stubborn part of him had held out anyway, and watching that hope not pay off was its own small, separate grief layered on top of everything else.

There was no way to bring a jet bridge to the door — no ground power, no one to marshal one into place even if the systems had somehow still been running. He sat with that problem for a minute, working through the aircraft's own limited options, and landed on the only real answer available to him: the emergency slides. He armed the forward door, braced himself the way training told him to even though there was no one behind him to brace with, and popped it.

The slide deployed with a hiss and a thump, unspooling down onto the tarmac in the gray Nova Scotia morning, and Sam stood in the open doorway a long moment before he made himself step out onto it and ride it down alone — an absurd, undignified way to arrive somewhere, he thought distantly, for a captain who'd just flown two hundred and seventy-some people most of the way to Europe.

He landed hard on the tarmac and sat there a second, catching his breath, the aircraft looming huge and silent behind him, before he stood and started walking toward the terminal.

Halifax was a strange place to have come down, and he knew enough of its history to feel the strangeness of it while he walked in. A full international airport for Atlantic Canada — runways and a terminal built to move a couple of million people a year — and, once, the field that had taken in something like forty airliners and seven thousand stranded passengers on a bad morning a generation ago, when American airspace closed without warning and every westbound flight over the ocean had to come down somewhere it hadn't planned. Walking into it now, and finding it like this, was worse for knowing that.

An overnight security officer near a dark checkpoint. A cleaner slumped against a mop cart. Further in, a scatter of the earliest travelers and ground staff for a morning flight south that was never going to board, a coffee kiosk employee still behind her register. He checked a few of them the way procedure and some deeper human need both demanded, confirming what he already knew before he touched them — the same cold absence of pulse and breath he'd already found three times over that morning, twice in his own cockpit and once the length of his own cabin. He stopped counting after the first handful. The emptiness was the thing, more than the bodies — an airport built to move thousands of people an hour, with no one left in it at all.

He sat down eventually in one of the gate seats, alone in an airport built to handle exactly the kind of emergency he'd just flown, and let himself feel, for the first time since the flight deck had gone quiet, the full weight of what he'd actually landed into.

He had his phone out before he'd fully decided to, one weak bar of signal flickering in the corner of the screen — enough, maybe, to matter. He called Elena first, her name at the top of his recents the way it always was, and got nothing back but ringing, flat and endless, the kind that never resolved into a greeting or a busy tone or anything else that might have told him something. He tried again in case his hands had fumbled it. Same ringing. He called his mother next, then Danny Reyes, an old squadron buddy from his C-17 days, then his sister — working down a short list of the people who mattered, the same instinct a man four and a half hours and an ocean away was working through at that very moment without either of them having any idea the other existed yet. Two of the calls found voicemail, his mother's outgoing message and Danny's, both recorded in ordinary voices that had no idea they were about to become the last thing anyone would ever hear from either man. The rest just rang.

Elena and Maya were back in Marietta, four and a half hours behind him now, and there was no version of the next few minutes that got him one step closer to either one. He sat with that until it stopped being a thing he could sit with.

Then the same practical part of him that had gotten the airplane onto the ground started in on what came next, and the first thing it said was that a gate chair wasn't it. There could be someone else in this city — hurt, waiting, doing exactly what he was doing and getting the same nothing back. He wasn't going to find them sitting here.

The rental counters were down on the arrivals level, dark, the lot beyond them full of cars nobody had come to claim. The third one he tried had the keys in the console and most of a tank, and it started clean on the first turn. He drove out through an open gate onto a highway with no one on it and headed for downtown Halifax through the gray of a morning that never quite brightened.

The city was the terminal written large. Cars stopped at angles across the lanes, a few drifted gently together, none of them burning. A harbor full of boats and not one wake on the water. He drove the length of the downtown with the window down, calling out at the intersections, leaning on the horn once and listening to it roll away between the buildings and come back with nothing behind it. He got as far as a hospital lobby and a nurses' station and made himself stop there. He checked a few of the people he passed, the way procedure and something older than procedure both demanded, and found exactly what he already knew he would. By early afternoon he had covered most of the peninsula and had not seen one thing move that he hadn't moved himself.

He drove back to the airport with the light already going, past his own dead airplane on the ramp, and let himself into the hotel joined to the terminal — a glass-walled block off the arrivals level he'd walked past a hundred times over the years and never once needed. The doors were unlocked. The front desk stood dark, a rack of keycards behind it and a monitor still cycling its screensaver, and it took him longer than it should have to accept that there was no one to check him in and no one who would ever care that he hadn't been. He found a master card in an unlocked drawer.

He got into a locked café off the lobby with a luggage cart and ate standing up — cold sandwiches still holding temperature in a dead case, a bottle of juice, an apple — and pocketed a few more bars and a second bottle for the morning. Then he rode a silent elevator to the third floor and picked a room at the end of the hall, away from the windows that faced the ramp. He sat on the edge of a turned-down bed and worked the phone until it was plain there was nothing left to work: Elena again, and again, some stubborn part of him convinced enough tries might change the answer; the news apps, every one of them frozen; every search he could think to run that might turn the day into a shape he could do something with. None of them gave him one. Everything else in the room was exactly as ready for an ordinary traveler as it had been at two o'clock that morning.

He stood in the shower until the hot water started to go, both hands flat against the tile. It was the first time since the flight deck that he'd been somewhere he didn't have to be doing something, and everything he'd been outrunning since he woke into that silence finally caught all the way up. He didn't fight it. There was no one to hold it together for — no airplane, no checklist, no voice on a radio. When it passed he dried off, pulled the blackout curtain against the last of the gray, and lay down on top of a stranger's turned-down bed.

He was asleep before he'd expected to be, the flattened, dreamless sleep of a body that had simply run out.
