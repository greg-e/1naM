# 2 — Sam, Day 0

Delta 84 pushed back from Atlanta a few minutes before eleven, climbing out into a clear night sky toward Paris — a long-haul route, close to nine hours of block time, flown by an augmented three-pilot crew to comply with federal pilot time regulations. Sam had the left seat. Forty-three years old, a decade at Delta after eight years flying C-17 Globemasters for the Air Force, and a lifetime of general aviation before that — Cessnas and gliders as a teenager, a flight instructor rating to help pay his way through college, ratings added one after another for twenty-five years straight until there wasn't much left to challenge his skills. He'd earned the left seat of an A330-900neo the same patient way he'd earned everything else, and he still didn't take it for granted.

The crew tonight was seasoned, first officer Theresa Foreman and relief pilot Jack Sommers, both senior operators in their own right, both good friends, they had flown this route together quite a few times in the last 2 years. Tonight's rotation had all three of them up front for departure, with each one taking a break through the overnight segments so each pilot got a real rest block in the crew bunks before the descent into Paris. Routine. He'd flown this exact rotation more times than he could count, long enough to know its rhythms by heart — a night departure, a quiet cockpit, a sunrise somewhere over the Continent that he still hadn't gotten tired of.

Sam had gone down for his own rest block a little after one, leaving Theresa and Jack up front together. His alarm woke him a little before 5:45 for the handoff back, and he climbed up still working the last of the sleep out of his eyes, already running through the pre-descent to Paris flow in his head. The quiet on the flight deck registered before anything else did — not the ordinary quiet of a long night flight. "Hey guys... sleeping on the job?" Sam said laughing. Then he saw them. Theresa, slumped forward against the harness. Jack, the same. Neither of them moved and there was no response. His whole body went cold, "Hey stop fooling around, this isn't funny..." quiet, nothing, no sounds no response.

He checked for a pulse on each of them and found nothing on either — skin cool, they'd been like this for a while. Then he was moving on procedure alone — confirm the aircraft's still flying itself properly on autopilot falling back on years of ingrained training, fly first, then work out the details, the route had drifted and they were off course but flying and all systems in the green. He walked the length of the cabin, slow, checking rows, "Hello? anyone?", pause "Hey, is there anyone here?" no response... only an off odor he started to become conscious of. Horror is an emotion Sam had never experienced, maybe a shallow sense of it from watching too many horror movies in his teens... but now, something was welling up in him he had never experienced. Two hundred and sixty-one passengers and 13 of his colleagues, gone, dead? He couldn't accept that, methodically he went to each row and checked pulse and breathing... after about 30 checks, as far as he could tell, he was the only thing still alive on a fully loaded A330 that had been flying itself while he had been sleeping.

Back on the flight deck, he keyed the radio out of pure procedural instinct, calling for anyone — company ops, Gander Center, another aircraft on the emergency frequency, anything. The carrier tone was there, the radio working exactly as it should. No voice ever answered it, on any frequency he tried. Have to move them... he first unbuckled Theresa and gently pulled her up and out of the left seat and pulled her back past the flight deck to the left first class aisle... then the same for Jack, not easy... Jack was 6'3 and at least 230... all muscle, he pulled Jack back to the right side first class aisle.  He double checked Helen and Robert, the lead flight attendants, some last ditch hope or thoroughness... 

It had been thirty minutes, from the moment he woke up to the moment he made himself decide. Not because the horror had faded — it hadn't, and wouldn't for a long time — but because twenty-five years of training had built something into him that didn't care how he felt underneath it: when everything goes wrong at once, a pilot's job is to act, not to sit with it. Sitting with it could come later, on the ground, his job was to fly and make informed good decisions.

Procedure said the same thing his gut did: find the nearest suitable field and put the airplane on the ground. Nearest was St. John's, out on the eastern edge of Newfoundland — a real international airport, a proper ILS. He declared an emergency out of habit, no response, the same static or a working radio but nobody on the other end — banking the big aircraft north and west, watching the heading bug swing until St. John's sat on the nose and Paris fell away behind him for good.

The transit in gave him more time than he wanted, alone with the autopilot doing most of the actual flying while his mind kept circling back to the two empty seats behind him. He briefed the approach into St. John's, then briefed it again, until his hands barely needed to think about it.

The coast came up under him wrapped in cloud — not the thin morning haze he'd half expected but a solid gray deck lying right down on the water, edge to edge, the kind of fog the Avalon grows off the cold current all summer and deep into fall. No ATIS to give him a number, no controller to read him the visibility, but he didn't need either; he could see the top of it, unbroken, and he knew exactly what was underneath. He flew the ILS down anyway, coupled, and at minimums he broke out into nothing at all — no runway, no approach lights, no ground, just the same white pressed flat against the windshield. He took the go-around, climbed back into clear air on top, and set up to fly it again, slower this time, because a man alone with no one to check his work will talk himself into a second look. The second approach ended exactly where the first had. Somewhere around two hundred feet he was staring at instruments that said he was over a runway and a windshield that said there was no such thing, and he did the one thing every hour he had ever logged had trained him to do: he did not land.

He had fuel and he had daylight, and he was not going to make a blind autoland at an unmanned field his opening bet. Gander was the other answer — a hundred miles inland, up out of the marine layer, ten thousand feet of runway, the field the whole North Atlantic system kept on the shelf for exactly this. Probably the one he should have picked first. He rolled west off the missed approach and let the coast fall away behind him toward the interior, briefing his third approach of the morning into his second airport, out loud, to nobody.

Gander, at least, he could see. It gave him nothing else — no tower to confirm the runway was clear, no ATIS for winds, no ground vehicle to check for debris, no controller to talk him down. He circled the field once at a distance, just looking, then flew a long low pass down the length of the runway, gear up, reading the surface at altitude and speed for anything that would turn a landing into a second disaster. The lights were on, still cycling through their pattern for no one; the strip was clear, no wreckage, no obstruction he could see. He circled out once more for whatever wind he could read off the sock and the trees, found nothing conclusive, and set up the approach for real — patient with himself in a way he'd never have had the luxury to be with two hundred and sixty other lives depending on the decision, but conscious now of a fuel gauge that already had two diversions written into it and nothing to spare for a third. Flaps, gear, a checklist he ran alone, out loud, his own voice the only one answering back.

He flew that low pass and the approach after it in a silence he had never once experienced in this cockpit — no calls from the back, no company chatter, no controller's voice walking him down, no second and third set of hands beside him doing half the work the way they always had before today. Just the engines, his own breathing, his own checklist calls answered by no one, and somewhere below the fear, a single question turning over and over without an answer: *what happened, and why am I the only one left to ask it.*

The touchdown was almost anticlimactic after everything — smooth, controlled, tires chirping once against pavement that had been sitting patient and unused since long before he'd turned the aircraft around. He taxied clear on instinct more than plan, no ground control to direct him, and brought the aircraft to a stop on a wide stretch of apron with the terminal building dark and silent off to one side.

Shutdown took longer than it should have, his hands running checklists out of habit while the rest of him sat frozen, staring out at a terminal with no lights, no vehicles, no ground crew jogging out to meet an airplane that had just declared an emergency and landed itself with a single pilot aboard. Nobody came. He hadn't really expected anyone to, not after the last hour, but some old, stubborn part of him had held out anyway, and watching that hope not pay off was its own small, separate grief layered on top of everything else.

There was no jetway to connect to, obviously — no ground power, no one to marshal a bridge into place even if the systems had somehow still been running. He sat with that problem for a minute, working through the aircraft's own limited options, and landed on the only real answer available to him: the emergency slides. He armed the forward door, braced himself the way training told him to even though there was no one behind him to brace with, and popped it.

The slide deployed with a hiss and a thump, unspooling down onto the tarmac in the gray Newfoundland morning, and Sam stood in the open doorway a long moment before he made himself step out onto it and ride it down alone — an absurd, undignified way to arrive somewhere, he thought distantly, for a captain who'd just flown two hundred and seventy-some people most of the way to Europe and back.

He landed hard on the tarmac and sat there a second, catching his breath, the aircraft looming huge and silent behind him, before he stood and started walking toward the terminal.

Gander was a strange place to have landed, and he knew enough of its history to feel the strangeness of it while he walked in. A town of barely ten thousand people with an airport built for a whole vanished era of transatlantic traffic — runways and a terminal sized for the days when every airplane crossing the ocean had to stop here for fuel, kept alive since on diversions and charters and not much else. It was the field that had taken in thirty-odd stranded airliners and thousands of passengers on a bad morning a generation ago, and the town had simply absorbed them, fed them, put them up in their own houses. Walking into it now, and finding it like this, was worse for knowing that.

Because the hour had been what it was — two in the morning, before the first departure bank would have pulled anyone in — the terminal held fewer of them than he'd braced for. An overnight security officer near the checkpoint. A cleaner slumped against a mop cart. Further in, a scatter of the earliest travelers and ground staff for a morning flight south that was never going to board, a coffee kiosk employee still behind her register. He checked a few of them the way procedure and some deeper human need both demanded, confirming what he already knew before he touched them, the same cold absence of pulse and breath he'd already found twice over on his own aircraft. He stopped counting after the first handful. The emptiness was the thing, more than the bodies — an airport whose entire reason for existing was taking in people who had nowhere else to land, and no one left in it at all.

He sat down eventually in one of the gate seats, alone in an airport built to handle exactly the kind of emergency he'd just flown, and let himself feel, for the first time since the flight deck had gone quiet, the full weight of what he'd actually landed into.

He had his phone out before he'd fully decided to, one weak bar of signal flickering in the corner of the screen — enough, maybe, to matter. He called Elena first, her name at the top of his recents the way it always was, and got nothing back but ringing, flat and endless, the kind that never resolved into a greeting or a busy tone or anything else that might have told him something. He tried again in case his hands had fumbled it. Same ringing. He called his mother next, then Danny Reyes, an old squadron buddy from his C-17 days, then his sister — working down a short list of people who mattered, the same instinct a man four and a half hours and an ocean away was working through at that very moment without either of them having any idea the other existed yet. Two of the calls found voicemail, his mother's outgoing message and Danny's, both recorded in ordinary voices that had no idea they were about to become the last thing anyone would ever hear from either man. The rest just rang, and rang, and eventually he stopped trying new numbers and started calling Elena back, over and over, some small stubborn part of him convinced that enough tries might change the answer.

They didn't. Somewhere underneath the weight he still hadn't let himself fully open, one thought kept surfacing on its own, procedural and useless and impossible to put down: Elena and Maya, back in Marietta, four and a half hours behind him now, and no way — even with a working phone in his hand and a bar of signal to spare — to actually reach either one of them.
