# 2 — Samuel Reyes, Professional Pilot, Day 0

Delta 27 pushed back from Atlanta a little after eleven, wheels up off 26L at twenty-five minutes to midnight, on a track that would carry them north-northwest over the Midwest and Manitoba, across the Canadian Arctic and northern Alaska and down the Bering Sea into Seoul fifteen hours later. It was an ultra-long-haul run, flown by an augmented four-pilot crew to stay inside federal duty-time limits. Sam had the right seat. Forty-three years old, a decade at Delta after two years flying corporate and charter jets out of Atlanta and eight years before that in C-17's for the Air Force.

Jack Sommers had the left. Sixty-three, two years from the mandatory retirement age, 30 years with Delta. Sam had flown with him a handful of trips over the years. Theresa Foreman was the relief captain and Ruth Gunn the relief first officer, and all four of them were up front for the departure and the climb. After top of climb: Jack and Sam went to the bunks for the first break, Theresa and Ruth taking over for the first leg. Theresa took the left seat from Jack and Ruth took over for Sam. 

At half past midnight, somewhere over western Indiana, Sam punched the combination into the crew rest door, climbed the stair into the crown of the airplane above and behind the flight deck, and found Jack already in the forward bunk with his curtain drawn. Sam set his alarm for 03:15, fifteen minutes ahead of the end of the break, and was asleep before he'd finished thinking about Elena and Rosa.

The alarm woke him. He lay there a moment, his chest raw, a scratch low in it like a cold coming on, and coughed into his fist, keeping it quiet with the captain asleep an arm's length away. He dressed in his bunk, knotted his tie by feel.

He went down the stair, through the crew rest door into the forward entry area, past the forward galley, where the lights were down to night level and nobody was working, and to the flight deck door. He pressed the entry request on the keypad. Inside, the chime would be sounding; Theresa or Ruth would look at the door camera and reach down to the pedestal and the lock would release. He waited, then pressed it again.

Then he entered the emergency access code. The keypad light went to steady, and the chime inside the flight deck would be running continuously now, for anyone on the other side to deny it. He watched the second hand go around and coughed again and kept his hand on the door handle. Thirty seconds. The lock released with a click he felt through the handle, and he pulled the door open.

Theresa was slumped against the left side console, her head turned toward the side window, held in the seat by her lap belt. Ruth had sagged forward and to her right, her forehead nearly on the glareshield, her arms loose in her lap. The flight deck was lit the way they kept it at night, the displays turned down, the overhead dark.

"Theresa." He put his hand on her shoulder. "Theresa, hey." Her head moved with his hand and settled back against the side window. He reached across for Ruth. "Ruth. Ruth, talk to me." He tipped her back off the glareshield and held her there with a hand on her collarbone. Her eyes were partly open and didn't move to him.

He checked them. Theresa first, two fingers at her throat, then her wrist. Nothing. He tried the other side of her neck. Ruth the same. Both of them cold and clammy to his touch.

Then he looked at the airplane. Autopilot 1 engaged. Autothrust on. Flight level three-six-zero. Managed navigation, still on the flight plan route, the magenta line running northwest off the top of the nav display. Nothing on the ECAM, the center screen where the airplane posted any failure along with the checklist to deal with it. Fuel on board, sixty-six tons, right on the plan. The airplane was over northern Saskatchewan, Lake Athabasca off the left side somewhere in the dark below, still tracking northwest at eight miles a minute, away from every runway he might want. He reached up to the glareshield, pulled the heading knob, and turned it to one-eight-zero. The airplane came off the route and banked left, a long slow turn, and settled heading south, back toward Fort McMurray and Edmonton and Saskatoon, the en route alternates on the flight plan.

He took the interphone handset off the pedestal and called the crew rest. Up in the crown the handset beside Jack's bunk would be chiming a foot from his head. Sam let it ring. He hung up and called again and let it ring longer.

He went out through the door and let it lock behind him, up the stair, and pulled Jack's curtain back. Jack lay on his back with the blanket at his chest and his reading glasses folded on the shelf beside him. His mouth was a little open. "Jack." Sam put two fingers under his jaw and held them there and found nothing, and the skin under his fingers was cool, the temperature of the room.

He drew the curtain closed again. Back down the stair, the emergency code on the keypad, thirty more seconds with his hand on the handle, coughing.

He unlatched Theresa's lap belt, slid the seat back on its rails, got his arms under hers, and lifted her out over the armrest and into the observer seat behind the captain's position, and belted her there. She wasn't a large woman, and it still took him two tries. Ruth he left in the right seat, belted in. He slid into the left seat and put on his headset. Outside the windows there was nothing, no horizon, no lights; on the nav display the airplane tracked south, Lake Athabasca falling behind.

VHF 1 was still on Winnipeg Center, and the nav display put that sector well behind them. The last CPDLC uplink in the ATC mailbox was from Winnipeg, time-stamped 0558Z, two minutes before two in the morning back in Atlanta. He pulled the Edmonton Center frequency for the sector he was in off the en route chart on his EFB, dialed it in, and keyed the mic.

"Edmonton Center, Delta Twenty-Seven Heavy, flight level three-six-zero."

Nothing. He gave it ten seconds and called again, then a third time. The radio showed him transmitting, and he heard his own voice in the sidetone. He tuned the same frequency on VHF 2 and called on that one, and switched his audio over to Ruth's side panel and called again from there, in case the fault was in his panel. Nothing on either. He went back to Winnipeg on the old frequency and got the same silence.

VHF 2 had been on 121.5, the emergency frequency every airliner, control center, and military field kept a radio listening to. He set it back there and called any station. He tuned 123.45, the air-to-air frequency, on the standby side and asked for any aircraft. There were other airliners on these polar routes tonight, and some of them were within a couple of hundred miles. Nobody answered.

He typed a message to Edmonton on CPDLC, the datalink controllers used in place of the radio up here, and sent it. It sat in the log as sent and nothing came back. He sent a free-text message over ACARS to Delta's operations center in Atlanta: DAL27 FO REYES. CAPT AND RELIEF PILOTS INCAPACITATED. NO ATC CONTACT. CALL SATCOM. The status went to SENT. He dialed dispatch on the SATCOM, and the call connected and rang on the other end. Twelve rings. He hung up and dialed the medical line, the doctors on the ground who talked crews through a passenger having a heart attack at forty thousand feet, and that rang until he hung up too.

He picked up the interphone handset and called the forward galley. No answer. He made an all-call, every attendant station in the airplane at once. "Cabin crew, flight deck. Anyone, pick up." No answer. He pressed the emergency call, the one that flashed red at every station. He held the handset to his ear and listened to it ring.

He sat there with the handset in his hand and looked at the closed flight deck door. Three hundred and six passengers and thirteen cabin crew were on the other side of it.

He called Elena on the SATCOM. It went straight to her voicemail. It was coming up on four in the morning in Atlanta, and her phone would be face down on the nightstand. He told her where he was and that he loved her and that he'd call when he was on the ground.

Twenty-five minutes after he'd first come through the door, every channel he had was checked and checked again, and the equipment was good. He keyed VHF 1, on the Edmonton frequency, and said something he had only ever said in a simulator.

"MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY. Edmonton Center, any station, Delta Twenty-Seven Heavy, Airbus A350. Crew incapacitation, three pilots, one pilot at the controls. Three hundred twenty-three souls on board."

He sent it again on 121.5. He set 7700 in the transponder, the code that would light the airplane up as an emergency on every radar screen that could see it, and sent a MAYDAY downlink on CPDLC. Then he pulled up the nearest airports page.

Fort McMurray was closest, a hundred and fifteen miles southwest. He brought up the airport on the EFB: seventy-five hundred feet of runway. Then he looked at the weights. They'd left Atlanta near maximum takeoff weight for a fifteen-hour flight, and the airplane had burned barely four hours of it. He was some thirty-five tons over maximum landing weight. An overweight A350, at night, alone, on seventy-five hundred feet, with nobody in a fire truck he could count on at the far end. Edmonton was two hundred thirty-five miles, eleven thousand feet of runway, an ILS, a major international airport. Fifteen minutes farther.

The checklists said nearest *suitable* airport, not nearest. If anyone behind that door was alive, they needed him on the ground in one piece more than they needed him there fifteen minutes sooner.

He entered CYEG as the new destination, selected direct, and pushed the heading knob to hand navigation back to the FMS. The airplane swung right onto the new magenta line, two-one-five on the heading tape.

"Delta Twenty-Seven Heavy, transmitting in the blind. Diverting Edmonton, direct. Leaving flight level three-six-zero in one-five minutes."

He pulled up the overweight landing checklist and worked it item by item, the configuration, the approach speed, the landing performance for runway two-zero at the weight the airplane would be when it got there. The numbers came back inside the runway with room to spare. Vref was higher than he had ever landed this airplane at. He briefed the approach out loud, to nobody, the way he would have briefed it to Jack: the ILS to two-zero, the frequencies, the minimums, the missed approach, the autobrake setting, brake temperatures afterward. Then he started down.

The descent took him over the boreal forest of northern Alberta, black and unlit to the horizon. He transmitted every five minutes on the center frequency and on 121.5. At ten thousand feet he called Edmonton Approach, and at twenty miles Edmonton Tower, on the published frequencies, and got the same nothing from both. The airport came up out of the dark ahead, the approach lights and the runway edge lights on.

He let the autopilot fly the ILS down to a thousand feet, gear down, flaps in the landing configuration, the speed sitting on the high Vref the checklist had given him. At a thousand feet he disconnected it and flew the rest by hand. He held the sink rate down as low as he could and still put it on the touchdown zone, and the mains went on with a long heavy shudder he felt through the seat. Reversers. The autobrake took hold. He let the airplane roll most of the runway's length.

On the taxiway he brought up the wheel page. The brake temperatures were climbing, and he watched them while he taxied to an empty spot on the apron, set the parking brake, and shut down both engines with the APU running for power. 04:36 Eastern on the clock, 02:36 local.

He looked out both side windows for the fire trucks. After a MAYDAY landing they should have been rolling alongside him on the runway and then parked off his nose with their lights going, and someone on the ground frequency should already have been asking him how many on board and whether he needed the stairs. There were no trucks, no lights. He pulled the ground frequency off the airport chart on his EFB, dialed it into VHF 1, and called. "Edmonton Ground, Delta Twenty-Seven Heavy, on the apron, request emergency services." Nothing. He called again.

He picked up the SATCOM and dialed Delta's operations center in Atlanta. It connected and rang. He let it ring until it cut off, and then he dialed again and listened to it ring again, his eyes on the empty apron.

He sat for a moment in the left seat with his hands flat on his thighs, then went back to the forward galley.

One of the flight attendants was sitting on her jumpseat with her head against the bulkhead and a paperback face down in her lap. The other two were on the floor of the galley, one of them on her back with her eyes open and her mouth open. There was dried blood under her nose. He checked each of them. In the business cabin the suites were in their bed positions. Most of the passengers were under their blankets. Not all of them. A man in a window suite was half sitting up, one hand at his collar, his eyes open looking up to the ceiling. A woman across the aisle had her mouth open wide and a dark line of dried blood from her nose to her lip. He went to every one of them, two fingers at the throat, and nobody had a pulse, and he kept going. In the main cabin the reading lights were on over a few rows: a laptop still open, a tray table down, a man slumped against the window with his eyes open and his mouth open. He checked each of the first rows, seat by seat, and then, when his hands started to shake, every fifth row, looking down each one for anything, a hand, a face turned toward him, anyone breathing. He called out. "Hello? Can anyone hear me?" No response, only an off smell he started to become conscious of. Horror was not an emotion Sam had ever had cause to feel, only the shallow thrill of a movie as a teenager. He called it again halfway back, and again at the aft galley, where two more of the crew were. The other cabin crew were in their bunks in the crew rest above the aft galley, and he climbed up and checked them too. No pulse, no breathing.

It took him forty minutes. He walked back up the aisle toward the front of the airplane through three hundred and nineteen people who were dead, coughing into his sleeve.

At the forward door he looked out through the porthole. The apron was lit. The terminal windows were lit. Nothing moved on the ramp: no tug, no fuel truck, no van with its yellow light turning. He stood there in the forward entry area with his forehead against the cool plastic around the window.
