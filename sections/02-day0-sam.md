# 2 — Samuel Reyes, Professional Pilot, Day 0

Delta 27 pushed back from Atlanta at ten after eight, wheels up off 27L fifteen minutes later into a clear evening sky, on a track that would carry them up over the Great Lakes, across Manitoba and the Mackenzie Delta, over northern Alaska and down across the Bering Sea into Seoul fifteen hours later. It was an ultra-long-haul run, flown by an augmented four-pilot crew to comply with federal duty-time regulations. Sam had the left seat. Forty-three years old, a decade at Delta after two years flying corporate and charter jets out of Atlanta and eight years before that flying C-17 Globemasters for the Air Force.

The crew tonight was seasoned: first officer Theresa Foreman and two relief pilots, Jack Sommers and Ruth Gunn, all senior operators in their own right, all good friends and trusted colleagues. Tonight's rotation had all four of them up front through the climb, then split them into pairs for the long, dark middle of the flight: Jack and Theresa up to the bunks first while Ruth took the right seat beside Sam, then the pairs trading places. Routine. He'd flown rotations like it more times than he could count, long enough to know their rhythms by heart — an evening departure, a quiet cockpit going quieter, a sunrise somewhere over Siberia that he still hadn't gotten tired of.

At half past midnight Sam climbed the short ladder into the crew rest, a compartment tucked directly above and behind the flight deck, for the second block of the rotation. Jack and Theresa had gone up first, not long after top of climb, and had come back down to take the seats so Sam and Ruth could go. He left the two of them up front together, somewhere over northern Saskatchewan. Ruth took the other bunk and drew her curtain, and she was asleep before he'd finished setting his alarm. It woke him a little before three. Ruth's curtain was still drawn. He rapped twice on the bulkhead beside it. "Ruth. Twenty minutes." He got nothing and let her be; she always slept hard on the second block, and she'd be down a few minutes behind him. He came back down still working the last of the sleep out of his eyes, already running the next few hours of the flow in his head. The quiet on the flight deck registered before anything else did, and it wasn't the ordinary quiet of a long night leg. "Hey, guys... sleeping on the job?" Sam said, half a laugh in it. Then he saw them. Theresa, slumped forward against the harness. Jack, the same. "What's the status?" Neither of them moved.

He checked for a pulse on each of them and found nothing on either. Their skin was cold. Okay. Fly first, work out the details after: autopilot engaged, flight level three-six-zero, the airplane still tracking the flight plan route, no warnings on the ECAM, the Electronic Centralized Aircraft Monitor that flagged any failing system, everything in the green. Only then did he think of Ruth. He went back up the ladder faster than he'd come down it and pulled her curtain aside. She lay on her side the way she'd settled in, blanket to her shoulder, one hand under the pillow. He said her name, then shook her shoulder, then pressed two fingers under her jaw. Cool, like the others. Nothing.

Back on the flight deck, he plugged his headset into the jumpseat audio panel and leaned between the seats to the pedestal. The radio management panel still had VHF 1, the first of the very-high-frequency voice radios, on Edmonton Center. Nobody had handed them off at the Alaska line, and the nav display put it well behind them. The last CPDLC uplink in the ATC mailbox (controller-pilot data link, air traffic control's way of texting the cockpit) was from Edmonton, time-stamped 0552Z, eight minutes before two in the morning back in Atlanta. He pulled the Anchorage Center frequency off the en route chart on his EFB, the electronic flight bag tablet that had replaced the paper charts, dialed it in, and keyed the mic.

"Anchorage Center, Delta Twenty-Seven Heavy, flight level three-six-zero."

He let go of the mic switch and listened. No controller's voice, no other pilot, just silence in his headset. He gave it ten seconds and called again, then a third time. He took a deep breath and said something he had only ever said in a simulator.

"MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY. Anchorage Center, Delta Twenty-Seven Heavy, Airbus A350. Crew incapacitation. Three pilots unresponsive, no pulse, one pilot at the controls. Position six-niner north, one-five-five west, flight level three-six-zero. Stand by for intentions."

The word was supposed to command silence on the frequency, but there was no chatter, nothing to interrupt. VHF 2 was always set to 121.5, the international emergency frequency, the one every airliner, control center, and military base in the world kept a radio listening to. He sent the same message there, addressed to any station. His hand went to the radio panel for 243.0, the military emergency frequency, before he remembered he wasn't in a C-17 anymore. Civilian aircraft had no UHF, the ultra-high-frequency band the military flew on. The Air Force tankers out of Eielson listened on 121.5 too, and so did every military tower in Alaska. He keyed it again: "Any military aircraft, any station, Delta Twenty-Seven Heavy, MAYDAY..." Nothing. He tuned 123.45 on the standby side, the informal air-to-air frequency pilots used to talk to each other directly, with no controller in between, and asked for any aircraft. There had to be other airliners on the polar routes tonight, close enough to hear him. Nobody answered. He set 7700 in the transponder, the box that identified the airplane to radar on the ground. It was the universal emergency code, and it would turn Delta 27 into a flashing alert on every controller's screen that could see it, no radio call needed. Then he pulled up the emergency page on the controller data link, the same text channel Edmonton's last message had come in on, and sent a MAYDAY as a typed message, in case something was going on with their radios. Then a free-text message over ACARS, the Aircraft Communications Addressing and Reporting System that tied the jet to the airline's computers, to Delta's operations center: CAPT REYES DAL27. FO AND RELIEF PILOTS INCAPACITATED. EMERGENCY DECLARED. NO ATC CONTACT. CALL SATCOM. The message status went to SENT. No reply followed it. He dialed dispatch on the SATCOM, the satellite phone built into the flight deck, and the call connected and rang on the other end twelve times before he hung up. He called Elena on the SATCOM too, and it went straight to voicemail. Of course it did; she'd be asleep with her phone on silent. He left a message.

He walked the length of the cabin, slowly, checking rows. "Hello? Anyone?" A pause. "Hey — can anyone hear me?" No response, only an off smell he started to become conscious of. Horror was not an emotion Sam had ever had cause to feel, only the shallow thrill of a movie as a teenager. Now something was coming up in him he had no name for. Three hundred and six passengers and thirteen of his colleagues — gone, dead? Methodically, he went row by row and checked for a pulse. Then every fifth row, looking carefully down each one for any sign of life, of... By the time he had walked the length of the cabin and back toward the flight deck, it appeared he was the only living person aboard.

Back on the flight deck, the ATC mailbox was empty and so was the ACARS queue. He ran the calls again, Anchorage Center on VHF 1, any station on 121.5, any aircraft on 123.45, and redialed dispatch on the SATCOM. The radios were working as expected. Nobody answered.

He had to move Theresa and Jack. He unbuckled Theresa first, pulled her gently up and out of the right seat and back past the flight deck door into the forward cabin. Jack was harder. He was at least six-three and all muscle, and it took everything Sam had to get him up out of the seat and back to the cabin with some dignity. He left Ruth where she was, in her bunk, and drew the curtain closed again.

It had been thirty minutes. The plane was over the western end of Alaska's North Slope by then, the De Long Mountains somewhere in the dark below, seven hours and thirty-two hundred miles out of Atlanta, with Kotzebue and Deadhorse and Nome on the nav display, and Fairbanks, a little over four hundred miles southeast, the long-runway alternate. He pulled it up and started reviewing: runway length, weather. Why? Why go to Fairbanks? Nothing on the airplane was failing. No fire, no decompression, no fuel emergency. For that matter, he was the only person still alive. That was not going to change on the ramp at Fairbanks. Land-fast doctrine existed to protect people who could still be saved. He didn't have any of those left. What he had was Elena and Rosa in Marietta, three thousand miles to the southeast. He entered KATL, Hartsfield-Jackson's four-letter code, in the FMS, the flight management system that navigated the airplane, as the new destination, then keyed VHF 1.

"MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY. Anchorage Center, any station, Delta Twenty-Seven Heavy, Airbus A350. Crew and passenger incapacitation. Weather clear at flight level three-six-zero. Intentions, return to Atlanta. Position six-niner-three-zero north, one-six-zero-three-zero west, turning left heading zero-seven-five, flight level three-six-zero. Fuel remaining five hundred minutes. Three hundred twenty-three souls on board." He let the key up, then pressed it again. "One alive."

He switched to VHF 2 and sent it again on 121.5. Then he turned the airplane. He dialed zero-seven-five into the heading window and the autopilot rolled left. It was nearly half a circle, and at thirty-six thousand feet and Mach point eight five, the autopilot holding its bank shallow in the thin air, it came slowly, the nose sweeping south through the dark while the numbers on the compass unwound: two-two-zero, one-six-zero, one-zero-zero. He switched on the strobes and the landing lights and checked that the traffic collision avoidance system was still armed. He took the airplane down five hundred feet, to flight level three-five-five, off the altitude any other airplane on the polar routes would be holding. Whatever had happened to his crew might have happened in other cockpits, and those airplanes would still be up here, flying themselves. When the heading settled on zero-seven-five, he went back to the FMS, selected a direct routing to KATL, and re-engaged NAV. The magenta course line swung around on the nav display to point east, and the autopilot took it.

He tried the radios the whole way back to Atlanta. Every twenty minutes he transmitted the MAYDAY on 121.5 and on the center frequency for the airspace under him, and he pulled each new frequency off the chart as the boundaries slid past: Anchorage, then Edmonton, Winnipeg, Minneapolis, Chicago, Indianapolis, Atlanta Center. "Delta Twenty-Seven Heavy, transmitting in the blind." Position, altitude, fuel, intentions, squawking seven-seven-zero-zero. On the hour he redialed dispatch on the SATCOM. The call connected and rang every time. Nothing ever answered. Somewhere over Manitoba, with Lake Winnipeg a long gray shape off the left wing, the sun came up off the left side of the nose, ordinary and indifferent, and he watched it light the wing. He called Elena again. Straight to voicemail again.

Atlanta rose out of the haze ahead, the familiar shape of the skyline, the familiar bend of the river, the point where he always started thinking about Elena and Rosa and what they would be doing together the next few days.
