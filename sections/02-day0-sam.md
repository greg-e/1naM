# 2 — Sam, Day 0

Delta 80 pushed back from Atlanta a few minutes before eleven, climbing out into a clear night sky toward Frankfurt — a long-haul widebody route, close to nine hours of block time, flown by an augmented three-pilot crew so no one had to sit the whole thing on too little rest. Sam had the left seat. Forty-three years old, a decade into the airline after eight years flying C-17 Globemasters for the Air Force, and a lifetime of general aviation before that — Cessnas and gliders as a teenager, a flight instructor rating paid for one lesson at a time through college, ratings added one after another for twenty-five years straight until there wasn't much left with wings he hadn't flown at least once. He'd earned the left seat of an A330-900neo the same patient way he'd earned everything else, and he still didn't take it for granted.

Tonight's rotation had all three of them up front for departure, then down to two-and-one through the overnight cruise segments so each pilot got a real rest block in the overhead bunks before the descent into Frankfurt. Routine. He'd flown this exact rotation more times than he could count, long enough to know its rhythms by heart — a night departure, a quiet cockpit, a sunrise somewhere over the Continent that he still hadn't gotten tired of.

Sam had gone down for his own rest block a little after one, leaving his first officer and the relief pilot up front together, and he was still in the bunk at two in the morning Eastern — the exact hour, though he had no way of knowing it yet, that the rest of the world went quiet. Nothing about the aircraft told him anything was wrong. No alarm, no turbulence, no chime out of place. It simply kept flying, autopilot holding altitude and heading exactly as briefed, for the better part of four hours with no one making a single input on the flight deck.

His own alarm woke him a little before 5:45 for the handoff back, and he climbed up still working the last of the sleep out of his eyes, already running through the pre-descent flow in his head. The quiet on the flight deck registered before anything else did — not the ordinary quiet of a long cruise, but something underneath it, absent in a way he couldn't immediately name. Then he saw them. His first officer, slumped forward against the harness. The relief pilot, gone still in the right seat beside him. Neither of them had moved in a long time. His whole body went cold before his mind had finished processing what he was looking at.

He checked for a pulse on each of them anyway, some clinical, trained part of his brain overriding the rest of him long enough to be sure, and found nothing on either — skin already cool, well past the last few minutes, gone since long before he'd ever climbed the ladder. Then he was moving on procedure alone — confirm the aircraft's still flying itself properly on autopilot, understand the scope of it before you decide anything. He walked the length of the cabin once, slow, checking rows he already suspected he didn't need to check, confirming what the stillness had already told him before he'd even started counting. Two hundred and sixty-one passengers, gone the same way, the same number of hours ago. Fourteen crew, himself included. As far as he could tell, he was the only thing still alive on a fully loaded widebody that had been flying itself, alone, since two in the morning — five hundred nautical miles east-northeast of Newfoundland by the time he finally understood what he was looking at.

Back on the flight deck, he keyed the radio out of pure procedural instinct, calling for anyone — company ops, Gander Center, another aircraft on the emergency frequency, anything. The carrier tone was there, the radio working exactly as it should. No voice ever answered it, on any frequency he tried.

He gave himself maybe twenty minutes, all told, from the moment he'd found them to the moment he made himself decide. Not because the horror had faded — it hadn't, and wouldn't for a long time — but because twenty-five years of training had built something into him that didn't care how he felt underneath it: when everything goes wrong at once, a pilot's job is to act, not to sit with it. Sitting with it could come later, on the ground, if there was a ground worth calling that anymore.

Every instinct that training had built into him said the same thing procedure always said in a situation like this: don't try to make the full crossing, find the nearest suitable field. St. John's was the obvious answer — a real international airport on the eastern tip of Newfoundland, long runways, a proper ILS, built to handle exactly this kind of widebody emergency, still there and still lit whether or not anyone was left on the ground to work it. He declared an emergency out of habit more than expectation, knowing there was likely no one on the other end to hear it, and began the turn — banking the big aircraft north and west, watching the heading bug swing until St. John's sat dead ahead on the nose and Frankfurt fell away behind him for good.

The transit in gave him more time than he wanted, alone with the autopilot doing most of the actual flying while his mind kept circling back to the two empty seats behind him. By the time the Newfoundland coast finally resolved out of the morning haze, he'd rehearsed the approach so many times in his head that his hands barely needed to think about it anymore.

The real caution came from what happened once he got there. No tower meant no one to confirm the runway was clear, no ATIS for current winds, no ground vehicle to check for debris, no controller to talk him down. He circled the field first at a distance, just looking, before he trusted himself any closer. Then a long, low pass over the runway itself, gear up, scanning the surface at altitude and speed for anything that would turn a landing into a disaster on top of everything else. The lights were on, still cycling through their pattern for no one, and the strip itself looked clear, no wreckage, no obstruction he could see. He circled out again to check whatever wind indication he could find visually, found nothing conclusive, and flew a full approach anyway, only to go around at the last moment rather than commit to something he wasn't fully certain of. He set up a second time, patient with himself in a way he wouldn't have had the luxury to be with two hundred and sixty other lives depending on the decision, and stayed on it, configuring flaps and gear on a checklist he ran alone, out loud, his own voice the only one answering back.

He flew that low pass and the approach after it in a silence he had never once experienced in this cockpit — no calls from the back, no company chatter, no controller's voice walking him down, no second and third set of hands beside him doing half the work the way they always had before today. Just the engines, his own breathing, his own checklist calls answered by no one, and somewhere below the fear, a single question turning over and over without an answer: *what happened, and why am I the only one left to ask it.*

The touchdown was almost anticlimactic after everything — smooth, controlled, tires chirping once against pavement that had been sitting patient and unused since long before he'd turned the aircraft around. He taxied clear on instinct more than plan, no ground control to direct him, and brought the aircraft to a stop on a wide stretch of apron with the terminal building dark and silent off to one side.

Shutdown took longer than it should have, his hands running checklists out of habit while the rest of him sat frozen, staring out at a terminal with no lights, no vehicles, no ground crew jogging out to meet a widebody that had just declared an emergency and landed itself with a single pilot aboard. Nobody came. He hadn't really expected anyone to, not after the last hour, but some old, stubborn part of him had held out anyway, and watching that hope not pay off was its own small, separate grief layered on top of everything else.

There was no jetway to connect to, obviously — no ground power, no one to marshal a bridge into place even if the systems had somehow still been running. He sat with that problem for a minute, working through the aircraft's own limited options, and landed on the only real answer available to him: the emergency slides. He armed the forward door, braced himself the way training told him to even though there was no one behind him to brace with, and popped it.

The slide deployed with a hiss and a thump, unspooling down onto the tarmac in the gray Newfoundland morning, and Sam stood in the open doorway a long moment before he made himself step out onto it and ride it down alone — an absurd, undignified way to arrive somewhere, he thought distantly, for a captain who'd just flown two hundred and seventy-some people most of the way to Europe and back.

He landed hard on the tarmac and sat there a second, catching his breath, the aircraft looming huge and silent behind him, before he stood and started walking toward the terminal.

St. John's International had never been a sleepy field — provincial capital, real passenger volume, real staff on a normal morning — and that scale was exactly what made the terminal so much worse to walk into than a smaller airport would have been. He found the first bodies at the security checkpoint, a couple of uniformed officers and a handful of early travelers where they'd been standing when whatever this was came through. Further in, near baggage claim, more — ground staff, a coffee kiosk employee still behind her register, a family that had clearly been waiting on a departure of their own. He checked a few of them the way procedure and some deeper human need both demanded, confirming what he already knew before he touched them, the same cold absence of pulse and breath he'd already found twice over on his own aircraft. He stopped counting somewhere past twenty.

He sat down eventually in one of the gate seats, alone in an airport built to handle exactly the kind of emergency he'd just flown, and let himself feel, for the first time since the flight deck had gone quiet, the full weight of what he'd actually landed into.

He had his phone out before he'd fully decided to, one weak bar of signal flickering in the corner of the screen — enough, maybe, to matter. He called Elena first, her name at the top of his recents the way it always was, and got nothing back but ringing, flat and endless, the kind that never resolved into a greeting or a busy tone or anything else that might have told him something. He tried again in case his hands had fumbled it. Same ringing. He called his mother next, then Danny Reyes, an old squadron buddy from his C-17 days, then his sister — working down a short list of people who mattered, the same instinct a man four and a half hours and an ocean away was working through at that very moment without either of them having any idea the other existed yet. Two of the calls found voicemail, his mother's outgoing message and Danny's, both recorded in ordinary voices that had no idea they were about to become the last thing anyone would ever hear from either man. The rest just rang, and rang, and eventually he stopped trying new numbers and started calling Elena back, over and over, some small stubborn part of him convinced that enough tries might change the answer.

They didn't. Somewhere underneath the weight he still hadn't let himself fully open, one thought kept surfacing on its own, procedural and useless and impossible to put down: Elena and Maya, back in Marietta, four and a half hours behind him now, and no way — even with a working phone in his hand and a bar of signal to spare — to actually reach either one of them.
