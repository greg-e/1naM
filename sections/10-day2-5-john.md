# 13 — John, Day 3-6

They stayed longer than any of them planned to, and none of them apologized for it.

The days at Grace's house folded into each other in a way the week before hadn't allowed — no deadline, no leg to fly, no address folded in a jacket pocket pulling them toward the next unknown. Grace, still working a cast and a pair of borrowed crutches, turned out to be constitutionally incapable of sitting still for it: by the second day she'd found the breaker in the garage that had been tripping the well pump, rewired around it, and had Sam holding a flashlight while she rebuilt a carburetor on an old generator she said she'd been meaning to fix for a year and had finally run out of excuses not to. "First useful thing I've done since I got here that wasn't bleeding on somebody," she said, and looked, for the first time since the overlook, entirely like herself.

Sam kept his promise to Tyler on the third clear night, the two of them out on the deck long after the others had gone in, Sam working through star charts on his phone by memory more than the dying battery, until he found it — a fast, steady point of light crossing low across the water, no blink to it, moving with total purpose against a sky that had nothing else moving in it at all. "There," he said, and Tyler didn't say anything for a long moment, just watched it all the way to the horizon, and when he finally spoke it wasn't a question at all, just a fact he needed to say out loud to someone. "That's her window going by right now. Probably." Sam didn't correct the *probably*. He just stood there with him until it was gone.

Catherine had her own quiet moment two days in, alone at the kitchen sink long after midnight, that John only found by accident coming down for water — her shoulders doing something small and controlled that stopped the second she heard him on the stairs. He didn't ask. He just filled a glass, set it down next to her without a word, and sat at the table until she was ready to either talk or not, and when she chose not to, that was fine too. Some debts got repaid exactly like that — nobody keeping score, everybody having had their own version of that sink at some point this week.

For John, the days meant something different: the first real chance since Alpharetta to stop moving long enough to actually think. He set up in the spare bedroom Grace wasn't using — the workstation finally out of the back of the car and onto a real desk, its uplink antenna propped in the one window that faced clear sky, and by the second afternoon Grace had wandered in, taken one look at his signal strength, and disappeared to come back twenty minutes later with a length of coax and a scavenged directional antenna mounted on a painter's pole lashed to the deck railing. "You're getting maybe a third of what this thing can actually do," she said, running the cable in through the window frame. "Let's fix that." The bars on his screen doubled inside the hour, and John understood, watching her do it without being asked twice, exactly what the cast note had meant about her never having gotten to use what she actually knew how to do out here.

He kept running the same passes he'd started that first night at PDK — new 911 traces, filtered the same way, hunting for another Grace — and found almost nothing, three days running, which told him something true and unwelcome about how few of them there really were left to find. It wasn't discouraging exactly. It was just honest, the kind of number he'd rather sit with than pretend away.

It was Tyler, oddly, who nudged him sideways into the thing that actually mattered. "Do planes still fly themselves like the one Sam found you on?" he asked one evening, watching John work. "What about boats? Do boats do that too?"

John hadn't actually checked. He'd built his entire mental model of that first morning — a diverted flight on FlightAware with no name attached, the tell that had led him to Sam in the first place — and never once thought to ask whether the same signature existed anywhere else. He pulled up the maritime equivalent that night, an AIS tracking feed built for shipping brokers and marine insurers, the kind of thing that had never mattered to him a week ago and now took about four minutes to find. He had the model run the same pattern-matching logic he'd used on 911 dispatch logs — not looking for a voice this time, looking for a hand — and went to bed leaving it running.

By the third morning it had found what he hadn't quite let himself hope for: a genuine ghost fleet spread across the North Pacific, dozens of vessels still transmitting positions, still technically under way, every one of them holding a course some dead officer had set days ago and nobody had touched since. And one that didn't fit the pattern at all — a container ship, the *Pacific Tender*, whose track showed something no autopilot on earth could do on its own: a controlled slowdown, a deliberate turn into Puget Sound, and a clean, careful anchoring in Elliott Bay, right off Seattle, sometime the day before yesterday.

He sat with it a long moment before he called Sam in from the porch.

"Somebody's flying this one too," Sam said, reading over his shoulder, and John heard the exact same note in his voice that he imagined had been in his own on that very first morning, looking at a diverted flight with no name on it yet. "Same as mine. Same as yours, that first day."

"Same as mine," John agreed. "Except this one made port. Whoever's on that ship didn't just survive the last six days. They sailed themselves home."

Sam was already pulling up a chart on his phone, the old habit resurfacing before either of them had said the word out loud. "Seattle's nothing from Portland. Forty minutes, maybe less."

"Then we go look," John said, and for the first time since Grace's cast had come off the makeshift splint, it felt less like chasing an emergency and more like following a very good, very deliberate piece of news.

They told the others over dinner that night — Catherine already reaching for the first-aid kit out of habit before John finished the sentence, Grace asking practical questions about the ship's size before anyone had even confirmed there was a person to meet, Tyler wanting to know if it was as big as an aircraft carrier. Nobody voted against it. Nobody needed to.

"Tomorrow, then," Sam said, and outside, the tide kept working at the rocks the same patient way it had all week, entirely unbothered by how much had just changed.
