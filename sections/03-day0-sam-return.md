# 3 — Samuel Reyes, Edmonton to Atlanta, Day 0

He stood at the porthole until the cold of the plastic had gone out of it. Then he went back to the flight deck for his things.

His flight bag was in the crew rest, along with his jacket and cap. He went up the stair past Jack's drawn curtain and got them without opening it. On the flight deck he unplugged his iPad from its mount, wound up his headset, and took his phone out of the side pocket of the flight bag. He took it off airplane mode. There was a text from Rosa, sent at 10:40 the night before: *night dad love you*. He read it twice and put the phone in his shirt pocket. Out the side window the terminal was still lit. Somebody had to be in there.

At door 1L he checked the slide arming lever, set it to armed, and checked it again. He opened the door, and the slide blew out of its pack with a bang and a roar of gas. He waited until it was fully inflated and settled on the concrete. He set the flight bag on the slide and let it go, and watched it ride down to the bottom. Then he sat on the sill, let go, and came down after it in the dark. He picked the bag up off the end of the slide and stood next to the airplane in the noise of the APU, looked up at the open door eighteen feet over his head with the slide hanging out of it, and coughed.

It was 03:41 by his phone, local. He picked up the bag and walked across the apron toward the terminal.

The jet bridges along that end of the building were pulled back against the wall, their wheels chocked. Under the nearest one a steel door at ramp level stood open a few inches, held with a rubber wedge, the way ramp crews leave a door on a cool night so they don't have to badge in every trip. Inside was a baggage room with a belt coming down out of the wall and a train of empty carts parked along it. A man in coveralls and a reflective vest sat on the tongue of the first cart, bent forward over his knees, his ear protectors around his neck. Sam crouched in front of him and put two fingers against his neck. The skin was cool. He stayed there a moment, then stood up and found the stairs.

The concourse was bright and empty. The departure board over the nearest gate showed the first flight of the morning, a 06:10 to Calgary, ON TIME. Halfway down the concourse a floor scrubber sat at an angle across the tile with a woman in a cleaning company smock on the floor beside it, on her side, one arm out. At the next gate a young man lay across three seats with his backpack under his head and his shoes off, set side by side under the seat. Sam checked them both. "Hello?" he called down the concourse. "Is anyone here?" His voice came back off the glass. The coffee shop across from the gate was shuttered, the menu boards lit behind the grille.

He sat down at the gate with the window in front of him. Out on the apron the A350 sat where he had left it, the slide hanging from the forward door, the APU exhaust shimmering off the tail.

He called Elena's cell, and it went to voicemail. He called the house line. It rang until the machine picked up and he heard his own voice tell him nobody could come to the phone. He called Rosa's phone. Voicemail. He called Delta crew scheduling, and it just rang, nothing, nobody picked up. He called Delta's operations center from the cell this time, in case the SATCOM had been the problem, and it rang out the same as before. He opened the Canada Flight Supplement on his iPad and pulled up the Edmonton page. It listed numbers for NAV CANADA's Edmonton Area Control Centre and the airport operations centre. He called the control centre and let it ring. He called airport operations. The gate podium had a desk phone, and he went around behind the counter and picked it up and got a dial tone, and he dialed airport operations again from there, and listened to it ring somewhere in the same building.

He called and left Elena a second message. He didn't know what to say in it, so he told her he was on the ground in Edmonton and that he was all right.

Then he opened FlightAware.

He found Delta 27 first. The map showed his own track, a long line up out of Atlanta and across the prairie, the turn south over Saskatchewan, the turn for Edmonton, and the airplane icon sitting at the field with 7700 next to it. Then he zoomed out.

The map was full of airplanes. Airplanes over the Atlantic and over Europe, freighters over the Pacific. Airplanes over Siberia, over India, over the middle of Africa. All of them moving. He tapped on one over the Bay of Biscay, a 787 out of Newark for Tel Aviv, and it showed level at flight level three-niner-zero, on course, ground speed normal. He tapped another, and another. He looked for the airplanes climbing and descending, the ones on approach into Chicago or Toronto or Los Angeles or anywhere, and there weren't any. Nothing climbing, nothing descending. He set the map to the departures out of Atlanta, and the last one had gone at 01:51. Out of Chicago, 01:54. Out of Los Angeles, a red-eye at 22:52 local, 01:52 Eastern. He went to Heathrow, where it was mid-morning and the first wave of the day should have been landing and leaving, and the ground there was full of airplanes and none of them had moved.

He sat at the gate and scrolled. Nothing had taken off anywhere he could find since a few minutes before two in the morning Eastern. Nothing had landed anywhere since then, except one airplane, at Edmonton, at 02:36 local, with 7700 on it.

He opened a news site. The top story was about a baseball game. The time stamp on it was 01:58 Eastern. He tried another site, and its newest story was 01:55. He closed it and put the phone back in his pocket.

For a long time he sat and looked out at Delta 27.

There was one thing he could do. Elena and Rosa were in Dunwoody, nineteen hundred miles southeast. He could not help anyone on that airplane. He could not help the man on the baggage cart or the woman by the floor scrubber or the kid with his shoes off. He could get home.

Nobody was going to fly him there. The A350 was out on the apron with its slide hanging off it, and it was too much airplane for one pilot with nobody to push it back or start it. He needed something small enough to fly alone and with the legs to get across the plains. A field this size would have a general aviation side, charter operators, corporate hangars. He opened the airport diagram on the iPad. The FBOs and the hangar rows were on the east side of the field.

Down in the baggage room an airside pickup with a yellow beacon on the cab was parked by the door, the keys in the ignition. He put his flight bag on the passenger seat and drove it out along the apron service road with the beacon off, past his own airplane, toward the hangars.

The general aviation ramp was a row of hangars and an FBO building with its lobby lit. A fuel truck sat at the edge of the ramp with its engine idling and its headlights on, pointed at nothing. Sam went to the cab. The driver was slumped against his door with his face on the glass, his eyes open, in a hi-vis vest and a company ball cap. Sam opened the passenger door, climbed up, and reached across and put his fingers against the man's neck. He turned off the engine and left the headlights on.

The FBO's front door was unlocked. Inside, the lobby smelled of coffee that had been on the burner for hours. A line technician was in the chair behind the front desk in front of the ramp camera monitors, his head back, his mouth open, a line of dried blood from one nostril. His radio was on the desk, turned up, silent. Sam checked him. Then he went down the hall and checked the pilots' lounge, which was empty, and the flight planning room, which was empty, and the restrooms.

He went back out and walked the hangars along the ramp, trying doors. Two were locked. The third was open, its personnel door propped with a fire extinguisher, and inside under the lights were a King Air with its cowlings off on a maintenance stand and, nose out toward the hangar door, a Pilatus PC-12 NGX in white and gray with Canadian registration. He walked around it. Tires good. No covers on the pitot or the intake. The cabin door was unlocked, and inside it was in a charter interior, six leather seats, a small cabinet aft. In the cockpit the log was on the right seat with the last entry in it, Fort McMurray–Edmonton, yesterday afternoon, and the fuel figure written next to it. Half tanks.

He sat down in the left seat and took his phone out and looked at FlightAware again. Nothing had changed. The 787 for Tel Aviv was over France, still level, still on course.

He took the iPad out of his flight bag and started planning.

Edmonton to Peachtree-DeKalb, the airport closest to his home in Dunwoody, direct was about seventeen hundred nautical miles. The NGX's book range was a little over eighteen hundred at long-range cruise. He needed a stop. Minneapolis-St. Paul sat almost on the line, nine hundred forty miles out, then seven hundred eighty to PDK. Two legs, each inside the airplane's range with fuel to spare. Big airport, long runways, fuel trucks on the ramp. He'd be there by midmorning Central and home by early afternoon Eastern.

He'd flown eight hundred hours in a PC-12 out of college, an older one with round gauges. The NGX had glass, a Honeywell suite, and an autothrottle. He found the airplane's own pilot's operating handbook in the seat pocket behind the left seat and sat in the cockpit with the hangar lights on and read the normal procedures section front to back, then the limitations, then the emergency procedures. The engine was the same family of Pratt & Whitney turboprop he knew. The rest was familiar enough.

He opened the hangar door from the control box on the wall. He took the second fuel truck, the one that had been parked with nobody in it, and fueled the PC-12 to full, 2,704 pounds, standing on the wing with the nozzle and watching the meter. He cleared the airplane's water drains and checked the samples under his phone's flashlight. He did the walkaround twice.

It was still full dark when he climbed in. He set up the avionics the way the handbook said and then set up the flight plan: CYEG, direct KMSP, direct KPDK. He checked the weather on his phone, the last forecasts the sites had, already hours old. Clear across the prairies. Some haze in the Southeast.

Then he went into the transponder page on the display and found the field for the flight ID. It held the airplane's registration, C-GKPX. The trackers would show it as a Canadian PC-12 going somewhere unknown. He deleted the registration and typed, one letter at a time on the keypad, D-A-L-2-7-P-D-K. If anyone was looking, they would know what airplane he'd come off of and where he was going.

He started the engine with the battery and the starter wound up the PT6 to light-off, and the temperature came up and peaked inside the limit and settled. He taxied out to runway 30 and stopped short of it.

"Edmonton Tower, Charlie Golf Kilo Papa X-ray, holding short runway three-zero." He waited. "Any station, Charlie Golf Kilo Papa X-ray, departing Edmonton, runway three-zero, southeast-bound for Minneapolis."

He lined up and pushed the power lever up, and the airplane went down the runway and lifted off in about a quarter of it. 05:31 local. He climbed out over the city, and there were lights in the houses, streetlights along the avenues, traffic signals changing over empty intersections. On the freeway south of the airport a semi was stopped in the right lane with its lights on and its trailer lights on, and nothing else was moving on the road. He turned southeast and kept climbing.

He leveled at flight level two-eight-zero over the Saskatchewan border, and the sun came up ahead of him a little after six. On the hour and the half hour he transmitted on the center frequency for the airspace under him and on 121.5, giving his position, altitude, and destination. Edmonton Center, then Winnipeg, then Minneapolis. Nobody came back on any of them. The cough was still there, but didn't seem to be getting worse.

From five miles up the prairie was a grid of section lines to the horizon, brown and gold, the towns gray smudges along the straight threads of the highways and the rail lines. What he looked at was the sky. On a clear September morning over the northern plains there should have been contrails, the eastbound traffic out of Seattle and Vancouver and Calgary, white lines drawn across the blue above and below him and spreading in the wind. There weren't any. His traffic display showed nothing within its range. He looked out both sides every few minutes for the rest of the leg and didn't see another airplane.

Minneapolis came up ahead a quarter to ten local, the two downtowns and the lakes and the river, and the airport south of the river. He flew a low pass down runway 30L at five hundred feet to look at it. The runway was clear. A Delta A321 sat at a gate on the main terminal with the jet bridge on it and the cabin lights on, and dozens of airplanes behind it at their gates the same way. He came around and landed.

At an FBO on the field he found a fuel truck in a row of three, keys in it, and nobody near it. He fueled the airplane back to full from the truck, checked the oil and the drains, and used the restroom in the FBO without looking into the offices, and he was off the ground again forty minutes after he had landed. 10:30 Central.

The last leg ran southeast over the farmland of Iowa and across the Mississippi, over Illinois, Kentucky, Tennessee. He kept the transmissions going on the hour and the half hour. He had called Elena's cell again on the ramp at Minneapolis and gotten her voicemail. In cruise he took his phone out and read Rosa's text again.

Over the Cumberland Plateau the haze came up to meet him, a white layer with the ridges of the Appalachians standing up out of it to the east. He started down at Chattanooga. Atlanta rose out of the haze ahead, the towers downtown and Midtown, then Buckhead closer, and north of them the trees of Dunwoody, his street under them somewhere, where Elena's car would be in the drive and Rosa's bike against the garage.
