# Kendra Vance

### 11/2/2028, GMT 307, 17:31 - International Space Station

Luca had the tracking laptop open on the Columbus bulkhead beside the radio, and he called the time off it as if someone on the ground were taking it down. "Four minutes. Port Angeles, maximum elevation sixty-one degrees. Good one."

Kendra hooked a toe under the handrail and pulled herself in beside him. The radio was a black Kenwood the size of a hardback book, with a hand mic on a coiled cord and a strip of gray tape on the faceplate where somebody before them had written the frequencies in marker: *DN 145.800 / UP 144.490 (Reg 2), 145.200 (Reg 1)*. The station talked on the first one and listened on the others.

Ellen Pruitt came through the hatch from Node 2 and stopped herself on the overhead rail. She was the commander, and she came to every Port Angeles pass now. Two weeks ago the radio's little screen had shown them a packet message: *PORT ANGELES WA. 6 ALIVE. LISTENING EVERY PASS 145.800.* It had come in over the packet relay on some pass while they slept, and been sitting there for a day before Luca thought to scroll back. Since then they had called on every pass over the Northwest, and twice they had heard a carrier come up on the uplink, and a man's voice under the noise, and then nothing they could read.

"Two minutes," Luca said.

There was no window in Columbus. On the laptop's map the station's track came in off the Pacific toward the coast of Washington, a line of dots with a circle around the one that was them. From the cupola, in September, she had watched the lights of North America go out over three nights, the Southeast first, a whole region dark on one pass that had been lit on the pass before. Since then the night side had been dark from one coast to the other, except for fires. She didn't look for Huntsville on the night passes anymore.

"AOS," Luca said, and the speaker hissed louder and then went quiet. He keyed the mic. "Port Angeles, Port Angeles, this is November-Alpha-One-Sierra-Sierra, the International Space Station. How do you read? Over."

He let it go. The quiet held.

"NA1SS, this is Ediz Hook, Port Angeles, Washington." It was a man's voice, low, with an accent she placed as German before he got to the end of the first sentence. "I read you five by five. Position four eight degrees zero eight minutes north, one two three degrees two four minutes west. Six persons alive at this station. Over."

Luca turned his head and looked at her, and at Ellen, and keyed the mic. "Ediz Hook, NA1SS. Five by five. Seven persons aboard, all well. We copied your packet message. Who are you? Over."

"Brandt, Johan. Second officer, motor vessel *Pacific Tender*." The voice stopped, and when it came back it was slower. "Hartman, John. Reyes, Samuel. Dahl, Catherine, a doctor. Jacobs, Della."

Luca had a pencil out and was taking the names down on the back of the pass sheet.

"Vance—"

She heard her own name. Somebody down there had a crew list, she thought, the NASA press kit, and he was going to read the seven of them back like a roll call.

"—Tyler. Ten years old, from Huntsville, Alabama. His mother is Kendra Vance. Over."

Luca's pencil stopped. The speaker gave them the quiet of an open channel with nobody on it, and under it the fans, and nobody keyed.

"Sixty-one," Luca said, low, because calling it was his job. "That's the top." He held the mic out.

She didn't take it. At some point she had let go of the handrail, and the air off the vent was carrying her back and to the left, and her shoulder came up against the face of the rack behind her with a knock she felt and didn't turn around for. In eight months aboard she had not touched a wall she didn't mean to touch.

Ellen's hand came down flat between her shoulder blades and held her there.

"Kendra. He's waiting on you."

She took the mic and keyed it. The transmit light on the faceplate went red, and she held the key down with the channel open and her mouth open, and nothing came. Three seconds of dead carrier went down to Port Angeles. She let go.

She keyed again, and this time what came out was the loop voice, the one she had used at six in the morning on the fifth of September to a console in Houston that had stopped answering. It came out flat and clear, every word set down by itself. Her hand on the mic was shaking hard enough that she put the other one over it.

"Ediz Hook, this is Kendra Vance. Say again the last name. Say again the boy. Over."

"Vance, Tyler." The German voice was in no hurry, and gave each word the same weight. "Ten years old. He is alive. He is well. Over."

She keyed the mic. "Get him to the radio. Over."

She let go of the key. The speaker gave her quiet, and then a hiss coming up under the quiet, and then a sound that could have been a voice or the edge of one, and then the noise came over the top of it.

"Thirty degrees and falling," Luca said. "They're going behind the mountains, maybe. Or it's their antenna."

She keyed again. "Ediz Hook, NA1SS. Say again. Over."

The noise held, and then, broken, the German voice came through, three words out of a sentence: "...next pass... one niner..." Then nothing she could pull out of it, and then the squelch closed, and the speaker went dead.

Luca looked at the laptop. "Next pass over them is 19:05. West of them, out over the water, maximum elevation fourteen. Short. Maybe four minutes. Maybe three good."

"He said 'one niner,'" Ellen said.

"He has a list of passes, then," Luca said. "Or he worked it out."

Kendra gave him the mic back. She found the rail with her hand and held herself still in front of the radio.

"Ninety-three minutes," Ellen said. "Kendra."

"I know what ninety-three minutes is."

"I'm going to say it anyway. You don't know who answers on that pass. You know a name on a list."

"I know."

"Go do something with your hands," Ellen said. "I'll come get you."

### 17:44

She went to Node 3 and did the water.

It was what she did with her hands now. The station's water ran in a loop: everything the seven of them breathed out and sweated and passed went through the machines in this module and came back drinkable, and when one of them faulted she was the one aboard who could open it up and tell why. The urine processor had been running rough for a week. She pulled up its data on the laptop at the rack and read it, temperatures and pressures and the motor current on the distillation drum, and none of it was new, and she read it again.

On Day 0 she had been at this rack at 05:30 GMT, two hours before the crew's wake-up, because the ground had asked her in early to work a fault on this same machine. Dave Ruiz was on the overnight console in Houston and reading her the procedure over the loop a step at a time. At 06:00 he was on step fourteen. "Okay, Kendra, for step fourteen we'd like you to hold the—"

Then nothing, not static, not the hiss and click that meant the link had dropped. The loop was open and the link was good, and the voice had stopped in the middle of a word.

She had called him on Space to Ground Two, then on One. She had checked the comm page and found both links locked and green. By 06:20 she had woken Ellen. By 07:00 they had called every number on the station's phone, which ran through Houston on the same link: the flight director's console, the Huntsville payload center where she had worked for ten years, the European control center outside Munich, SpaceX in California. Everything rang. Nothing answered. The Russians came through from their end with the same story about Moscow. At 07:30 Ellen opened the daily planning conference on schedule and read the control centers' names into the loop in order, Houston, Huntsville, Munich, Tsukuba, Moscow, and waited after each one, and nobody joined.

At 07:52 the station passed from one relay satellite to the next, a handover that happened twenty times a day without anyone aboard noticing, and the link didn't come back. It never had.

She had thought of calling home at 06:20. It was the first thing she thought after she woke Ellen. She had put it away, because you don't use the phone for yourself in the first hour of an anomaly, you work the anomaly. Ellen had made a list of who to call and in what order, and Kendra's mother and her brother and her son were not on it yet when the link went at 07:52. She had counted it many times: ninety-two minutes.

That night, after the rest of them had gone to their sleep stations, she had come back here and opened the procedure and read step fourteen through to the end, and done it.

She did the numbers now, because they were here on the same laptop. The water held, and the oxygen came out of the water, and as long as she kept the machines running neither of them would be the reason they came down. Food came up on cargo ships, and no cargo ship was coming. Seven people on what was aboard, at the rations they had cut to in September, took them into February. Four people took them into July. She had shown Ellen that page in October. The Russians had their own Soyuz and their own decision to make about it, and Andrei had said, the week before, that they were making it.

She closed the spreadsheet. On the laptop the clock said 18:41.

She went to her sleep station in Node 2 and pulled the curtain and looked at the picture Velcroed to the wall, Tyler at nine in the NASA shirt that was too big for him then, grinning with all his teeth, at the Rocket Center. Then she put her reading glasses on the cord back around her neck, where they lived, and went back to Columbus.

### 19:04

Luca had selected the antenna that looked down and west, and he had turned the squelch up a notch to stop the noise opening it. Ellen was on the overhead rail. Pavel had come up from the Russian segment and was holding on at the hatch, and he nodded to her and didn't say anything.

"AOS in one minute. It will be weak at first."

She took the mic from him before he could offer it.

"AOS."

The speaker hissed and opened and closed and opened, and on the second opening there was a voice already talking, the German voice, partway into a call. "...Hook, Ediz Hook calling NA1SS, NA1SS. Over."

She keyed. "Ediz Hook, NA1SS. Kendra Vance. Go ahead. Over."

The hiss ran longer than it should have, and then a different voice came in, thin and then full, hoarse, and higher at the end of the word.

"Mom?"

She had her hand over her mouth and she took it away. "Hey, baby. I hear you. I hear you, Ty. Are you hurt? Over."

"No. I'm okay." Then, after a moment, remembering: "Over."

"Who's with you? Over."

"Sam and Dr. Dahl came and got me in an airplane. On Sunday. That was a long time ago. And John and Della and Johan are here, he's the one with the radio, and there's a dog named Dan. We're on a Coast Guard ship. There's lots of water." He stopped. "Over."

She keyed and couldn't say anything, and let go. She keyed again. "Ty. Uncle Carl?"

The hiss ran on.

"He was on the couch," Tyler said. "When I woke up. I stayed in my room." A pause. "Grandma too. Sam went in. He brought me her Bible." Another pause, and she heard him breathe in. "Over."

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Thank you for telling me. You did right, staying in your room. You did right. Over."

"I counted the water," he said. "Like you said. Before I was thirsty." The voice broke up and came back. "...Mom, are you..." It stopped. She knew the stop. He did it at the dinner table, he did it on the family calls through Houston, when a question went somewhere he didn't think she could carry.

"Say it, Ty. Ask me. Over."

"When are you coming home?"

Luca had his eyes on the laptop. He held up two fingers, then one.

She had never lied to him on a call and she didn't now. "I don't know yet, baby. I don't know when. We're working on it, every day. I'm going to talk to you every pass I can. You listen for me. I love you. Over."

"I love you too. I'll be—"

The noise came over the top of him and stayed. Luca reached past her and turned the squelch down, and they all listened to the noise for a while, and then he turned it back up and the speaker went quiet.

"LOS," he said.

Nobody moved. Then Pavel pushed off from the hatch and went back down toward the Russian segment, and touched her shoulder on his way past.

Luca took the station's log off its Velcro, the ham log, and wrote in it: the date, 19:05 GMT, Ediz Hook, Port Angeles, the frequencies, the signal reports. He held the pen out to her.

She took it and wrote *Tyler Vance* in the column for the station worked, and under that, smaller, *Huntsville*, and then she gave him back his pen.
