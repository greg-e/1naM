# 8 — Sam, Day 0-1

The dark came down faster than the flight plan wanted it to, the way it always did once the sun actually committed to going, and by the time the GPS showed him crossing back into range of the mainland proper, there was nothing left outside the windscreen but instrument glow and the black, textureless nothing of the Gulf of Maine below him. He didn't mind the dark itself. Dark was just dark, something an instrument-rated pilot flew through on a hundred ordinary nights without a second thought. What he minded, what had been sitting low and cold in his stomach for the last twenty minutes, was the particular kind of dark waiting for him at the other end of this leg — a mainland he already knew, from a man he trusted, was losing its lights one region at a time.

He told himself it wouldn't be Bangor. Bangor was a real field. Bangor had been built to catch exactly this kind of airplane on exactly this kind of night, decades of Cold War contingency planning baked into a runway long enough to swallow anything that needed it. He held onto that the way he'd held onto a lot of things today, right up until the moment the GPS counted down the last ten miles and he still hadn't picked out a single light on the horizon where an entire small city was supposed to be glowing.

He tried the CTAF first, because the boring, ordinary explanation was always the one to rule out before the frightening one. Five clicks on the mic, the universal handshake for pilot-controlled lighting at a field with nobody home to flip a switch for him. Nothing answered. He circled a mile out and tried again, seven clicks this time for full intensity, in case the system needed a firmer hand than he'd given it. The ground stayed exactly as dark as it had been a minute ago. Not a runway edge light, not a beacon, not the glow of a single streetlight anywhere in what should have been a whole downtown's worth of illumination. Whatever backup power Bangor International kept for a night like this one, it had already run out, or never existed the way Newfoundland's apparently had, and there was nothing waiting down there for him but a fifty-thousand-pound airplane's worth of very bad reasons to attempt a landing on a runway he couldn't see.

The fear that hit him then was a different animal than anything he'd felt yet today — quieter than the grief, colder than the shock of finding his crew, a specific, focused animal terror that had nothing to do with dying men and dead families and everything to do with the simple, present fact that he was alone in the dark with a limited amount of fuel and nowhere yet confirmed to put this airplane down. He made himself breathe through it the way training had taught him to, climbed back to a safe altitude, and started working the actual problem instead of the fear of it.

He had options. Not many, not close together, but some — a scatter of small fields within range if he was willing to spend the fuel finding out which of them, if any, still had a pulse. Bar Harbor came up first on the chart, close enough to be worth a look, and he flew the fifteen minutes it cost him with his jaw tight the whole way, keyed the mic over a field that turned out to be nothing but a black gap in the tree line, no response, no lights, nothing. Augusta next, further inland, a longer detour that ate more of his fuel reserve than he liked watching go — same result, silence answering silence, a small municipal field that had never had much backup power to lose in the first place.

He was running out of easy answers and getting close to the point where he'd have to make a harder decision than he wanted to make tonight, when the old reflex finally surfaced — not a chart, not an app, just twenty-five years of a career that had started long before any of this, flying transports out of fields most civilians never thought about twice. Loring. Way up in Aroostook County, near enough the Canadian border to feel like an afterthought on most maps, a Strategic Air Command base once upon a time, built in an era when the entire point of a runway like that was surviving exactly the kind of catastrophe nobody expected to survive. Bases like that didn't run on the same fragile grid as a regional airport's terminal building. Bases like that were built with their own hardened power, redundant on redundant, designed by people who'd spent the Cold War assuming the rest of the country's lights might go out around them.

It was a gamble, and a long one — twenty extra minutes of fuel he'd rather have kept in reserve, spent on a hunch instead of a confirmed field. He took it anyway, because the alternatives were worse, and because some part of him trusted a decommissioned Air Force base to have been built by people as stubborn and thorough as the men who'd trained him.

The lights came up the second he keyed the mic, clean and full and unmistakable, a long pale ribbon of runway blooming out of nothing in the middle of all that dark like the whole night had been holding its breath waiting for him to ask the right question. Sam felt something in his chest come loose that he hadn't fully realized was clenched, a relief so total it was almost its own kind of pain, and flew the approach on hands that only started shaking once the wheels were actually down and rolling and there was nothing left to do but breathe.

He taxied clear, shut down on a stretch of ancient, oversized ramp built for aircraft ten times the size of his, and sat there a long moment in the sudden ringing quiet, forehead resting against the yoke, before he let himself feel any of it at all. Bangor was gone as an option. Whatever tomorrow's route looked like now, it looked different than the one he'd planned sitting on a fuel truck in St. John's — longer, further north than he'd meant to be, one more problem to solve on top of all the others. But he was down. He was alive. And somewhere south of here, a man he'd never met in person was still going to be standing on a ramp at four o'clock tomorrow, waiting.

That would have to be enough for tonight.
