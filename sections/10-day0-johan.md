# 10 — Johan, Day 0

Nobody woke him for the watch change.

In twenty-six years Johan had trained his body to a ship's rhythm well enough that he rarely needed the knock at his door, but this time he surfaced late, the light through the porthole already gray instead of black, the *Pacific Tender*'s engines running as steady beneath him as they had every hour of the crossing from Yokohama.

He dressed and went up to the bridge expecting to find Okafor irritated with him, ready with some dry comment about senior mates who slept through their own relief, and found him slumped forward against the console, one hand resting on the autopilot panel. The ship was holding her course where Okafor had last set her, the GPS track clean and unbroken on the display. Johan checked for a pulse, the same procedure that had carried him through two typhoons and a cargo-hold fire off Vladivostok, found nothing, and made himself keep moving.

The ship's clocks had run nine hours behind Greenwich the whole crossing. By the bridge log, nothing had been entered since just before nine the evening before. Johan did the arithmetic to Greenwich anyway, for something to hold onto.

He found Second Engineer Alvarez dead in the engine control room, upright in his chair with a logbook open in front of him, the entry unfinished mid-sentence. He found the cook dead against the galley counter, a pot on the stove gone dry and scorched black. He worked through the accommodation block after that, cabin by cabin, all twenty-two of them, and got the same answer every time: no struggle, no mess. It took him the better part of two hours to be sure. He was the only man alive aboard the *Pacific Tender*.

He tried the VHF first, working through every channel that mattered and getting nothing back but static. The single-sideband set gave him the same silence on frequencies that should have had coastal traffic at any hour. He pulled up the AIS display next and sat with it a long moment. A dozen other vessels showed within range, every one of them still making way, not one of them answering a call. A ghost fleet, steaming on through the North Pacific on whatever course its last watch had set, same as his.

The satellite terminal in the radio room was the last thing he tried, and for twenty minutes it gave him almost nothing: a handful of news fragments loading in slow, broken pieces before the connection gave out, half-sentences about the eastern seaboard, about Europe, about numbers that didn't finish loading before the page died. It wasn't only the *Pacific Tender*, then. It wasn't Japan, or the mainland he was steaming toward, or any one country.

Grief came for him too, eventually, sitting alone in a radio room built for six men: for Okafor, for Alvarez and his unfinished logbook entry, for the cook, whose name he was ashamed to find he'd have to look up on the crew manifest.

Then there was the actual problem, and Johan was a practical man. Twenty-two men dead did not change the fact that this ship was still three days from Seattle and still capable of getting there: engines sound, fuel more than sufficient, autopilot holding a course that would put her near the Strait of Juan de Fuca whether or not anyone touched the helm. He was a senior mate with an electrical engineer's understanding of half the systems keeping her alive, and he had stood watches alone before, if never for three straight days with no one to relieve him.

He climbed back up to the bridge in the flat gray light and took the helm off autopilot long enough to feel the ship answer to his hand.

"All right," he said out loud, to no one. "Seattle, then."

He put her back on course.
