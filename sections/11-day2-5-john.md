# 11 — John, Day 2-5

They stayed longer than any of them had planned.

The days at Grace's house had no deadline, no leg to fly, no address in a jacket pocket. Grace, in a cast and a pair of borrowed crutches, couldn't sit still: by the second day she'd found the breaker in the garage that had been tripping the well pump, rewired around it, and had Sam holding a flashlight while she rebuilt a carburetor on an old generator she said she'd been meaning to fix for a year. "First useful thing I've done since I got here that wasn't bleeding on somebody," she said.

Sam kept his promise to Tyler on the third clear night, the two of them out on the deck after the others had gone in, Sam working through star charts on his phone until he found it, a steady point of light crossing low over the water, no blink to it. "There," he said. Tyler watched it all the way to the horizon without saying anything. When he spoke it wasn't a question. "That's her window going by right now. Probably." Sam didn't correct the *probably*. He stood there with him until it was gone.

Two days in, John came down for water long after midnight and found Catherine alone at the kitchen sink, her shoulders moving, small and controlled, until she heard him on the stairs and they stopped. He didn't ask. He filled a glass, set it down next to her, and sat at the table until she was ready to talk or not, and she chose not to.

For John, the days were the first chance since Alpharetta to stop moving long enough to think. He set up in the spare bedroom Grace wasn't using, the workstation out of the back of the car and onto a real desk, its uplink antenna propped in the one window that faced clear sky. By the second afternoon Grace had come in, taken one look at his signal strength, and come back twenty minutes later with a length of coax and a scavenged directional antenna mounted on a painter's pole lashed to the deck railing. "You're getting maybe a third of what this thing can do," she said, running the cable in through the window frame. "Let's fix that." The bars on his screen doubled inside the hour.

He kept running the same passes he'd started that first night, new 911 traces filtered the same way, looking for another Grace, and found almost nothing three days running. It told him how few of them were left to find.

It was Tyler who pointed him at the thing that mattered. "Do planes still fly themselves like the one Sam found you on?" he asked one evening, watching John work. "What about boats? Do boats do that too?"

John hadn't checked. That first morning a diverted flight on FlightAware had led him to Sam, and he'd never thought to look for the same signature anywhere else. He pulled up an AIS tracking feed that night, one built for shipping brokers and marine insurers, and had the model run the same pattern-matching he'd used on the 911 logs, looking this time for a hand on the helm instead of a voice. He went to bed with it running.

By the third morning it had found a ghost fleet spread across the North Pacific, dozens of vessels still transmitting positions, still under way, every one holding a course set days ago that nobody had touched since. And one that didn't fit, a container ship, the *Pacific Tender*. Her track showed a controlled slowdown, a turn into Puget Sound, and an anchoring in Elliott Bay off Seattle, sometime the day before yesterday. No autopilot did that on its own.

He looked at it a long moment before he called Sam in from the porch.

"Somebody's flying this one too," Sam said, reading over his shoulder. "Same as mine. Same as yours, that first day."

"Same as mine," John agreed. "Except this one made port. Whoever's on that ship brought her in themselves."

Sam was already pulling up a chart on his phone. "Seattle's nothing from Portland. Forty minutes, maybe less."

"Then we go look," John said.

They told the others over dinner, Catherine reaching for the first-aid kit before John finished the sentence, Grace asking about the ship's size before anyone had confirmed there was a person aboard to meet, Tyler wanting to know if it was as big as an aircraft carrier. Nobody voted against it.

"Tomorrow, then," Sam said.
