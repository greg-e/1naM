# 12 — John, Day 2

Sam had the chart spread across both their knees before the engine was even fully warm, working the numbers out loud the way John had come to understand he worked everything that mattered. "Direct route's about thirteen hundred miles as the airways actually run it, not as the crow flies. Memphis, Little Rock, Tulsa, Amarillo, Albuquerque, then down into Phoenix past Winslow. Twenty-eight thousand feet is where this airplane wants to live for a leg this long — thin air, good true airspeed, fuel burn that doesn't bankrupt us."

"That sounds like a plan," John said.

"It's half a plan." Sam tapped the fuel column with his pencil, the same patient, unhurried tap John had watched him use on a chart in a different airplane two days ago. "Full tanks is four hundred two gallons usable. Call it twenty-seven hundred pounds of Jet-A. Run the burn for this whole distance — climb, five hours of cruise fighting a headwind the whole way, descent, plus the reserve I'm not willing to skip — and the number I keep landing on is closer to thirty-one hundred pounds needed." He let that sit a second. "That's not close. That's four hundred pounds of airplane that doesn't exist."

"So we don't make it."

"Not in one bite, no." Sam didn't sound bothered by it, which John was starting to recognize as its own kind of information — the sound of a man who'd rather find the hole on paper than find it at nine thousand feet over New Mexico. "Good news is the math actually gets easier than I guessed last night. I told you probably two stops. Real numbers say one does it, if we put it in the right place." His finger came down on a spot roughly two-thirds of the way across the route, right where the airway bent south toward the desert. "Amarillo. Sits almost exactly on the line we're already flying, real airport, real fuel, and it splits the trip into two legs short enough that neither one comes anywhere close to the edge of what this airplane can actually do. We're not guessing our way into a headwind out over nothing. We're planning the stop before we need it."

"Don't launch with known holes," John said, half to himself, the phrase he'd heard Sam use enough times now that it had started to feel like his own.

Sam glanced over, something almost like approval in it. "Now you're flying it right."

They were airborne within the hour, Huntsville falling away gray-green and quiet behind them, and once Sam leveled them off at altitude he did what he'd started doing every leg since PDK — unstrapped enough to turn fully toward John and nodded at the yoke. "She's yours for a while. Heading two-seven-zero, hold two-eight-oh, and don't fight the autopilot, just watch what it's already doing and tell me why." John flew it stiff at first, overcorrecting the way he remembered doing twenty-five years ago in a rented Cessna, and then, somewhere over the brown seam where Arkansas gave way to Oklahoma, stopped thinking about his hands entirely and just flew the airplane. Sam didn't say much. He didn't need to. Watching him not need to was its own kind of lesson.

Tyler woke somewhere past Tulsa, blinking against the light coming low and gold through the windows, and after a while he unbuckled and came forward far enough to kneel in the aisle behind the two front seats, chin on his folded arms, watching the ground scroll by ten thousand feet down. "Is that Texas?"

"Panhandle of it," Sam said. "We'll be on the ground there in about forty minutes. Just for fuel."

"Do you think—" Tyler started, then stopped, the way John had noticed he did whenever the sentence was headed somewhere he wasn't sure he wanted to go. He tried again, smaller. "When you go up high enough, can you ever actually see the space station? Like, for real, not just a light."

"Not from up here," Sam said, gentle about it. "We're not nearly high enough. But if the sky's clear tonight, wherever we end up, I'll help you find it. It moves fast once you know where to look."

Tyler nodded like that was a fair trade for now, and stayed kneeling there a while longer, quiet, watching Texas go by underneath a boy whose mother was somewhere directly overhead and impossibly far away at the same time.

Amarillo, when it came up out of the haze, looked like every other field they'd found this week — real runways, a real ramp, nobody home. Sam did the low pass out of habit more than necessity now, found it clean, and brought them down onto a general aviation apron with a self-serve pump that gave up its access panel almost apologetically compared to what St. John's had cost him. John held the nozzle this time while Sam ran the numbers on the second leg, a small, deliberate handoff neither of them commented on — useful, not just company. Forty minutes on the ground, tanks full, and they were airborne again with the sun still comfortably high and a clean, unhurried number written on Sam's kneeboard for the leg into Phoenix.

They picked up the desert a couple hours later, the ground turning red and folded and enormous under them, and Sky Harbor's runways swam up out of the haze looking bigger than anything they'd landed on all week — a real international field, wide enough that Sam's usual low pass felt almost small against it. He flew it anyway, unhurried, found the field clear, and turned them onto a long final with the city spread out dead and silent in every direction beneath them.

They were still rolling out on the runway when John saw it — a single car, moving fast and wrong, cutting across taxiway grass that wasn't built for it, aimed at a stretch of perimeter fence like the fence wasn't going to be a factor in the decision. It hit the chain-link at speed and went through it in a spray of torn metal and dust, fishtailed once on the ramp concrete, and came to a hard stop a hundred feet from where Sam was already braking them down to taxi speed.

"Don't shoot," Sam said dryly, easing the throttle back, "she got here faster than we did."

The woman who climbed out was maybe fifty, steady on her feet despite what her hands were doing, dressed like she'd come straight from a shift she hadn't clocked out of in three days. She didn't run at the airplane so much as walk at it with total certainty, the particular gait of someone who'd spent thirty years walking fast toward emergencies instead of away from them.

"I heard the engine from four blocks out," she called, before either of them had the door fully open. "I've been at that hospital since Tuesday morning because it was the only thing left that made sense to do, and I have not heard one single working engine in three days that wasn't my own car, so you'll forgive me for driving through a fence to make sure I wasn't imagining it." She stopped a few feet off the wing, looking between the airplane and the two men and the boy now peering out from behind them, something in her face cracking open for just a second before she caught it and put it back the way John was already learning everyone out here eventually had to. "Catherine Reyes. I'm an ER doctor, or I was, and I am extremely glad to see you."

"John," he said. "This is Sam. And Tyler."

"Huntsville," Catherine said, working it out loud, looking at Tyler with something soft crossing her professional composure. "You've been busy."

"We have a few more stops after this one too," Sam said. "But right now we just came a long way to find you."

Catherine looked at the torn fence behind her, then back at the three of them, and something that might have been the first real laugh any of them had heard in days came out of her, short and disbelieving. "Well," she said. "You found me."
