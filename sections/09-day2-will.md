# 9 — Will, Day 2

Catherine's things were in the back of the Subaru: a hard-sided medical bag with her name stenciled on the lid, a duffel, a case of bottled water. She carried the bag herself and let Sam take the duffel.

"There's one more," Will said. "A woman on the Olympic Peninsula, west of Port Angeles, Washington. She called 911 once, Monday night their time, and read it her coordinates. The location record puts her at the Elwha River bridge on Highway 101. We never got her on a line. We don't know if she's hurt, and we don't know if she's still there. If you'd rather stay here, set up somewhere safe, we'd understand."

Catherine looked at him like the question was slightly insulting. "I've spent three days in an empty office listening for engines because I didn't know what else a doctor was for anymore," she said. "You're telling me there's a woman who called for help and nobody came, and asking if I want to go?" She shifted the bag to her other hand. "Absolutely."

Sam fueled the airplane to full off a truck on the GA ramp while Will walked Catherine through the cabin and showed her where to strap in. Tyler moved his things to make room without being asked. Sam had the chart out on his knee before they'd closed the door. "Call it a thousand nautical, nonstop. Three hours forty with what the wind's been doing. Full tanks, and the most fuel to spare we've had on any leg." He said it like he didn't quite trust it. "First easy one."

"Then let's not waste it," Will said, and Sam pushed the power lever forward.

They climbed out over the valley at half past one. The desert gave way to the plateau, and the plateau to the Great Basin, brown and ridged, the Sierra standing up white-topped off the left side. Catherine sat behind Will with her knees drawn up and watched the ground. Somewhere over Nevada she asked Sam how much oxygen the cabin was holding them at, and he told her the cabin altitude, and she nodded and didn't say why she'd asked. Tyler fell asleep. North of the Columbia the Cascades came up green and black with the volcanoes standing out of them, and past Seattle the Olympics lifted out of the haze ahead, snow in the high bowls, and the water of the Strait beyond them catching the late sun.

Port Angeles sat on a strip of flat ground between the mountains and the Strait, with the long hook of a sandspit curving out into the harbor. William R. Fairchild International was on the west side of town, a single main runway on a bench above the water. Sam made his low pass down runway 8 at two hundred feet and looked it over. Clear. A row of T-hangars, a few airplanes tied down on the aprons, a red-and-white medical helicopter on a pad at the far end. Nothing moving. He landed long, the way he wanted, and taxied to the terminal. 17:20 local.

The FBO was in the terminal, Citizen Air on the glass, the door unlocked. Behind the counter a pegboard held rental contract folders and a ring of keys tagged CREW CAR. The car was a white sedan out front with a full tank, and it started on the first try.

The Elwha bridge was ten minutes west on 101, through second-growth fir and past a scatter of houses with their porch lights out. The highway came down out of the trees and ran straight onto the bridge, a concrete span high over the river, and on it, a little past the middle, two vehicles sat where they'd stopped. A pickup had come across the center line into the westbound lane and taken a small gray SUV on the driver's side front corner. The SUV was crushed back to the door pillar and pushed against the rail. The pickup's front end had folded around the impact, and it sat skewed across both lanes.

Will pulled over short of the bridge. Catherine was out before he'd set the brake.

The pickup's driver was still behind the wheel, a heavy man in a canvas coat, held upright by his belt, his head tipped against the side window. Catherine opened his door, checked him with two fingers, and then did something none of the rest of them had done: she pulled the collar of his coat back and looked at the skin of his neck and his hand where it lay on his thigh. The color was the same flushed pink Will had seen on the driver in the ditch on Old Milton.

"Cherry red," she said. "That's cyanide. Or carbon monoxide." She looked at the cab, the windows, the dash. "There's no fire, no exhaust leak, he wasn't parked in a garage. It's the same as every one I've looked at since Tuesday." She closed the door on him. "Every one."

The SUV was empty. The driver's door had been forced open from inside, and there was blood on the frame and on the asphalt below it, old and brown. A trail of it went to the bridge rail and along it to the end of the span, where the rail stopped and the bank fell away through salal and alder toward the river.

"Hello?" Sam called down. "Anybody down there?"

The river answered, and nothing else, and then, from under the bridge, a woman's voice, hoarse and flat: "Took you long enough."

She was in the gravel under the first span, where the bank leveled off above the water, sitting with her back against the concrete of the pier. A short, thickset woman in canvas work pants and a hi-vis vest gone gray with dirt, her black hair in a braid that had come half undone. Her left leg was splinted along both sides with two lengths of alder cut to size and bound with strips of a flannel shirt, the knots tied tight and square. An empty water bottle sat next to her, and a second one, half full of river water, beside it. Her phone lay face down on a flat rock, dead.

"Grace," she said, before anyone asked. "You're the 911 people."

"In a way," Will said. He crouched down to her level. "My name's Will. It logged your call. That's how we found you." He tipped his head at Catherine. "This is Catherine. She's an ER doctor."

Catherine was already on her knees beside the splint, her fingers working along it without disturbing the knots. "Who tied this?"

"I did."

"It's good." Catherine felt the foot, pressed a thumbnail, watched the color come back, and had Grace wiggle her toes. "Fibula, most likely. Lower third. You've been walking on it?"

"Crawling. Down here, and to the water."

Catherine nodded. "It's going to hurt getting you up that bank. I'm not going to pretend otherwise."

It took them half an hour, Tyler holding the flashlight and carrying the water bottles, Will and Sam with Grace's arms over their shoulders and Catherine below her steering the leg, a few feet at a time up through the brush. Grace didn't make a sound beyond her breath coming sharp through her teeth. At the top she sat on the guardrail with her face gray and her mouth open, breathing hard and fast, and stayed there a full minute before she would let them move her to the car.

Olympic Medical Center was on the east side of town, a low hospital on a hill above the harbor, the main building lit while every street around it was dark. Its own plant, Will figured, the backup generator every hospital in the country had to keep, with fuel for days and nothing in the building left to draw it down but the lights `[verify OMC generator]`. The ambulance doors opened for them. The emergency department was small, smaller than Catherine was used to; Will watched her take in the bay count and the single trauma room at a glance. The night shift was where they'd been at eleven o'clock Monday night: a nurse slumped at the station, a tech on the floor of the hall beside a supply cart. Catherine looked at each of them once and went on past.

She found X-ray herself and ran the portable unit without asking anyone how. The film came up on the screen in the reading room, and she leaned in to it. "Fibula. Distal third. Non-displaced, clean edges. You did this right." She cleaned and closed a gash on Grace's shin that had been bleeding into the splint for three days, gave her a tetanus shot and the first dose of an antibiotic out of the department's dispensing cabinet, and put her in a fiberglass cast from below the knee to the toes. Within the hour Grace was sitting up on a gurney with the leg on a pillow and a bag of fluids running into her arm.

Then Catherine took a pulse oximeter off the wall and clipped it to Grace's finger. Grace was still breathing fast, twenty-something breaths a minute, sitting still. The display settled at 98. Catherine looked at it a while. Then she clipped it to Sam's finger, and he coughed while it read, 98. Will, 97. Her own, 99. She wrote the four numbers on a strip of tape on the back of her hand, and when Will asked, she said, "Nothing yet," and put the oximeter in her bag.

They needed a place for the night that wasn't a hospital full of people Catherine had worked beside in everything but name. Two blocks from the harbor there was a motel, two stories of rooms opening onto outside walkways, a lobby with the night clerk dead behind the desk. Sam covered him with a blanket from a housekeeping cart. Will took a master key card off the clerk's desk and he and Sam walked the second floor of the east wing door to door, knocking first. Two of the rooms had people in them. They closed those doors again and wrote the numbers on the back of a towel card and taped it to the stair rail, and took the four empty rooms at the far end, and that was the end of what they could do about that.

There was a grocery three blocks up with its doors unlocked and its coolers dark. Will came back with pasta, garlic, a jar of olives, tomatoes going soft but not gone, a bottle of olive oil and a loaf of bread, and set up the camp stove from REI on a folding table on the walkway outside the end room, where the smell would go out over the parking lot. He'd never cooked for five people outdoors in his life. It steadied him to have something to do with his hands. Sam sat on the walkway with his back against the wall and Dan's head in his lap. Tyler sat on the steps. Catherine pushed Grace's wheelchair out of the room and parked it where she could put the cast up on a cooler, then sat down beside her and poured water for everyone from her case.

Grace looked down the walkway toward the motel's dark bar sign. "Bar's closed, I guess," she said, and nobody answered, and she let it go.

"This is good," Catherine said, around a mouthful of pasta, sounding almost offended. "Where did you learn to cook like this?"

"Waffle House, then sixteen years of living alone and getting bored of my own cooking," Will said.

They ate. Afterward, with the plates on the walkway and the harbor going dark below them, Grace told it, flat and in order, the way she'd have written up a fault. She'd been driving home from a late shift. She was a civilian mechanic at the Coast Guard air station out on the Hook, diesels and electrical, and she'd stayed late on a generator. Westbound on 101, a little before eleven. "Right before he came over the line I couldn't get a breath. Like somebody sat on my chest. I remember thinking I was having a heart attack at thirty-two. Then his lights were in my lane." She'd figured afterward it was panic, or the airbag. She'd come to in the SUV with her leg broken and the pickup's engine still running, and the man in it hadn't answered. Her door had taken her shoulder to open. She'd called 911 from the asphalt and gotten the voice, and read it her coordinates off her map app, and it had told her help was being dispatched. Then she'd looked at the sky and decided she wasn't waiting on the bridge in the open for help to come, and she'd gone down the bank to the water. She'd cut the splints with her pocketknife. Three days. She'd heard no cars and no airplanes until theirs, and when she'd heard it, she'd heard it go over low and come back around, and she'd known it was landing at Fairchild.

Catherine had gone still when Grace said *couldn't get a breath*. She didn't say anything. Will saw her look at the strip of tape on the back of her hand.

Over the second helping Catherine set her fork down and looked around the walkway. "There's something I should tell all of you," she said. "My brother runs a shop outside Reno. TruckHouse. Overland vehicles, campers built to live in anywhere, off the grid. I don't know if he's alive. I don't know if any of it's still standing. But if we're going to keep doing this, finding people, moving people, eventually an airplane isn't going to be enough, and that's the only place I know of that was built for whatever comes next."

Nobody answered right away. Out past the harbor a buoy bell rang, once and then again.

"Reno, then," Sam said finally. "Eventually."

"Eventually," Catherine agreed.
