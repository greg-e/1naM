# 3 — John, Day 0–1

It was not quite ten when John let himself back into the house, and Dan met him at the door a second time that morning as if the first homecoming hadn't fully counted. The knot under his ribs hadn't eased — he hadn't carried one like it since Naomi died, sixteen years back — and he had no appetite for the second breakfast he never skipped.

He didn't sit down. If he let his body stop before his mind had somewhere to point itself, the whole morning would land on him at once — so he went looking for a problem, a real one with edges, the kind you work instead of endure. There was only one that mattered yet: how big was this. He'd been circling it since the kitchen, since the frozen news feeds and the empty flight map and the stretch of GA-400 he'd stood on himself. How far did it reach, and how far down. That was the thing to find out first, before he let himself think about what a person did with the answer.

Thirty years of building things that weren't allowed to fail had taught him how. Never one sensor. A second source, a third, and the truth living wherever they disagreed.

He had firsthand already, and wished he didn't — three people that morning with no mark on them, one of them Harry from next door, twenty years a friend, gone cold in his own bed. But that was one street. One subdivision, one stretch of parkway. The screens said the whole country looked like that, and they were probably right, except that feeds go dark together for dull reasons all the time, and a career spent living inside telemetry had taught him its one dishonesty: it was a picture of a place, and long enough in it and you forgot it wasn't the place. He wasn't going to take *the entire country* on instruments alone. Not from a chair. He put Dan in the passenger seat and drove.

He was out the better part of six hours. He got onto 400 south the way he would have on any workday, easing wide around the rig still jackknifed across the lanes where the highway bridged over Old Milton, and about five miles down he found the thud that had woken him at five past two. Off the shoulder to the right, down the embankment and into the trees, a tanker truck lay burned to its frame — the cab folded and black, the trailer split open the whole length of it. Whatever it had been carrying had gone up all at once, hard enough to strip the leaves off the trees for fifty yards and scorch a bare fan of red clay around the wreck. And it was still going — not the truck now, the ground, a low grass fire creeping up the embankment and along the fence line with nobody coming to put it out, most of a day after it lit. Not a transformer, then.

There was nothing he could do about it standing there, but a fuel fire working its way into the trees off a state highway was about as real as an emergency got, and some stubborn part of him wanted the call that morning to have been a fluke — a queue backed up, a person thirty seconds from picking up. He pulled onto the shoulder and dialed 911 again. First ring this time. The same synthetic voice, the same even cadence about call volume and response times. He gave it the road and the nearest exit and the words *active wildfire, spreading, no units on scene* — flat and clean, a position report. It thanked him and said a unit would be dispatched as resources allowed. He asked for a person. It went back to the top of the script. He pulled back onto the highway and drove on, and didn't try again.

He kept south, toward the city, because south was where the people were, or had been — down through Sandy Springs, the long slide through Buckhead with 400 running between the office towers, the connector toward Midtown. Cars everywhere, stopped the way the cameras had shown him, except that in person you could see into them. A MARTA train sat motionless on the elevated track near Lindbergh, every window lit. Somewhere in Buckhead a dog trotted loose across six empty lanes and was gone. Nothing else was burning. He kept expecting to come around a curve into smoke and never did, and after the tanker the not-burning was somehow worse than fire would have been — the city perfectly intact, perfectly stopped. He passed a fire station with both bay doors up and both engines still inside. He drove past a hospital and made himself pull into the ambulance bay and get as far as the sliding doors, and turned around there. Twice he stopped for someone slumped in a stopped car and made himself check, two fingers to the neck the way he had on Old Milton, and both times it was the same, and after the second one he stopped stopping. Then he pointed the car north and drove home, Dan pressed warm against his side across the console, and the cameras had not lied, and he had not really expected them to.

It was mid-afternoon when he got back. Now he'd sit down.

His office was the last bedroom down the hall, a room that had given up pretending to be anything else years ago. Against the far wall, under its own quiet cooling, sat the workstation the company had issued him when he moved over to the public-sector group — a squared-off thing no bigger than a two-drawer cabinet that drew more power than the rest of the house put together and ran, locally and off the network, a build of the model his own team was still developing. He was cleared to have it. It came with a toolchain he used for evals — connectors into aviation telemetry, weather, the government data feeds his clearances let him touch — and an understanding, never written down anywhere, that he kept it aimed at test problems and not at anything real.

That afternoon he woke it up and aimed it at the realest problem there had ever been, and told himself, accurately, that all he was doing so far was looking.

He started with the roads, because the roads were his, professionally, and because roads don't lie. He pulled traffic-camera feeds the way he'd pulled them a thousand times before a bad commute, except now he pulled all of them — every state DOT system he had a way into, one metro at a time, working west. Atlanta he'd just driven. Charlotte matched it. Nashville, St. Louis, Dallas, Denver, Phoenix, the whole spine of California — the same still photograph in every city, scaled up or down: cars stopped at wrong angles across empty lanes, a few nosed gently into each other where they'd drifted together at low speed, no responders anywhere in any frame, nothing moving, nothing burning. It had been two in the morning everywhere at once. Almost nobody had been on the road, and the few who were had simply come to a stop. By evening he had a map with a great many pins in it and not one exception anywhere on it.

The sky he kept in a window in the corner of the screen the whole time, and the whole time it emptied. He'd seen it near-empty at eight that morning; now he watched the number actually come down, hour by hour, as airplanes that had been holding their last heading since two a.m. ran their tanks dry and fell off the display, one and two at a time, most of them over water. By the time the light went outside there were three left. All three were the long ones — flights that had launched off the far side of the world in the last hour before it happened and still had fuel because they'd been built to cross half the planet without stopping.

There wasn't anything else useful to do yet, and there was a great deal to learn, so he worked the evening the way he'd have worked a bad incident, pulling every feed he still had a path to and writing down what each one told him. Maritime AIS: hundreds of ships still under way in every ocean, every transponder reporting position and course and speed, not one of them answering a call — a whole shadow fleet steaming nowhere. The 911 networks: a scatter of them still up, every one answering in the same synthetic voice he'd argued with on the shoulder of Old Milton, running a script into an empty room. Distress beacons — the transmitters built into life rafts and pilots' vests and the tails of small aircraft — showed him a handful of activations worldwide in the last twenty-four hours. A handful. Each one a dot on a map that meant a person had been alive, and scared enough to reach for a switch. He sat with that number for a while.

And the grid. He had a view into grid telemetry that most people didn't, and it showed him a collapse already well along: generating stations dropping offline as the frequency wandered past tolerance and nobody stepped in to hold it, whole regions sagging, the nuclear plants doing the one thing they're built to do when the grid around them fails — scramming themselves safe and settling onto backup power with a clock now running on that. He could see which way it would go. Hydro would last longest, the big dams that don't need anyone in the room — the Pacific Northwest, Quebec, Newfoundland, patches of the Tennessee Valley. Everything else was days. His own corner of Georgia, sitting where it sat between a gas-fed grid and a pair of reactors, was not one of the good ones, and some cold working part of him filed that away as a problem for later with a date on it.

The first of the three went out a little before ten, out over the Pacific — he watched its speed fall off and its altitude start down, the marker holding its last point a while as if the system were waiting for it too, before it dropped off the map. The other two would go the same way before morning, somewhere over open water, and there was nothing to be done about either one and no reason to sit and watch a screen until they did. He marked where they were and left them flying.

He stood in the office doorway with the house dark around him and ran the accounting one more time. Every feed said the same thing. Every system said no one.

Which brought him back around to the one thing he'd set aside that first morning to do the bigger count first. A Delta flight out of Atlanta, bound for Paris, that had turned back off the ocean sometime after two a.m. — held a long racetrack over the water to burn down to landing weight, shot an approach to one field and climbed away from it, run a hundred and fifty miles down the coast and shot another, and finally set down soft on a ramp at Halifax. He'd watched the track do all of it. A machine holding its last instruction does not do that. Somebody had flown that airplane — careful, methodical, scared, in exactly the register John had been in himself for twenty hours now — and was sitting on that ramp with a working phone and no one to call. Delta 84. The only line in the whole picture that led to somebody alive. He wanted a name. He wanted a number. Under both of those, plainly, he wanted to hear a living voice say something back to him. And he was going to pull on it.

He knew exactly how, and that was the part that sat wrong. Somewhere in his twenties, before the degrees, before any of the rest of it, he had been very good at getting into systems he had no business being inside of — good enough that the government had made him a quiet, one-time offer instead of an example, and he had taken it and spent every year since on the other side of that line. Building the locks. Writing the tests that catch people doing what he used to do. Teaching a model, lately, to look at a request shaped exactly like the one he was about to make and say no to it.

The method was ugly and it was clear. Strip the refusals off the local model — he had the access, it was a research build and the safety layer came away in pieces if you knew where the pieces were seated — and run it alongside Claude, the shipped model, still answering on the API for now because somewhere a data center was still on backup power. The freed one to do the things a deployed system wouldn't. The production one for reach. Point the pair of them, through his own tooling, at Delta's crew-scheduling system, and then at the personnel records sitting behind it. Flight 84, September 5. Three names on the flight deck. Three contact numbers in an HR database that no longer had an HR department attached to it.

He stood there and let himself feel the weight of it honestly, because it had earned that much from him. Then he did the arithmetic, the way he did everything, and the weight came apart in his hands. There was no operator to harm — nothing behind any of those systems but automation running down. The privacy he'd be stepping on belonged, almost certainly, to the dead — and to exactly one person who was not dead, who was sitting alone right now in an airport built for people with nowhere else to go, and who needed a phone to ring more than he needed his personnel file kept sealed. There was no competing harm on the other side of the ledger. There was nothing on the other side of the ledger at all. It was the easiest hard decision he had ever made, and he sat down and set it running.

Then there was nothing to do but let it work — the freed model probing, Claude reasoning through what it turned up, his own tooling carrying the two of them from one system into the next the way water finds its way downhill, none of it needing him to sit and watch. He had been awake since six that morning. He'd stood in a ditch and a median and a friend's bedroom, driven the length of a dead city, watched the sky come down to its last three. He left the machine running, lay down on the office couch where he could hear its fans, and was gone before he'd decided to be.

He woke a little after six into a Wednesday, stiff, first light gray in the window and the workstation still going through whatever he'd left it doing. He didn't look at it right away. He did instead the thing his whole body knew how to do without being asked: the shower, hot; the blender, the same blueberry-avocado-beet-spinach shake he made every morning of his life, drunk standing at the counter; the leash off its hook, Dan turning circles by the door. He walked the two miles, quick about it. Past the Pattersons' with no bus and no kids, past Harry's dark house, out to Old Milton where the car still sat nose-down in the ditch and the box truck still sat in the median exactly where they'd been the day before, and back. It didn't help, exactly. It just held the morning to a shape he could stand inside while he got ready to do the rest.

Standing at the counter afterward, glass empty, he pulled up the flight map on his phone. It was empty too — not near-empty, empty. The last track, the one that had been alone over the Atlantic when he lay down, had wound down and dropped off somewhere out there right around two in the morning — twenty-four hours after it all started, near enough to the minute. He'd slept through the end of it. A hundred years of the sky never once being empty, and it had finished while he was on the couch.

Then he went to the office and looked. The machine had finished in the night. Scheduling had given up the pairing first: Captain, First Officer, Relief Pilot, three employee numbers logged against Delta 84 on the fifth. Personnel had given up the rest. Three names he had not had the day before, and a mobile number written beside each one on the notepad in handwriting that had steadied as the list got shorter.

He pulled up FlightAware before he touched the phone, some part of him needing to know the thing he was about to call was still real. The Delta 84 page still held the same frozen track, ending at Halifax where the airplane had rolled to a stop the morning before. Nothing new had moved down there since — no airplane, no vehicle he could see, no way at all to know whether the person who'd flown that approach was still on that ramp or had walked off into a dead city and kept walking. The only thing that said otherwise was a name and a number on a notepad.

He used his own phone. The house hadn't had a landline in years, and the bars were down to one and flickering, and he decided one was enough. He sat with the three names for the better part of an hour, watching the light come all the way up in the window, before he could make himself dial the first.

He called the First Officer first, going down the list in order without letting himself think about why the order might matter. It rang, and rang, the flat endless kind he had already learned to expect, and no one picked up. He called the Relief Pilot. That one went to voicemail on the second ring — a man's voice, easy, unhurried, telling him to leave a message, recorded on some ordinary afternoon by someone who'd had no idea it would be the last thing his phone ever said. John hung up before the tone.

He looked at the third name a moment. The Captain — the one who'd have been in command, the one most likely, if it was anyone, to have been the hands on that careful approach. He dialed it.

It rang once. Twice. A third time — and then a fumble, a caught breath, and a voice, rough and wary and entirely alive: "Hello?"

For a second nothing would come. Then John got it out, level, the way it had come out on the shoulder of Old Milton. "My name's John Lauer. I'm in Atlanta." A silence on the line — but no click. "You flew a Delta out of here two nights ago. Turned it around over the water and put it down at Halifax. I watched the whole track come in. I've spent a day and a half trying to work out who was flying it."

The breath again, and when the voice came back it had cracked straight down the middle. "Yeah," the man said. "Yeah — that was me." A pause he didn't fill. "I didn't think anybody was—" and he stopped, and John stood in the office with the phone pressed hard to his ear and listened to a living man be unable to finish the sentence.

"You're not the only one," John said. "That's the whole reason I called. You're not the only one."

It was a voice — Sam's, the first name on the notepad — a real one, coming back down a line he had spent two days building toward. He put his free hand flat on the desk to steady it, and for the first time since the thud woke him at five past two John let himself feel something that wasn't dread.

The call didn't last long; neither of them trusted it to. They talked over each other in bursts, each trying to get the other one caught up before the line quit — Sam's crew, both of them gone at the controls while he slept; the fog, the field he couldn't get into, the one that landed itself; a hotel bed he didn't remember. Then, from John's side, the short honest version of the two days behind him: the empty sky, the one track that wasn't holding a dead heading, Delta's crew records opened by a machine that was never built to open them. "There was nobody left to ask," John said, and Sam made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh and said he didn't think he cared.

Then the breathing on the other end changed — a man working himself up to something. "My wife," Sam said. "Elena. And my daughter — Maya. They're in Marietta." A breath he had to push through the middle of. "I've been calling since Halifax. Nothing, neither of them. And I've got a whole day of flying between me and Atlanta, and no way to that house even after I'm down." A pause. "You said Alpharetta. You're twenty minutes from them. Would you go — not call, go, stand in it, and then tell me what's true."

"I'll go." It was out before John had finished deciding it, and he meant it more than he'd meant anything since the thud woke him.

They squared what they could while the line held — both numbers, said twice each way; the Marietta address, which Sam gave slow and careful, like a thing he'd had by heart for fifteen years and didn't trust himself with today. The connection was fraying by the end, his voice landing a half-beat behind itself.

"Whatever you find over there," Sam said, from somewhere near the edge of the static. "Tell me straight. Don't try to soften it. I'd rather know."

"I will."

The line dropped a few seconds after that — no goodbye, just the flat nothing of a call that had ended itself. John held the phone a moment, then set it down.

He didn't pack anything; he'd be back by lunch. He filled Dan's water, put the good flashlight in his jacket in case a house was dark inside, and stopped once in the office doorway on his way past — the workstation still humming through everything he'd asked of it, still turning up things he hadn't looked at yet. It would keep. This wouldn't.

Dan in the passenger seat. Out of the driveway, south, toward Marietta, into a city holding as still as it had held since two in the morning the day before.
