# 3 — John, Day 0–1

It was a little after ten when John let himself back into the house, and Dan met him at the door a second time that morning as if the first reunion hadn't fully counted. He didn't sit down. Sitting down felt like the one thing he couldn't afford yet — some instinct telling him that if he let his body stop before his mind had somewhere to point itself, the whole morning would land on him at once. He needed a problem. A real one, with edges, the kind you work instead of endure.

He had one, and he'd been carrying it since the kitchen: the Delta flight, bent off its route to Paris and set down, deliberately, on a runway in the middle of Newfoundland that was lit for nobody. Someone had flown that approach. Someone had run it down toward the coast, pulled off it, and tracked a hundred miles inland to try again somewhere else — John had watched the track do it — and that was a person thinking, being careful, being scared, in exactly the register he was thinking in himself right now. He wanted a name. He wanted a phone number, and under that, plainly, he wanted to hear a living voice say something back to him before the day found another way to get worse.

But not yet. Thirty years had taught him not to move on the first data point when more were still coming, and the truth was he didn't actually know yet what he was standing inside of. Before he pulled on that one thread he was going to find out how big the rest of it was.

His office was the last bedroom down the hall, a room that had given up pretending to be anything else years ago. Against the far wall, under its own quiet cooling, sat the workstation the company had issued him when he moved over to the public-sector group — a squared-off thing no bigger than a two-drawer cabinet that drew more power than the rest of the house put together and ran, locally and off the network, a build of the model his own team was still developing. He was cleared to have it. It came with a toolchain he used for evals — connectors into aviation telemetry, weather, the government data feeds his clearances let him touch — and an understanding, never written down anywhere, that he kept it aimed at test problems and not at anything real.

This morning he woke it up and aimed it at the realest problem there had ever been, and told himself, accurately, that all he was doing so far was looking.

He started with the roads, because the roads were his, professionally, and because roads don't lie. He pulled traffic-camera feeds the way he'd pulled them a thousand times before a bad commute, except now he pulled all of them — every state DOT system he had a way into, one metro at a time, working west. Atlanta he already knew. Charlotte matched it. Nashville, St. Louis, Dallas, Denver, Phoenix, the whole spine of California — the same still photograph in every city, scaled up or down: cars stopped at wrong angles across empty lanes, a few nosed gently into each other where they'd drifted together at low speed, no responders anywhere in any frame, nothing moving, nothing burning. It had been two in the morning everywhere at once. Almost nobody had been on the road, and the few who were had simply come to a stop. By mid-afternoon he had a map with a great many pins in it and not one exception anywhere on it.

The sky he kept in a window in the corner of the screen the whole time, and the whole time it emptied. He'd seen it near-empty at eight that morning; now he watched the number actually come down, hour by hour, as airplanes that had been holding their last heading since two a.m. ran their tanks dry and fell off the display, one and two at a time, most of them over water. By the time the light went outside there were three left. All three were the long ones — flights that had launched off the far side of the world in the last hour before it happened and still had fuel because they'd been built to cross half the planet without stopping.

He stayed up with them. There wasn't anything else useful to do and there was a great deal to learn, so he worked the night the way he'd have worked a bad incident, pulling every feed he still had a path to and writing down what each one told him. Maritime AIS: hundreds of ships still under way in every ocean, every transponder reporting position and course and speed, not one of them answering a call — a whole shadow fleet steaming nowhere. The 911 networks: a scatter of them still up, every one answering in the same synthetic voice he'd argued with on the shoulder of Old Milton, running a script into an empty room. Distress beacons — the transmitters built into life rafts and pilots' vests and the tails of small aircraft — showed him a handful of activations worldwide in the last twenty-four hours. A handful. Each one a dot on a map that meant a person had been alive, and scared enough to reach for a switch. He sat with that number for a while.

And the grid. He had a view into grid telemetry that most people didn't, and it showed him a collapse already well along: generating stations dropping offline as the frequency wandered past tolerance and nobody stepped in to hold it, whole regions sagging, the nuclear plants doing the one thing they're built to do when the grid around them fails — scramming themselves safe and settling onto backup power with a clock now running on that. He could see which way it would go. Hydro would last longest, the big dams that don't need anyone in the room — the Pacific Northwest, Quebec, Newfoundland, patches of the Tennessee Valley. Everything else was days. His own corner of Georgia, sitting where it sat between a gas-fed grid and a pair of reactors, was not one of the good ones, and some cold working part of him filed that away as a problem for later with a date on it.

The last three airplanes went out over the course of the night. The first before ten, somewhere over the Pacific. The second a little after midnight. He made himself stay awake for the third, a track that had been alone over the middle of the Atlantic for hours, and somewhere around two in the morning — twenty-four hours, almost to the minute — he watched its speed decay and its altitude start down, and then the position marker just stopped, held its last point for a while as if the system were waiting for it too, and dropped off the map. He refreshed. Nothing moved on it anywhere in the world. Not one aircraft. The sky was empty for the first time in a hundred years, and he was reasonably sure he was the only person anywhere watching it happen.

He slept four hours and woke into a Wednesday with an empty sky.

He had to go see it. The screens had been honest with him and he trusted the screens, but there is a difference between knowing a thing through instruments and knowing it, and he had spent his whole career on the wrong side of that difference — trusting the telemetry so completely he'd half forgotten it was a picture of a place and not the place. He put Dan in the passenger seat and drove.

He was out about four hours. He went south, toward the city, because south was where the people were, or had been. Roswell Road, then the long slide down through Buckhead, then Midtown, the towers standing there with their lobby lights on and nobody in them. Cars everywhere, stopped the way the cameras had shown him, except that in person you could see into them. A MARTA train sat motionless on the elevated track near Lindbergh, every window lit. Somewhere in Buckhead a dog trotted loose across six empty lanes and was gone. Nothing was on fire — it had happened at two in the morning, no stoves on, no cigarettes going — and the not-burning was somehow worse, a city perfectly intact and perfectly stopped. He passed a fire station with both bay doors up and both engines still inside. He drove past a hospital and made himself pull into the ambulance bay and get as far as the sliding doors, and turned around there. Twice he stopped for someone slumped in a stopped car and made himself check, two fingers to the neck the way he had on Old Milton, and both times it was the same, and after the second one he stopped stopping. He turned around somewhere past the Perimeter with Dan pressed warm against his side across the console, and drove home, and the cameras had not lied, and he had not really expected them to.

Back in the house before noon he stood in the office doorway and ran the accounting one more time. Every feed said the same thing. Every system said no one. Except one — a track on a map, more than a day old now, that had circled a field and looked it over and gone missed and tried again and finally set down soft. A person, deciding things, on a ramp in Newfoundland with a working phone he couldn't reach anybody with. Delta 84. It was the only line in the whole picture that led to somebody alive, and John was going to pull on it.

He knew exactly how, and that was the part that sat wrong. Somewhere in his twenties, before the degrees, before any of the rest of it, he had been very good at getting into systems he had no business being inside of — good enough that the government had made him a quiet, one-time offer instead of an example, and he had taken it and spent every year since on the other side of that line. Building the locks. Writing the tests that catch people doing what he used to do. Teaching a model, lately, to look at a request shaped exactly like the one he was about to make and say no to it.

The method was ugly and it was clear. Strip the refusals off the local model — he had the access, it was a research build and the safety layer came away in pieces if you knew where the pieces were seated — and run it alongside Claude, the shipped model, still answering on the API for now because somewhere a data center was still on backup power. The freed one to do the things a deployed system wouldn't. The production one for reach. Point the pair of them, through his own tooling, at Delta's crew-scheduling system, and then at the personnel records sitting behind it. Flight 84, September 5. Three names on the flight deck. Three contact numbers in an HR database that no longer had an HR department attached to it.

He stood there and let himself feel the weight of it honestly, because it had earned that much from him. Then he did the arithmetic, the way he did everything, and the weight came apart in his hands. There was no operator to harm — nothing behind any of those systems but automation running down. The privacy he'd be stepping on belonged, almost certainly, to the dead — and to exactly one person who was not dead, who was sitting alone right now in an airport built for people with nowhere else to go, and who needed a phone to ring more than he needed his personnel file kept sealed. There was no competing harm on the other side of the ledger. There was nothing on the other side of the ledger at all. It was the easiest hard decision he had ever made, and he sat down and started.

It took a couple of hours, and it was neither fast nor clean — the freed model probing, Claude reasoning through what it turned up, John steering, the whole thing working patiently through one system into the next the way water finds its way downhill. Scheduling gave up the pairing first: Captain, First Officer, Relief Pilot, three employee numbers logged against Delta 84 on the fifth. Personnel gave up the rest. By the middle of the afternoon he had three names he had not had that morning, and a mobile number written beside each one in handwriting that had steadied as the list got shorter.

He used his own phone. The house hadn't had a landline in years, and the bars were down to one and flickering, and he decided one was enough.

He called the First Officer first, going down the list in order without letting himself think about why the order might matter. It rang, and rang, the flat endless kind he had already learned to expect, and no one picked up. He called the Relief Pilot. That one went to voicemail on the second ring — a man's voice, easy, unhurried, telling him to leave a message, recorded on some ordinary afternoon by someone who'd had no idea it would be the last thing his phone ever said. John hung up before the tone.

He looked at the third name a moment. The Captain — the one who'd have been in command, the one most likely, if it was anyone, to have been the hands on that careful approach. He dialed it.

It rang once. Twice. On the third ring something caught in the line — a small mechanical hitch, a breath drawn in on the far end — and then a man's voice, rough and wary and unmistakably, impossibly alive, said the one word that made John's whole body go loose with a relief so sudden it was almost indistinguishable from grief.

"Hello?"

John opened his mouth and found, for a second, that he didn't have a single one of the words he'd been saving all day. "My name's John," he finally managed. "I'm in Atlanta. I saw your flight yesterday morning — the diversion, into Gander." The line held, open, still there. "Is this the captain of Delta 84?"

There was a long silence on the other end, long enough that John's stomach dropped with the certainty that he was about to lose it, that whatever was left of the connection would give out before either of them said anything that mattered. Then the voice came back, quieter, stripped down to something that had been underneath it the whole time.

"Yeah," Sam said. "Yeah, it's me. How did you even—" He stopped himself, and John heard him breathe once, hard, on the other end of a line that had suddenly become the only thing holding the whole day together. "Is there anyone else out there? Please tell me I'm not the only one."

"You're not," John said. "I'm right here."
