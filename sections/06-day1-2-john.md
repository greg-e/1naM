# 6 — John, Day 1-2

Huntsville International came up out of the haze looking almost too ordinary to trust — real runways, a real tower, a terminal building catching the last low sun without a single light on inside it. Sam did the low pass anyway, the same unhurried circuit he'd have flown at a field he'd known for twenty years, and only lined up for real once he'd satisfied himself the runway was clear and nothing on it was going to surprise them. "Old habits," he said, when John didn't ask. "The kind you hold onto tightest the week everything else stops working."

They found the rental car lot at the edge of the airport — rows of them sitting exactly where an agency had left them, keys in the consoles, tanks topped off by whatever routine servicing had happened right before everything stopped — and picked one that started clean on the first turn, no hesitation in it at all. It had only been a few days. Batteries hadn't had time to die yet, and gas hadn't had time to go bad. John read the address off his notebook while Sam drove, streets emptying out around them the same way every street had emptied out for two days now — until they didn't, three turns from the house, when John caught himself staring at a working traffic signal cycling green to yellow to red for nobody, porch lights on down half a block like it was any ordinary evening. "That's not the grid holding," he said, working it through out loud because it helped to say it. "Not really. This whole region's on the same interconnection, and by now that zone's supposed to be dark end to end. This is smaller than that — a pocket. Whatever's feeding this block happened to end up balanced, generation and load close enough that nothing ever tripped. It happened for real once, back in '03 — a few blocks of western New York rode out a blackout nobody could explain for days while everything around them went black. Accidental. Won't last." It didn't feel like good news exactly. It felt like the kind of detail that mattered enough to hold onto anyway.

The house was a single-story brick ranch with a basketball hoop bolted over the garage — a detail that snagged at John harder than it should have, an echo of a different driveway two days and a lifetime ago — and the front door gave when Sam tried it, unlocked the way so many doors had turned out to be. They found a  man in the living room, stretched out on a couch in front of a television that had gone dark on its own sometime since, one arm hanging off the cushion in a way that told the whole story before either of them got close enough to check. John did the check anyway, out of the same grim discipline that had followed him through every one of these rooms so far, and got the same answer he always got.

The house past that point was silent in a way that felt different from the silence everywhere else — not empty silence, occupied silence, the specific quiet of someone deliberately, carefully not making a sound. They found the closed door at the end of the hall with a wooden name plaque on it, block letters spelling out TYLER over a hand-painted rocket ship, and John's chest did something complicated at the sight of it that he didn't have time to examine.

He knocked soft, twice. "Tyler? My name's John. This is Sam. We're not going to hurt you. We just want to make sure you're okay."

Nothing, for long enough that John started composing what he'd say next. Then, muffled through the door, small and cracked from disuse: "Are you real?"

Something in Sam's face cracked too, hearing that. "We're real, buddy," he said, crouching down so his voice came from lower, closer to whatever height a scared kid needed a voice to come from. "I'm a pilot. I flew a plane here. John found you on a computer, from all the way in Atlanta. We've been looking for people since Tuesday, same as I bet you've been hoping somebody would."

A long pause. Then the lock turned.

The boy who opened the door was smaller than John had pictured somehow, even knowing the address belonged to a kid — ten years old going on some much older, wearier version of himself, eyes red-rimmed and wary, a blanket wrapped around his shoulders like armor despite the warm evening. He looked at both of them for a long moment, cataloguing, deciding something. "I called 911 like eleven times," he said, like it needed defending. "The voice kept telling me help was coming. It never came."

"It sent us instead," John said. "Just took us longer than either of us wanted."

Tyler's eyes went past them, down the hall, toward the living room, and something closed over his face that was much too old for ten. "I already know about my dad," he said, flat, like he'd practiced saying it that way so it wouldn't come out any other way. "I found him. That's why I locked the door. I didn't know what else — " He stopped. Started over, smaller. "I didn't want to see him again."

"You don't have to," Sam said, immediate and certain. "You never have to again, if you don't want to. That's not your job anymore. That's ours."

Nobody had eaten a real meal since John couldn't remember, and it was Sam who noticed the boy swaying slightly on his feet and turned the whole evening toward the single most practical thing left to fix. They found a Waffle House four blocks over, sign still lit from whatever patch of grid was keeping this stretch of the county alive, and the parking lot told its own quiet story before they'd even gone inside — two cars pulled into employee spots around back, both occupied, both still, the unmistakable shape of two people who'd stepped out for a break sometime after two in the morning and never stepped back in. Neither John nor Sam mentioned it to Tyler. The restaurant itself, when they went in through the propped front door, was exactly as its staff had left it mid-shift — register open, a rag draped over the counter edge, the grill cold but otherwise untouched, nobody at all inside.

"Sit," Sam told Tyler, steering him into a booth with the easy authority of a man who'd apparently decided, somewhere over the last five minutes, that this was now also his job. John was already rounding the counter without a word, tying on an apron off a hook by reflex forty years old before he'd fully decided to. He hadn't touched a flat-top since he was twenty-two, paying his way through Georgia Tech one overnight shift at a time, and his hands remembered it anyway — the grill still had heat banked in the iron even cold, and he had it working again inside a minute. He came out from behind the counter twenty minutes later with a plate in each hand: hash browns scattered and smothered the way he used to plate them at three in the morning for truckers and drunks and, now and then, somebody who just needed a booth and someplace warm to sit a while — eggs over easy, a waffle with the edges gone dark the way he'd always liked his own.

"Where'd you learn to do that?" Sam asked, something almost startled in it.

"Told you. Worked the grill line through school." John set the plate down in front of Tyler and didn't say the rest of it out loud — that every hand who'd worked this line with him twenty-some years back was probably gone now, same as everyone, and here he was doing it again for exactly one person instead of a full counter, a scared kid instead of a drunk trucker, and it still felt like the same thing underneath: the only kindness he'd ever really had to offer at three in the morning was a hot plate and somewhere to sit.

Tyler ate like someone who hadn't in a while, careful and then not careful at all, and somewhere between the eggs and the waffle he started talking — not about his father, not yet, but about his mom. "She's not here because she's not *here*," he said, like he'd explained it a hundred times to people who never quite believed him. "She's an astronaut. NASA. She's been up on the space station since March." He said it plain, matter-of-fact, the one solid thing in his whole week that hadn't come apart on him.

Sam's fork stopped halfway to his mouth. John watched it land on him — not just *a mother*, but a specific woman, alive as far as either of them knew, sealed inside a few hundred tons of aluminum two hundred and fifty miles up, with no way of knowing whether the planet under her still had anyone breathing on it, and a ten-year-old son down here who might be the only proof left that it did. "She know?" Sam asked, quiet. "About — any of it?"

"I don't know how she'd find out," Tyler said, and for the first time all night his voice wasn't quite steady. "I used to watch her go over at night. I still do. I don't know if she can even see down here well enough to tell anything's different." He looked up, and something in the question underneath the question was aimed straight at Sam now, pilot to pilot, like he'd decided somewhere in the last thirty seconds that this was a man who might actually know. "Can they even land it? If they wanted to?"

"I don't know," Sam admitted, and didn't dress it up as anything better than it was. "But somebody built that thing to come home eventually. That much I know." He didn't say the rest of what was plainly moving behind his eyes — that a woman circling the planet every ninety minutes, watching the lights go out city by city with no one to radio and ask why, might be living through something worse than anything either man at this table had survived so far.

Tyler didn't ask yet whether his mother was still alive down here in the sense that mattered to him — whether anyone on the ground would ever get word to her, or she to him. John understood, watching him decide not to, that some questions a kid holds onto for as long as he can stand to, because the moment before you ask is still a moment where the answer could be anything. "Maya used to order hers just like that," Sam said after a while, quieter, nodding at the scattered hash browns on Tyler's plate — something to say that wasn't the space station, a small kindness offered sideways — and Tyler didn't ask who Maya was, just nodded back like the name meant something to him anyway.

"We'll figure that out," John told him anyway, gently, before Tyler could ask and before he could stop himself from promising something he didn't yet know how to deliver. "Not tonight. But we will."

Tyler looked at him for a long moment, the same cataloguing look from the bedroom doorway, and whatever he found apparently satisfied him, because he just nodded and went back to his waffle.

They drove back to the airport in the last of the light with Tyler asleep before they'd even cleared the parking lot, worn out in the specific, total way John imagined only a child who'd been alone with a locked door and a dead parent for two days could be worn out. Sam carried him from the car to the aircraft without waking him, laid him across the back row of seats with a jacket for a pillow, and came back up to the cockpit to find John sitting very still with both hands pressed flat against his own knees, not moving, not quite breathing right.

It came out of him all at once and without warning, the way he imagined it must have come out of Sam somewhere on his own way south — not for Elena and Maya, not even for the man on the couch four blocks back, but for a ten-year-old boy with a name plaque shaped like a rocket ship who'd sat alone behind a locked door for two days doing math on how long it would take before anyone came, and had gotten the answer *too long* before he'd gotten the answer *at all*. John hadn't cried yet. Not once, not for any of it, not standing over a stranger's body on his own road or covering Elena's shoulders or hearing Sam's voice go hollow on a dying phone line. It was Tyler's blanket wrapped around his shoulders like armor that finally did it, some small, specific, unbearable detail that broke through everything the last two days had trained him to hold shut, and he put his face in his hands in the front seat of a stranger's airplane and let it happen.

Sam didn't say anything. He didn't try to fix it, didn't offer the kind of hollow reassurance neither of them had any real use for anymore. He just sat down in the other seat and stayed there, one hand resting briefly on John's shoulder and then just present beside him, the two of them keeping quiet watch over a sleeping kid while one of them finally, fully broke and the other one held the space for it without asking him to stop.

It didn't last long. It wasn't supposed to. When John finally sat back up, wiped his face with the heel of his hand, and let out a breath that shook only a little on the way out, Sam still hadn't said a word, and somehow that was exactly the right amount of company. They sat up front a while longer after that, not talking much, listening to a kid breathe in his sleep like it was the most reassuring sound either of them had heard all week.

They slept in shifts in the cabin, parked on a ramp gone fully dark once whatever patch of grid had lit up the Waffle House finally gave out somewhere around midnight, and woke to a sky doing the same colorless gray thing it had done every morning since Tuesday, a whole different life ago. Tyler was quiet over what breakfast they could scrounge from a vending machine that still, against every reasonable expectation, took a dead man's abandoned dollar bills. He didn't cry. He asked, once, very small, whether they were leaving his dad here, and John told him the truth — that they were, that there wasn't a better answer available to any of them today, and that it didn't mean anyone had stopped caring about what was left in that house. Tyler nodded like he'd expected exactly that answer and hadn't needed to hear it made prettier than it was.

"Phoenix next," Sam said, once they were airborne, climbing out over a Huntsville that was already, mercifully, shrinking behind them. "Long way. We'll need a fuel stop, probably two, and we'll figure that out properly instead of guessing at it." He glanced back once at Tyler, already half-asleep again against the window, then at John. "Whoever's out there this time, we get to them faster than we got to him. That's the only version of today I'm interested in."

"Then let's fly it that way," John said, and they did.
