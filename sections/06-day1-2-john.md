# 6 — John, Day 1-2

Huntsville International came up out of the haze looking like any airport John had flown into a hundred times — real runways, a real tower, a terminal building catching the last low sun without a single light on inside it. Sam did the low pass anyway, one slow circuit over the runway to check it clear, before he lined up for real. "Old habits," he said, when John didn't ask. "The kind you hold onto tightest the week everything else stops working."

They found the rental car lot at the edge of the airport: rows of them sitting exactly where an agency had left them, keys in the consoles, tanks topped off by whatever routine servicing had happened right before everything stopped. They picked one that started clean on the first turn, no hesitation in it at all. It had barely been two days. Batteries hadn't had time to die yet, and gas hadn't had time to go bad. John read the address off his notebook while Sam drove, streets emptying out around them the same way every street had emptied out for two days now. Then they didn't. Three turns from the house, John caught himself staring at a working traffic signal cycling green to yellow to red for nobody, porch lights on down half a block like it was any ordinary evening. "That's not the grid holding," he said, working it through out loud because it helped to say it. "Not really. This whole region's on the same interconnection, and by now that zone's supposed to be dark end to end. This is smaller than that — a pocket. Whatever's feeding this block happened to end up balanced, generation and load close enough that nothing ever tripped. It happened for real once, back in '03 — a few blocks of western New York rode out a blackout nobody could explain for days while everything around them went black. Accidental. Won't last."

The house was a single-story brick ranch with a basketball hoop bolted over the garage, a detail that snagged at John harder than it should have, an echo of a different driveway two days and a lifetime ago. The front door gave when Sam tried it, unlocked the way so many doors had turned out to be. They found a man in the living room, stretched out on a couch in front of a television that had gone dark on its own sometime since, one arm hanging off the cushion in a way that told the whole story before either of them got close enough to check. John did the check anyway, out of the same grim discipline that had followed him through every one of these rooms so far, and got the same answer he always got.

The house past that point was silent in a way that felt different from the silence everywhere else — occupied silence, the specific quiet of someone deliberately, carefully not making a sound. They found the closed door at the end of the hall with a wooden name plaque on it, block letters spelling out TYLER over a hand-painted rocket ship, and John's chest did something complicated at the sight of it that he didn't have time to examine.

He knocked soft, twice. "Tyler? My name's John. This is Sam. We're not going to hurt you. We just want to make sure you're okay."

Nothing, for long enough that John started composing what he'd say next. Then, muffled through the door, small and cracked from disuse: "Are you real?"

Something in Sam's face cracked too, hearing that. "We're real, buddy," he said, crouching down so his voice came from lower, closer to whatever height a scared kid needed a voice to come from. "I'm a pilot. I flew a plane here. John found you on a computer, from all the way in Atlanta. We've been looking for people since Tuesday, same as I bet you've been hoping somebody would."

A long pause. Then the lock turned.

The boy who opened the door was smaller than John had pictured somehow, even knowing the address belonged to a kid — ten years old going on some much older, wearier version of himself, eyes red-rimmed and wary, a blanket wrapped around his shoulders like armor despite the warm evening. He looked at both of them for a long moment, cataloguing, deciding something. "I called 911 like eleven times," he said, like it needed defending. "The voice kept telling me help was coming. It never came."

"It sent us instead," John said. "Just took us longer than either of us wanted."

Tyler's eyes went past them, down the hall, toward the living room, and something closed over his face that was much too old for ten. "I already know about Uncle Carl," he said, flat, like he'd practiced saying it that way so it wouldn't come out any other way. "I found him. That's why I locked the door. I didn't know what else — " He stopped. Started over, smaller. "I didn't want to see him again."

"You don't have to," Sam said, immediate and certain. "You never have to again, if you don't want to. That's not your job anymore. That's ours."

Nobody had eaten a real meal since John couldn't remember, and it was Sam who noticed the boy swaying slightly on his feet and turned the whole evening toward the single most practical thing left to fix. They found a Waffle House four blocks over, sign still lit from whatever patch of grid was keeping this stretch of the county alive, and the parking lot said enough before they'd even gone inside — two cars pulled into employee spots around back, both occupied, both still, the unmistakable shape of two people who'd stepped out for a break sometime after two in the morning and never stepped back in. Neither John nor Sam mentioned it to Tyler. The restaurant itself, when they went in through the propped front door, was exactly as its staff had left it mid-shift — register open, a rag draped over the counter edge, the grill cold but otherwise untouched, nobody at all inside.

"Sit," Sam told Tyler, steering him into a booth with the easy authority of a man who'd apparently decided, somewhere over the last five minutes, that this was now also his job. John was already rounding the counter without a word, tying on an apron off a hook by reflex forty years old before he'd fully decided to. "What sounds good?" he called over, sliding a spatula down off its hook. "I can make anything on this menu, and about half of what isn't." Tyler looked at him like the question itself was strange, like nobody had asked him what he wanted in two days. "Waffle," he said finally. "And hash browns. Scattered." John had the grill lit and working under him inside a minute — he hadn't touched a flat-top since he was twenty-two, paying his way through Georgia Tech one overnight shift at a time, and his hands remembered it anyway.

Sam slid into the booth across from Tyler, close enough that his voice carried easily over the hiss of the grill, and started with the kind of question that didn't ask for anything hard back. "You do all that on your own? The last two days?"

Tyler nodded, working a loose thread on the edge of his blanket instead of looking up. "Nobody came," he said. "I kept thinking somebody would."

"Somebody did," Sam said. He let that sit a moment before he tried again, gentler. "That was your uncle, back there. Carl, you said at the door. Was that your dad's brother, or your mom's?"

Tyler picked at the syrup ring on the table instead of answering right away. "My dad died when I was four. Car accident. I don't really remember him — it's always kind of just been me and Mom." He said it flat, an old fact worn smooth from handling, nothing like the raw new one two rooms back. "Carl's Mom's brother. He came to stay with us in March, when she launched."

John's spatula paused over the eggs, just for a second. "Launched," he repeated, careful, not pushing.

"She's not here because she's not *here*," Tyler said, something sharpening in his voice like he'd had to explain this before to people who never quite believed him. "She's an astronaut. NASA. She's been up on the space station since March. Carl was only supposed to stay till she got back down in the fall." He said the last part flat — the one solid thing in his whole week that hadn't come apart on him, until it did a little at the edges, saying it.

Sam went still, fork forgotten in his hand. John made himself keep working the eggs, because stopping outright felt like it would scare the boy more than any of the last two days already had. He understood, watching it land on Sam, what that word meant here — a specific woman, alive as far as either of them knew, sealed inside a few hundred tons of aluminum somewhere overhead, with no way of knowing whether the planet under her still had anyone breathing on it, and a ten-year-old boy down here who might be the only proof left that it did. "She's on the ISS," Sam said, like he needed to hear himself say it before he could believe it.

"Right now," Tyler said. "Two hundred and fifty miles up. I used to watch her go over at night. I still do."

"She know?" John asked, looking up from the grill now. "About any of this?"

"I don't know how she'd find out," Tyler said, and for the first time all night his voice wasn't quite steady. "I don't know if she can even see down here well enough to tell anything's different." He looked past John, straight at Sam, like he'd decided somewhere in the last thirty seconds that this was a man who might actually know. "Can they even land it? If they wanted to?"

Sam didn't answer right away, and John, watching him from behind the counter, understood why — Sam was doing the same kind of math John had done walking through half a dozen silent houses the last two days, except Sam's version had a woman alive at the far end of it, circling the planet every ninety minutes, watching the lights go out city by city with nobody to radio and ask why. "I don't know," Sam admitted finally, and didn't dress it up as anything better than it was. "But somebody built that thing to come home eventually. That much I know."

"We'll figure it out," John said, plating eggs, keeping his voice level on purpose. "Not tonight. But we will."

Tyler didn't ask the question underneath the question — whether anyone would ever actually get word to her, or her to him. John, watching him decide not to, understood that some things a kid holds onto for as long as he can stand to, because the moment before you ask is still a moment where the answer could be anything.

John came around the counter a few minutes later with a plate in each hand — hash browns scattered and smothered the way he used to plate them at three in the morning for truckers and drunks and, now and then, somebody who just needed a booth and someplace warm to sit a while, a waffle with the edges gone dark the way he'd always liked his own. "Maya used to order hers just like that," Sam said, nodding at the scattered hash browns as John set the plate down, something to say that wasn't the space station. Tyler didn't ask who Maya was, just nodded back like the name meant something to him anyway.

"Where'd you learn to do that?" Sam asked John, something almost startled in it, like he needed a second to come back from wherever Tyler's question had taken him.

"Told you. Worked the grill line through school." John didn't say the rest of it out loud — that every hand who'd worked the line with him thirty-some years back was probably gone now, same as everyone, and here he was doing it again for exactly one person instead of a full counter, a scared kid instead of a drunk trucker, and it still felt like the same thing underneath. The only kindness he'd ever really had to offer at three in the morning was a hot plate and somewhere to sit.

Tyler ate like someone who hadn't in a while, careful and then not careful at all, and the three of them let the rest of the meal go quiet, the good kind of quiet, nobody reaching for anything harder to say.

They drove back to the airport in the last of the light with Tyler asleep before they'd even cleared the parking lot, worn out in the specific, total way John imagined only a child who'd been alone with a locked door and his uncle's body two rooms away for two days could be worn out. Sam carried him from the car to the aircraft without waking him, laid him across the back row of seats with a jacket for a pillow, and came back up to the cockpit to find John sitting very still with both hands pressed flat against his own knees, not quite breathing right.

It came out of him all at once and without warning — grief that skipped past Elena and Maya, past the man on the couch four blocks back, and landed on a ten-year-old boy with a name plaque shaped like a rocket ship who'd sat alone behind a locked door for two days doing math on how long it would take before anyone came, and had gotten the answer *too long* before he'd gotten the answer *at all*. John hadn't cried in front of anyone yet. Not standing over a stranger in a car on his own road, not at Harry's bedside, not waiting in Sam's hallway while Sam found his wife and daughter. It was Tyler's blanket wrapped around his shoulders like armor that finally did it, some small, specific, unbearable detail that broke through everything the last two days had trained him to hold shut, and he put his face in his hands in the front seat of a stranger's airplane and let it happen.

Sam didn't say anything. He didn't try to fix it, didn't offer the kind of hollow reassurance neither of them had any real use for anymore. He just sat down in the other seat and stayed there, one hand resting briefly on John's shoulder and then just present beside him, the two of them keeping quiet watch over a sleeping kid while one of them finally, fully broke — and the other one didn't ask him to stop.

It didn't last long. It wasn't supposed to. When John finally sat back up, wiped his face with the heel of his hand, and let out a breath that shook only a little on the way out, Sam still hadn't said a word, and somehow that was exactly the right amount of company. They sat up front a while longer after that, not talking much, listening to a kid breathe in his sleep like it was the most reassuring sound either of them had heard all week.

They slept in shifts in the cabin, parked on a ramp gone fully dark once whatever patch of grid had lit up the Waffle House finally gave out somewhere around midnight, and woke to a sky doing the same colorless gray thing it had done every morning since Tuesday, a whole different life ago. Tyler was quiet over what breakfast they could scrounge from a vending machine that still, against every reasonable expectation, took a dead man's abandoned dollar bills. He didn't cry. He asked, once, very small, whether they were leaving Carl here, and John told him the truth — that they were, that there wasn't a better answer available to any of them today, and that it didn't mean anyone had stopped caring about what was left in that house. Tyler nodded like he'd expected exactly that answer and hadn't needed to hear it made prettier than it was.

The GA ramp had its own version of the same luck that had gotten them the rental car the night before — a low cinderblock FBO with the lights dead and the door unlocked, and behind the counter, on a pegboard still holding a dozen tagged sets of keys, one marked simply FUEL TRUCK in a dispatcher's block handwriting. Sam had it started and rolling out onto the ramp before John had finished checking the office for a phone that still worked, the engine catching on the second try like it had been serviced within the week, because it probably had. "Jet-A," Sam said, reading the placard on the truck's tank. "PC-12 drinks the same thing as everything else out here — whole reason Pilatus built it that way. You can gas it up anywhere a jet lands, not just wherever the avgas pump happens to still be working." He worked the hose and the ladder himself, climbing up onto the wing to feed fuel into the tanks the way he'd clearly done it a thousand times on airplanes that cost a great deal more to fill, while John chocked the wheels and watched the quantity gauges climb.

Then he came down, wiped his hands on a rag that had been hanging off the truck's mirror since God knew when, and nodded John and Tyler both toward the nose. "Preflight," he said. "Every time, no exceptions, doesn't matter how good yesterday's flight was." He looked at Tyler, still hanging back a polite distance with his hands in his jacket pockets like he wasn't sure he was allowed closer. "You too. Come look. This is the part nobody skips."

They walked it together, the three of them, Sam narrating it the way John guessed he'd narrated it to a hundred new first officers over twenty years, except slower now, plainer, pitched for a ten-year-old and a pilot with eleven hundred hours in a Skylane and none at all in anything with a turbine. Fuel caps seated and safetied. Sumps drained clear into a little cup Sam handed to Tyler to hold — "checking for water, see how it comes out clean, that's what we want" — the prop pulled through by hand while Sam explained why one nick in any of the five blades was a bigger problem than it looked like, the pitot tube uncovered and clear, the single stout gear leg checked for proper strut extension the way you'd check a shock absorber. At the tail Tyler asked why there was a rubber strip running along the leading edge that looked like it didn't belong. "Deice boot," Sam told him. "Inflates when we need it, breaks the ice off before it can build up and change how the wing flies. Your mother's outfit runs the same idea up there, just backwards — everything on that station's built to keep something *out*. Down here it's mostly built to get something *off*." Tyler didn't say anything back, but John caught him glancing up once, briefly, out of what already looked like habit, and Sam saw it too and let it go without remark.

By the time they'd worked all the way around to the tail and back, John had a checklist taking shape in his head he hadn't carried the morning before — not memorized yet, but there, one more piece of the same slow accumulation Sam had been feeding him a leg at a time since Peachtree DeKalb. "Good enough," Sam said finally, giving the last inspection panel a pat like he was checking it was still there. He held the door for Tyler, then for John, and climbed in last, the way John was starting to understand he always would.

"Phoenix next," Sam said. He glanced back once at Tyler, already half-asleep again against the window, then at John. "What are we going to do about Tyler and his mom?"
