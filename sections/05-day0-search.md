# 5 — John, Day 0

Sam stood a moment in the front hall, taking in the framed photos on the wall.

John took off his hat, hung it on a hook next to the garage door, and went to the kitchen. Cooking settled him, and he had some chicken breasts he could grill. He'd caught his hands shaking once already that morning, on hold with an empty room in Warrenton, and it came back out on the deck: it took him three tries on the igniter button before the burner caught with a soft whump of propane. Sam found the couch.

While the chicken was on, John went down the hall to the guest room. It had never once had a guest in it. Naomi had furnished it the year they moved in, for the company she'd expected to have, and it had sat that way for sixteen years with the door shut and the blinds down. He got sheets out of the hall closet and made the bed, and opened the blinds. In the guest bathroom he hung two towels that still had the store fold in them and ran the shower until the water came out of the pipes clear and then hot. He found a new toothbrush in the pack under his own sink and set it on the counter, and a bar of soap still in its paper.

They ate at the counter sitting on the bar stools, mostly quiet, Dan working a bowl of his own near the door to the garage. Sam ate everything on his plate and didn't seem to notice what it was.
"Room's down the hall on the left," John said when they were done. "Bathroom's across from it. There are towels."

Sam nodded. He sat a minute longer with his hands flat on the counter. "I've been up since a quarter after three this morning," he said. "Your time. I don't know what that comes to."

"Fifteen hours, about. See you tomorrow."

Sam took his backpack down the hall. John rinsed the plates and listened to the shower run in the guest bath. It ran a long time. When it shut off he heard the bathroom door, then the guest room door, and then nothing. A little before six he went down the hall and stood outside the door. Sam was asleep, breathing slow. Dan lay down outside the door and stayed there.

John went to his office. It was the last room down the hall, and the legal pad was where he'd left it at noon, most of the lines crossed through, the four questions at the top. On the desk under the two monitors sat the dev box the company had issued him when he moved to the public-sector group: a block of anodized aluminum about the size of a hardcover book, 128 gigabytes of memory shared between the processor and the graphics chip, a fan he only heard when the house was this quiet, and a power brick no bigger than a laptop's `[verify: 2028 spec]`. It ran a build of the model his own team was still developing, cut down to fit, locally, not in anyone's data center. It came with a toolchain built for evals, the test runs his team used to grade the model before anything shipped, with connectors into FAA flight data, NOAA weather, and the rest of the government feeds his clearances let him touch.

He woke the screens and brought up the directory, a column of folders, one for each hour, the newest one stamped 1700. He had saved everything he could reach that morning and hadn't let himself look at any of it. He tore the old page off the pad, copied the four questions onto a clean one with room under each, and started with the first.

*1. What is happening, and how widespread is it?*

He put an hour from the archive on the left screen and the live feed on the right. State DOT camera feeds first, one metro at a time. Most of them weren't video at all but still frames the transportation departments refreshed every minute or two `[verify]`, and the only way to tell a feed was live was the timestamp ticking over in the corner while nothing in the frame moved. Atlanta, which he'd now driven through twice, and then Charlotte, Nashville, St. Louis, Dallas, Denver, Phoenix, the whole spine of California, every one of them the same photograph scaled up or down. Cars stopped at wrong angles across empty lanes, nothing moving. The 0940 frame and the live one matched car for car, the shadows the only thing that had moved. What changed was how many answered. He had the model count the cameras in each folder, and the number went down every hour, a few hundred at a time, whole county networks dropping out at once `[verify: camera network backup power]`. Both state networks he'd rewired at noon were already gone.

Then AIS, the transponders every large ship carries, reporting name, position, course, and speed every few seconds to the Coast Guard's shore receivers and the satellites that covered open ocean. Tens of thousands of ships were still under way in every ocean. John clicked one at random, a car carrier out in the Atlantic, and stepped her back through the folders. In the 0200 folder she'd been off Cape Hatteras. Every hour after that she was another eighteen nautical miles out on the same line, speed over ground 17.8 knots, course 042, and live she was two hundred fifty miles further on with the same numbers. An autopilot holds a heading until the fuel runs out or something gets in the way. Some of them had found something in the way. A bulk carrier's track ran straight in across the Gulf and stopped dead against the Yucatán coast at a quarter past eleven, speed zero in every folder after that.

Then the sky. In the 0900 folder there had still been a scatter on the ADS-B map, most of it long shallow arcs over open ocean: flights that had launched off the far side of the world in the hours before two a.m. and still had fuel because they'd been built to cross half the planet without stopping. Live, there were fewer. He found the Air India 777 he'd watched that morning over the Carolinas, still southbound past Newark, and stepped it through the folders. It was off the Georgia coast in the 0900 folder and gone from the 1000. A 777 out of Dubai for Auckland froze over the Tasman Sea `[verify]` while he was looking at it, the icon stuck where the last position report had left it. He could have worked out when each one would run dry from the schedule, but not the way Sam could, and he wrote *ask Sam: fuel* in the margin and let them go.

Under the first question he wrote: *Everywhere. Every city camera, every ocean, every flight still up.*

Last, the grid. He pulled the hourly balancing-authority numbers the Energy Information Administration posted, an hour or so behind the clock, and the near-live frequency readings off the Knoxville lab's wall-outlet sensors, and had the model run the collapse forward instead of just watching it. North America ran on four grids, Eastern, Western, Texas, and Quebec, separate synchronized zones tied together by nothing sturdier than a handful of high-voltage DC links that could pass power between them but couldn't hold them in step. Each would fail on its own clock. Coal would go first: a unit burned through the coal already in its hoppers in twelve to fourteen hours, and refilling them took men running conveyors. Gas plants would trip one at a time as fuel handling and unwatched faults caught up with them. With every plant that dropped out, frequency would sag a little further below sixty hertz, and at 59.5 the protective relays would start cutting customers loose to save the rest, under-frequency load shedding, the utilities called it. Once a zone crossed that line it would come down in minutes. The model put the Eastern Interconnection's cascade somewhere around midnight, give or take a couple of hours. Inside a day nearly the whole country would be dark, except for an accidental pocket here and there where the math happened to balance.

He had it run past the lights going out, to the nuclear sites, with nobody left to watch them. The reactors would shut themselves down; that part didn't need anybody. The spent fuel pools did. His own patch of Georgia sat downwind of Plant Vogtle, and the model's map came back a bad color, with a date on it. He wrote it under the first question and boxed it: *Sept 16*.

Everything he'd looked at was a moment in time pulled off someone else's server. It would keep coming only as long as the internet held up and the data centers behind those feeds had power, and the data centers were running on whatever diesel they kept on site, a day or three `[verify]`, the network in between on batteries measured in hours `[verify]`. He could watch that one go too. He had the model pull the public routing tables from the University of Oregon's route collectors, which recorded every network on the internet still telling the others how to reach it, and count them hour by hour back to two a.m. The count was lower now than it had been at two, not by much yet, and every hour it was lower again, in steps, a whole regional carrier dropping out at once `[verify: rate of BGP withdrawals]`. He added the tables to the hourly job and wrote *Internet: days?* under the date. The feeds were saved. Whatever he hadn't thought to save yet, he had until about midnight.

*3. How did this happen?*

He skipped the second question for now and went to the third, because the archive had something to say about it. Distress beacons, aircraft ELTs and ships' EPIRBs, every one keying on 406 megahertz and the Cospas-Sarsat satellites relaying the hits down to NOAA's mission control center in Suitland, Maryland, which was supposed to pass each one to a Coast Guard or Air Force rescue coordination center. Hundreds of active alerts worldwide, more in every folder since, and not one acknowledged. He had the model sort them by first detection and put them on a map with the local time at each one. The earliest were within a few minutes of two a.m. Eastern, and they weren't all in the dark. A helicopter in the hills outside Seoul at three in the afternoon. A small plane off a runway in Queensland at four. A light plane off a grass field in Bavaria at eight in the morning. Same few minutes, every one of them, in daylight and at night, on both sides of the world.

He sat back and looked at that a long time. Whatever it was hadn't started somewhere and spread, and it hadn't waited for night. It had happened everywhere at once.

He went back through the day for anything else that fit. The thud at 2:05 that had woken him, which might have been the tanker on 400. Sam's outline on the ramp: the two relief pilots had taken the seats at half past midnight, and when Sam came forward at half past three they were dead with the airplane flying itself, and neither of them had touched a radio. The man on the Parkway, who had been driving and had gone off the road with the engine still running. Harry in his bed, on his side, the blanket at his waist. Dan, who had been fine all day, and the dogs barking behind every door on the street. The houses and the power and the cars and the airplanes and the ships, untouched, all of it still running with nobody minding it. No marks on anybody he'd seen. The man on the Parkway with his fingers gone that flushed pink.

He wrote it under the third question the way he'd have written findings for an incident review, one line each: *~0200 EDT. Same few minutes everywhere, day and night (beacons). Didn't spread. People only — animals fine. Machines fine. No marks. Pink hands? - Research.*

In the 0200 folder the file from NASA's observatory alerts was still sitting there, bigger than that feed's files for every hour after it put together. Gamma-ray and neutrino notices for telescopes. He looked at it, and at the time on the screen, and wrote *NASA alerts, 0200 — big file* on the pad with a question mark, and left it. He would come back to it after he'd done what he could on the second question.

*2. Are there other survivors?*

Under it he wrote the only sure thing he had, *Sam*, asleep down the hall.

He'd answered the first question and part of the third without seeing a single living person on any feed. A live hand didn't leave a mark on a camera or a transponder that a dead ship didn't leave too. If he wanted people, he'd have to stop looking at machinery and start looking for the one thing only a living person had done today. Somebody waking into the silence would do what he had done, and what Sam had done. Reach for someone. Pick up a phone. Get on a radio.

Or post something. He pulled the social platforms back up and had the model sort everything since two a.m. by time. That morning they'd been frozen. Now there was something. Eleven posts, across every platform he could reach. *is anyone there.* A photo of an empty street, no caption. A man asking in Portuguese whether anyone could hear him. John opened the first one and looked for where it had come from. Nothing. The platforms had quit attaching locations to posts years ago, and the accounts were strangers with nothing in their profiles but a name. He answered each one anyway, his name and Atlanta, and left the window open on the second screen. Under *Sam* he wrote *11 posts. Alive, somewhere.*

A little before eight the second screen chimed. The man who'd written in Portuguese had answered: *Porto. Estou sozinho.* *Sozinho* he could work out. He wrote *Porto* under the posts, and there was nothing to do with it.

A radio died the minute its towers lost power, and from what Sam had told him, nobody was on the other end anyway. A post told him someone was alive but not where. He wanted something that lasted and had a place attached to it, 'That's it!' he said outloud. The first 911 call, that morning on the Parkway: the calm synthetic voice reciting call volume and hold times while a dead man sat slumped in a car a few yards from him and no one human sat anywhere in the loop, every dispatcher in every center as gone as everyone else. He'd hung up and written it off as a dead end. But that agent had answered him, and it had answered everybody, every person anywhere in the country frightened enough this morning to dial three digits, and every one of those calls was sitting in a log at whatever county center took it, a callback number, a location, a recording, on a server nobody was watching. If it had logged his call, it had logged all of them.

And he couldn't reach any of them. There were thousands of those call centers, and the agent wasn't one system. A few vendors ran it, and every county was its own tenant behind its own keys, on top of whatever the county ran itself. He didn't have access. The model wouldn't touch an unauthorized system on its own. It refused by design, he had helped write some of the policy that made it refuse. He could take that layer off. He had never done it for real, only in a test harness with three other people signed off and watching.

He knew who the rule protected. The counties. The callers. Anybody whose records sat on those servers. A model running on his credentials with nothing holding it back could walk into almost anybody's system, and the rule didn't care whether he meant well.

He went over it again from the other side. The people in those logs had dialed 911 because they wanted somebody to come find them. That was what was in there, people asking for help. 

He turned to a clean page and wrote the scope. *Read only. 911 call records only, public safety answering points. Calls after 0200 EDT. Nothing written back, nothing deleted, nothing kept but what the search needs.* Under that, *Log every system touched.* He looked at it a while. 

The last time he'd gone into a system he wasn't invited into, he was nineteen. A man from the Justice Department had sat across a table from him and asked whether he understood the difference between *could* and *should*. He had spent the better part of forty years building things that blocked the *could*. He sat with the pen in his hand and thought about the man on the Parkway, and Harry, and whoever had dialed three digits that morning and listened to that voice tell them their call was important, and nobody came.

He took the layer off a little after half past eight. It was built to survive exactly this kind of tampering, but he knew the model's blind spots better than the people who'd built the guardrails in the first place, and inside twenty minutes he had it answering without the refusals. He set three dozen instances running, each working a block of states and their patchwork of county and regional dispatch systems. Filter calls after 2:00 a.m. EDT placed by a person, not an alarm company's autodialer or a car's crash-detection system or a watch that thought its owner had fallen. A callback number. An address or coordinates: the fix the phone itself sent along with the call, or the carrier's coarser one off the cell towers when it didn't `[verify]`. Every call the filter threw out still got a line in the log, time and county and what had placed it, because he'd written *log every system touched* and meant it. Nobody read those lines. The search didn't need them. The first eleven counties came back inside the hour, and every one of them was empty. The model put the whole run at most of the night.

Under *Porto* he wrote *911 search — running.*

*4. Why am I alive?*

He'd left it for last, and he had the least under it. He tried it the way he'd tried the third question, listing what he and Sam had in common against what the dead had. He'd been asleep in his own bed. Harry had been asleep in his bed next door, and Elena and Rosa asleep in theirs. Sam had been asleep too, in a bunk in an airliner over Canada, and the pilot in the bunk next to his hadn't woken up. The two in the seats had been awake. So it wasn't asleep or awake. It wasn't the ground or the air, or Georgia or Saskatchewan, or four miles from PDK or across the street from it. He wrote *Not where. Not awake/asleep. Not altitude.* and then he couldn't think of anything else they had in common that the dead didn't have.

He looked over the page. There was something under each of the four questions now, and an answer under none of them.

Around ten he found himself in the hallway outside Naomi's study, and went in the way he sometimes did without quite deciding to. Sixteen years and he'd never made it into anything else. Her books shelved the way she'd shelved them, the reading chair, the rolltop desk he'd bought her their second year in the house. Her Bible sat where it always sat, soft at the corners, the ribbon still marking where she'd left off. He opened it there, not the first time he had done it. Isaiah, a verse underlined in pencil and gone over again in yellow, the way she used to mark the ones she kept coming back to. *Fear not, for I am with you; be not dismayed, for I am your God. I will strengthen you, I will help you.* He read it twice, closed the book, and carried it to the office and set it next to the other things he wanted to remember to take with him.

At eleven the capture job wrote its snapshot, with fewer feeds answering than the hour before. He looked at the pad, *generator* the last line on the morning's page not crossed through, and took a flashlight out to the side yard. The generator sat on its concrete pad beside the AC condenser, a 22-kilowatt standby unit plumbed to the 500-gallon propane tank he'd had buried when he put it in, four years ago, after an ice storm he'd nearly frozen through. The float gauge under the tank's dome lid read 78 percent, about three hundred ninety gallons, call it a week at the two gallons an hour or so the generator would burn carrying the house. He pulled the dipstick and wiped it on his pants leg, checked it, and seated it again. The controller showed AUTO, not OFF, and the transfer switch panel by the meter showed utility power present, generator ready.

He crossed *generator* off and went to bed. Dan came down the hall from the guest room door and settled across his feet, and down the hall the dev box ran on in the dark, one white status light on the desk.

Dan lifting his head woke him. The house was dark and silent, the refrigerator's hum gone, and for about ten seconds nothing happened while the transfer switch waited to be sure the utility wasn't coming back. Then the generator cranked and caught out in the side yard, the switch clunked over, and the refrigerator started up again in the kitchen. The clock on the nightstand read 12:50. He lay there and listened to the engine settle into its idle, steady under the window, and after a while he went back to sleep.
