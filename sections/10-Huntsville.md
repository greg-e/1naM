# Huntsville

**06:05 local Pacific Time, Sunday**

Sam walked around 2PR on the ramp with a flashlight, the sky over the Cascades going gray. He sampled the drains and pulled the tie-downs and the chocks. The airplane had carried the Jackery everywhere since PDK, and with that out of it and only two people going, the weight and balance on his iPad came out with more room than he had seen in it yet. Catherine had loaded the cabin the night before: the medical bag, a case of saline, two oxygen cylinders, the Life Flight stretcher, a box of N95s, and a box of body bags she had put in the back without saying anything about it.

John came down the brow with Dan on the leash and a cooler in his other hand, and Catherine came behind him with her duffel, still talking. "Every four hours, and I want the number written down, not remembered. She'll tell you she's done ten on the cylinder. Watch her do it."

"I read it back twice," John said.

"I know you did." She went down in that careful way of hers and put both hands in Dan's coat, then got up and climbed the airstair. Dan tried to go up after her and John took him by the collar.

John held out the cooler. "Chicken, and some of that bacon. Stay upwind of Browns Ferry."

"Tomorrow before dark," Sam said, and they shook hands.

Catherine had never sat up front. Sam put her in the right seat, got her belts on and her headset plugged in, and pointed at the guarded button on the overhead. "If something happens to me, you lift that cover and push the button. It picks a runway, flies there, lands, and shuts down the engine. It talks to you the whole way. All you do is keep your hands off everything."

She looked up at it. She reached up, lifted the cover with one finger, looked at the button, and closed it again. "All right."

The wind was calm. He taxied out past the hangar to the west end, past the place where he'd kicked the clamshells off, and took off on 8 at 06:35 with the sun not quite up. The ship went by on the right with her lights on. He held the climb straight out over the water, then came around to the southeast with Whidbey Island under the wing.

"Two Papa Romeo, John. Radio check."

"Loud and clear, John."

He called again at Everett and it was mostly static. Sam told him they were passing eleven thousand and didn't hear an answer.

The Cascades went by with the sun coming up behind them and snow on Glacier Peak. He leveled at 28,000 feet, set the power, and had a ground speed of 290 with the wind behind him. On the half hour he keyed 121.5. "Any station, Pilatus Six One Two Papa Romeo, transmitting in the blind, three zero miles south of Spokane, flight level two eight zero, eastbound." He gave it a minute and tried Seattle Center, then went back to watching the engine page.

"Does anybody ever answer that?" Catherine asked.

"John did."

Behind them eastern Washington had been brown, with the green irrigation circles in rows. Now the mountains started, and over the Bitterroots a column of smoke stood up out of a ridge in Idaho and leaned east, the top of it spread out flat a few thousand feet under them. Farther on he could see two more. Nobody was going to those. She watched the first one until it went behind the wing.

Over Montana she shifted in the seat, and a little later she did it again. She took a bottle out of her shirt pocket, shook one into her hand, and took it with water. "Volleyball," she said. "Twenty-one years ago."

"Four hours does it to me too."

The Yellowstone River came and went with Billings on it, no smoke from the refineries. She unpacked John's chicken and handed him a piece on a napkin.

"Tell me about Rosa," she said.

He told her. Twelve, and she swam for a club in Dunwoody and had just decided she hated it, and Elena had told her she could quit in the spring and not before. She read in the car. She'd started correcting his Spanish, which she had from school and he had from his grandmother's kitchen. Elena ran the house and most of the rest of it around his trips. He could hear what his voice was doing while he said all this. It was level and pleasant and it was the voice he used to tell three hundred people about the weather in Seoul.

"She texted me good night," he said. "I got it in Edmonton."

Catherine put her hand on his arm and left it there a little while. She didn't say anything, and then she took it back and looked out the side window at Wyoming.

**12:40 local Central Time**

He started down over central Nebraska with the Platte off to the right. At 36,000 feet in the A350 the feedlots had always been brown squares. At 6,000 they were pens, a lot of them, row on row along a county road, and the cattle in them were packed against one side of each pen where the long steel water tanks ran. A good many were down. Birds were working over the far rows.

"What's wrong with them?" Catherine asked.

"The waterers are on an electric pump, I'd guess."

She kept looking back until the wing took it away.

South of Omaha he could see Offutt, the long runway running down to the Missouri River. He'd brought a C-17 in there twice. There was a line of gray RC-135s on the ramp, and off by itself the big white 747 that was supposed to be ready to fly at any minute of any day, with a crew sleeping next door to it. It was still parked where it had been parked. He called Offutt Tower anyway, and went back to the airport frequency.

"Omaha traffic, Pilatus Six One Two Papa Romeo, low pass runway 14 Right." He flew it at two hundred feet. No wreckage on it, no deer, a Southwest 737 at a gate and a line of FedEx feeders. The river ran along the east side of the field. He came around and put it down at 13:05, four and a half hours off the Hook, and taxied to Atlantic's ramp with about 780 pounds left in the tanks.

The doors were unlocked and nobody was inside. A fuel truck sat by the line shack with its keys on the visor. He backed it up to the wing, and when Catherine started dragging the hose he saw how she was doing it and took it away from her. "This is John's job, and you get it now. Clip that cable to the airframe first, always first. Then stand here and read me that meter."

She read it to him, two hundred and ninety gallons in all. It was in the eighties with a south wind that had the smell of the river in it and, under that, the feedlots. They ate the rest of the chicken in the shade of the wing, with the airplane ticking as it cooled.

"Sixteen calls," she said. "They'd have made them all Tuesday, and I don't think the towers lasted into Wednesday. So what have they had since then?"

"I don't know. What's the number, for somebody hurt?"

"Without water? In Alabama? If they're hurt, three days. If they're not hurt, and they're inside, and they've got any sense, maybe a week." She folded the napkin and put it in the cooler. "It's been five."

They were rolling at 13:50.

**15:30 local Central Time**

Missouri went by under a layer of haze, then the Mississippi down at the bootheel, brown and wide with a line of barges swung sideways against the bank. They crossed the Tennessee River the first time in west Tennessee and picked it up again in Alabama, coming in ahead of them as a long lake. Sam brought it down to 5,000 feet.

The first thing he saw was the stack, a thin concrete line standing up six hundred feet off the north shore of the lake. Then he could see the plant under it, three reactor buildings side by side like big gray boxes, and a row of low fan towers with nothing coming out of them. He flew by it two miles to the north, because John would ask.

"Is that it?" Catherine asked.

"That's it. John's window opens on the sixteenth." South across the lake, at Decatur, a plume of black smoke came off something on the waterfront and lay out flat to the east, toward where they were going. He pointed at it and she looked and didn't say anything.

John had put the address on his iPad as a pin. It was a few miles northeast of the airport, off County Line Road, a subdivision of two-story brick houses on curving streets with new trees. Sam came over it at 800 feet with the power all the way up and the landing lights on. Trampolines, a pool with green water, cars in all the driveways. He came around and did it again, lower, right down the street with the pin on it, and a dog in a back yard looked up at him. Nobody came out on a lawn. He made the turn wide to the east for the third pass, and past the end of the turn, miles off over the trees, a thin white shape stood straight up by the interstate.

"Is that a rocket?" Catherine asked.

"Saturn V. It's at the museum."

He made the third pass and took it to the airport. Huntsville had two long parallel runways, both clear. He flew down 18 Left at two hundred feet, came around, and landed at 16:05.

Signature's door was locked. He opened it with the pry bar from Epps. Nobody had been in the place since it closed on Labor Day. Fuel could wait until they knew what they had. Behind the counter was a drawer of rental agreements with key fobs clipped to them, and one of those was a white Tahoe in the lot. They moved the medical bag, the saline, and the oxygen into it, and he drove out the airport road with Catherine holding the iPad.

I-565 had one tractor-trailer stopped across the right lane with its door shut, and not much else. It had happened at one in the morning here, with nobody on the road. He got off at County Line Road and went north past a shopping center and a church. When he put his window down he could smell it, even out here, the whole neighborhood the way the hall at the hospital had smelled on Saturday. On the walk in Alpharetta on Wednesday there had been dogs behind half the doors. Here he heard one a long way off, and that was all.

**16:45 local Central Time**

The house was brick, with a basketball hoop over the garage and a gray Altima in the drive. Sam parked behind it. He and Catherine put on their masks and went up the walk. The doorbell did nothing, so he knocked on the storm door and called out, "Hello? Anybody home?" and tried the handle. It was locked.

Catherine had stepped back onto the grass and was looking up. "Sam."

There were two windows over the garage. In the one on the left the blinds had a gap in them at about the height of a kitchen counter, with two slats bent down. As he looked the slats went back up.

"Was that—" he said.

"That was a child," she said.

He stood there on the walk. Then he went around the side of the house, fast, through a gate into the back yard, with Catherine coming behind him with the bag. There was a patio with a grill on it and a sliding glass door, and it wasn't locked.

The smell came through the mask. He'd been smelling it in one place or another for five days and it was much worse with the house shut up in this heat. He breathed through his mouth and went in, and the first room was the kitchen. A chair had been pulled over to the pantry and left there. The pantry door stood open, with gaps along the shelves where things had been taken from the front of them. Through the wide doorway a man lay on the couch in the living room in basketball shorts, with a blanket slid half off him and the remote on the floor by his hand. Catherine went past Sam and stood over him. She looked for a while, and she looked at the stairs, which came down at the far end of the same room, about ten feet from the end of the couch.

"Go on," she said. "I've got him."

He went up. It wasn't as bad at the top. The hall bathroom door stood open on his way past. The tub had an inch of water in the bottom of it, with a coffee mug sitting on the rim. The lid of the toilet tank was off and laid on a folded towel on the floor, and the tank was dry. Somebody had thought about this.

The door at the end of the hall was shut. There was an old rocket sticker on it, peeling at one corner, and a line of dark towel showing along the bottom. He knocked with one knuckle.

"My name's Sam," he said. "I'm a pilot. That was my airplane going over." It came out in the voice from the cockpit, the one from the PA, and for once he was glad to have it. "There's two of us. My friend downstairs is a doctor."

Nothing. A floorboard shifted on the other side of the door.

"You called 911," he said.

It was quiet long enough that he took his hand off the door. He pulled his mask down under his chin, and he sat down on the carpet with his back against the wall across from it, so whoever it was wouldn't open that door on a man standing up.

"Are you the unit?" The voice was very small and hoarse and it came from low down, near the floor.

"The what?"

"The lady said a unit would be dispatched."

Sam looked at the ceiling. "Yes," he said. "That's us. It took us a while. I'm sorry."

"She said as priority allows."

"I know she did."

Downstairs a door opened and closed, and he heard Catherine cross the room. After a while the voice said, "Did you see my uncle?"

"I saw him. The doctor's with him now."

"He's..." It stopped.

Sam waited to see if there was more. There wasn't. "He's dead," he said. "I'm very sorry. What's his name?"

"Uncle Carl."

"What's yours?"

"Tyler."

"How old are you, Tyler?"

"Ten."

He stayed where he was. There was a push-button lock in that knob that he could have opened with a nail. He looked at the wall beside the door, at a framed school picture of a kid with a big toothy smile, and he kept his hands on his knees. A towel dragged across the floor, and the button in the knob popped.

The door came open about a foot. The boy was small and thin, Black, with short curls gone flat on one side, in a NASA T-shirt gone soft and pale from washing, with his lips cracked and his eyes very big in his face. He looked at Sam, and at the mask under his chin, and down at his watch, and back up. The air from the room behind him was hot and came from an open window, and it was better than the hall.

"I thought there weren't any more planes," Tyler said. "I watched for them. Is it everybody that..." He looked down at the towel by his foot.

"Just about," Sam said. "There's five of us I know about, counting you. Can I come in?"

Tyler backed up. It was a boy's room with a bunk over a desk and the walls covered, the ISS in a big poster, a shuttle stack on a launch pad, a chart of the constellations. A pillowcase lay flat and empty by the bed. On the desk everything had been lined up. A jar of peanut butter, scraped most of the way down, with a spoon standing in it. Half a box of granola bars. A sleeve of crackers. Along the windowsill stood nine water bottles, sports bottles and plastic ones and a thermos, each with a number on a piece of masking tape, six of them empty. A sheet of notebook paper was taped to the wall above them, ruled in columns in pencil: the date, liters, bars, crackers, and the last line of it said *Sun 1.2 L left, 0.5/day.*

Sam stood and read that. He'd flown with first officers who kept worse logs. "Who taught you that?"

"My mom. You count it first, before you're thirsty. Then you know how long you have." He said it the way you'd say the Pledge of Allegiance. "I filled them up Tuesday. Then it stopped Thursday in the morning and I got the rest from the tub, and the tank on the back of the toilet, not the bowl. I figured it was okay if the water was clear and there wasn't cleaner in it."

"Where's your mom, Tyler?"

Tyler pointed at the desk. A picture stood at the end of the row, a small woman with short twists in a blue flight suit, with a flag behind her and the same grin. It took Sam a moment. Then he looked at the poster of the station, and back at her.

"She's up there," Tyler said. "Since March."

In five days John had counted ninety-five hundred airplanes and eleven thousand ships. He had the grid by the hour and every reactor in two hundred miles. Sam had flown under that thing for twenty years, and neither of them had said one word about it.

Tyler had gone to the desk. He had a spiral notebook there and he opened it and held it out, a column of times in the same careful pencil, a lot of them checked off. "I copied them off the app, before my phone died. It still goes over. I saw it Thursday and I saw it Friday, right on time, and there's one tonight at 7:41. So it's still up there, so that means..." He looked at the page. "Do you think they..."

Sam had the voice ready, and he looked at that kid and didn't use it. "I don't know, Tyler. I didn't even think of it until just now. But I'd like to see it. You show me where to look at 7:41."

Tyler nodded and closed the notebook and held on to it.

He heard Catherine on the stairs, and she came down the hall with the bag and her mask already off. She stopped in the door and looked at the boy, and her whole face changed. "Hi, you," she said. "I'm Catherine." She got down in front of him with her back straight, took his wrist in two fingers, and looked at his fingernails while she counted. "Any trouble breathing?"

"No."

"Headache?"

"A little."

She pinched up the skin on the back of his hand and watched it go down, looked at his tongue, and took a bottle out of the bag and cracked the cap. "This tastes like a salty lemon. Small sips, and I'm going to stand here and watch you do it. You don't need to save any of that now." While he drank she looked over his head at the wall of columns, and then at Sam, and he saw her read it. "You're dehydrated, Tyler," she said. "You're going to be fine."

Tyler took the notebook, the picture, and a phone that wouldn't turn on, and Catherine put them in his school backpack with some clothes from the drawers. At the top of the stairs she bent down to him. "I put a bedspread over your uncle. It was in the closet in the big bedroom. You can walk by with your eyes shut, and Sam'll steer."

He went down the stairs with his eyes open, one hand on the rail and the other holding the back of Sam's shirt. At the bottom he stopped. It was a blue and white spread and she had tucked it in along the back of the couch.

"That's my mom's," Tyler said.

"I'm sorry," Catherine said. "It's what I could find."

"No, it's okay. She'd..." He stood looking at it. Then he let go of Sam's shirt and walked through the kitchen by himself, around the chair, and out the sliding door.

He got as far as the edge of the patio. He stood in the sun with his backpack on, breathing, looking at the fence and the grill and the hoop on the garage, and his knees started to go, not all at once. Sam got to him before he sat down. He slid one arm under the kid's legs and the other behind his back and picked him up, and Tyler didn't argue about it. He didn't weigh anything. He weighed about what Rosa had the year she was ten. Sam carried him around the side of the house and through the gate with the boy's head against his collarbone, and got the back door of the Tahoe open with two fingers, and set him on the seat, and pulled the belt across him and clicked it without thinking about it.

He stood there with his hand on the roof. Catherine put the bag in on the other side and got in next to Tyler with the bottle. It was 17:50 on the dash when he got in and started it, a little more than an hour of daylight. He looked at the mirror. Tyler was looking at him out of that thin face.

"My grandma lives across town," Tyler said. "By A&M. She's by herself. I know the way, I just didn't..." He stopped.

Catherine looked at Sam in the mirror, and shook her head.

Sam put the Tahoe in reverse. "Tell me where to turn," he said.

**18:20 local Central Time**

Tyler called every turn from the back seat, in that hoarse voice, a block ahead of each one. His grandmother lived in Normal, a few streets from the Alabama A&M campus, in a small brick house with a carport and an old Buick under it. Sam stopped at the curb and shut off the engine, and nobody got out.

"The key's under the frog," Tyler said. "By the back step."

"You want me to go look?"

Tyler nodded.

The frog was concrete. Sam put his mask on at the back step and let himself into the kitchen. It was hot and very clean, a dish towel folded over the oven handle, and on the refrigerator were the school picture from the hall and a crew photograph from NASA with one face circled in ballpoint. She was in the back bedroom, in bed, on her side, with her glasses folded on the Bible on the nightstand. He stayed long enough to be sure of what he was going to say, and he drew the sheet up over her. He moved her glasses onto the nightstand and took the Bible, a heavy old one with papers sticking out of it and a rubber band around it. On his way out he opened the pantry. There were soup and crackers and a whole shelf of quart jars of peaches with the year written on the lids, and he filled two grocery sacks. He locked the door and put the key back under the frog before he thought about it.

He took the mask off before he got to the curb, opened Tyler's door, and went down on one knee on the pavement. "She's in her bed, Tyler. It looks like she was asleep. She's gone. I'm so sorry."

Tyler looked at the sacks. "Those are her peaches," he said. "She does them in July." Sam held out the Bible. "This was by her bed." Tyler took it in both hands and held it on his knees on top of the backpack. Catherine put her arm around him and he let her.

Nobody spoke until they were back on the highway. "What was her name?" Catherine asked.

"Grandma." He thought about it. "At church it's Miss Odessa."

**19:05 local Central Time**

The RV dealer was on the highway a few miles from the airport, a long lot of motorhomes in rows with little flags on a wire over them and the sun going down behind the service building. Sam wanted to be close to the airplane. The sales office was locked, so he put the pry bar through the glass and went in with his headlamp. A steel key cabinet hung on the wall behind the finance desk, with the tags on the hooks by stock number, and it took him a while to match them up. He came out with the keys to two Class A's parked side by side at the end of the second row, a thirty-seven foot Tiffin and a Newmar about the same.

The door of the Tiffin came open on a smell of new carpet and plastic. It was the first air he'd had all day that was only air, and Tyler went past him up the steps and stood in the middle of the coach with his backpack on, breathing it. The dealer had left a quarter tank in each one. Sam found the generator switch on the dash, the Onan caught back in its compartment, and in a few minutes the roof units were pushing cold air down the length of the coach.

"I'll take the other one," Catherine said. "I'll look in on him at midnight." She opened a jar of peaches at the dinette, heated a can of chicken soup on the propane stove, and stood over Tyler again and made him go slow.

At 7:30 he came and found Sam with the notebook. "We have to go outside."

They walked out past the end of the row to where the lot opened up, with the generators running behind them, and Tyler stood with his back to the highway and looked at the page with Sam's headlamp. It was almost dark. There was no glow anywhere, not from Huntsville or Decatur or the airport. The overcast had come in from the west since sundown, high and solid from one side of the sky to the other, without a star in it.

"It might break," Tyler said. He pointed a little to the right of a water tower. "It comes up over there. Northwest. It goes almost straight over and it goes out southeast. 7:41 and about ten seconds."

Sam looked at his watch. "How far up is it?"

"Two hundred and fifty miles. About."

"How many are up there?"

"Seven. Four on my mom's Dragon and three on the Soyuz."

Sam's watch said 7:41. They looked at the place over the water tower, and it was as dark as the rest of it. "Now," Tyler said anyway, and he put his arm up and pointed at the cloud, and he moved his arm slowly up and over with his head going back, his other hand holding on to Catherine's blanket. Sam found he was looking where the arm pointed. He'd seen it a few times from the flight deck over the years, said "There's the station," and gone back to what he was doing.

When the arm was almost straight up Tyler said, "She can't see us either. It's too dark down here. It's not like there's..." He didn't finish that.

"How do they get home?" Catherine asked him.

"The Dragon lands in the ocean with parachutes. Then there's a ship that goes out and gets them." He kept his arm moving. "It's got a crane."

Sam looked over at Catherine and she looked at him.

The arm came down toward the trees to the southeast and stopped, still well up in the sky. "That's the shadow," Tyler said. "It's still going, you just can't see it." He held his hand out for the headlamp, opened the notebook against his chest, and next to 7:41 he wrote *clouds*.

Sam put him in the back bedroom, on a bare mattress with Catherine's blanket and the air conditioning running, and took the sofa up front himself. He didn't expect to sleep and he didn't. Sometime after two he looked out through the big windshield and there was lightning along the bottom of the sky to the northwest, flickering up inside the overcast, too far off to hear.

"Are you there?" It came from the back of the coach, down the dark hall.

"I'm here."

"Where do we go tomorrow?"

"Washington State. There's a Coast Guard ship on a pier, with a man named John, and a lady with a broken leg. And a dog."

It was quiet for a while. "What kind of dog?"

"A good one. His name's Dan."

There was no more from the back after that.

**05:50 local Central Time, Monday**

There was no one to call for a briefing. The weather on his iPad had stopped updating Wednesday morning and the datalink page in the airplane said NO DATA. So he stood on the Signature ramp in the gray with a cup of instant coffee from the break room and did it the way he'd learned in gliders. The overcast was still up there, thicker and lower off to the west. It was seventy-four degrees and sticky at that hour, and the wind on his wet finger was out of the south, a little stronger than it had been. That was a front to the northwest, a long way off yet and coming this way. He could sit here and let it go by, a day, maybe two, but he had told John Tuesday night, and Tuesday night John would stop expecting them.

He backed the fuel truck up to the wing. "This is the bonding cable," Catherine told Tyler, holding the clip out to him. "It goes on the airframe, before anything else. Always first." Tyler carried it over and clipped it where she pointed, and pulled on it to be sure. They took on a hundred and forty gallons, right up to 2,704 pounds. Sam walked around the airplane twice, and Catherine put Tyler in the forward cabin seat with the belts cinched down, a headset too big for him, and a stack of airsick bags from Signature's counter.

They were off 18 Left at 06:50, into the south wind, and Sam came around to the right to the northwest with the sun coming up somewhere behind the overcast. Coming through 10,000 feet he went looking for the radar. He had used one on every kind of weather there was for twenty years, but on the A350 it more or less ran itself, he had never touched this one, and John wasn't in the seat to tell him where Garmin had put it. It took a couple of minutes on the touchscreen to find the page and the tilt. He brought the range out to 160 miles.

"What's that?" Catherine asked.

The top of the display was painted from one side to the other, a band running from down over Arkansas up toward Illinois, green along the near edge, yellow inside that, and in the yellow, spots of solid red.

"Rain," Sam said. "Green's rain. Yellow's a lot of rain. Red you don't go in, and you stay about twenty miles away from it, that's where the hail and the worst of the air are."

Out the windshield he could see the tops now, more than a hundred miles off, a white wall going up past anything he could reach, with the anvils smeared out ahead of it. On a normal day he'd have called Memphis Center. They'd have told him where the airplane ahead of him had gone through and what it found, and given him a heading. He leveled off at 11,000 feet, and the outside air read plus five.

"I thought you said higher was better," Catherine said.

"It is. But it's freezing from about fourteen thousand up, and all of that is wet. Those rubber boots on the wings will handle ordinary ice in ordinary cloud. I don't know what they'll do in there. Down here if I get wet, it's just water." He worked the iPad on his knee. Down low the engine burned half again what it did at altitude, about six hundred pounds an hour against four. Three and a half hours to Omaha with the detours left him six or seven hundred pounds when he got there. If it went bad he had Jonesboro, Paducah, Cape Girardeau, and Springfield under him, all with long runways and fuel on the field, and he could sit on any of them until it went by.

**08:15 local Central Time**

The Mississippi went under them at New Madrid, brown under a low brown overcast, and the light had gone the color of dishwater. They were under the anvil now. He had the range down to 60 miles and kept tilting the antenna down until he picked up ground, then back up a hair. There were two big cells ahead, one north of Poplar Bluff and one west of it, and a gap between them about thirty miles wide with nothing but green in it. He took twenty degrees left to line up on it. Rain started on the windshield, a few streaks and then a steady hiss he could hear through the headset.

"Tyler," he said on the intercom, "it's going to get bumpy for a little while. The airplane likes it fine. Keep your belts tight."

"Okay."

He slowed down to 165 knots and turned the panel lights up all the way, so a close flash wouldn't blind him. The first good jolt came a minute later and threw his head against the side of the headset, and the cooler slid across the cabin floor. Then it was steady, hard chop with the wings working and the altitude wandering, 11,200, 10,900. He left the autopilot on with his hand near the yoke. He was looking down at the radar every few seconds now. The green in the middle of the gap had a yellow spot on it that hadn't been there two sweeps ago, and it was already bigger. Behind the cell on the left the screen had a black wedge with nothing painted in it at all, and he knew better than to believe that. The rain was soaking up the radar there, and it told him nothing about the other side.

"That wasn't there," Catherine said. She had one hand flat on the glareshield.

"No."

The next sweep had yellow most of the way across the gap, with red coming up inside it. Off to the north the big cell had a soft edge on it, and past that was a long stretch of green he'd passed up a few minutes ago. He turned the heading bug ninety degrees right. The airplane rolled into it, and a flash went off behind the left wing, a hard white light through the whole cockpit, with no sound he could hear over the engine and the rain. He heard Tyler being sick on the intercom, then the click of the microphone.

"How're you doing back there, Tyler?"

Some breathing. "I threw up."

"So did I, my first time in one of these. Use another bag."

It was twelve minutes on that heading, up along the edge of the big one with the red staying twenty, twenty-five miles off the left wing and the airplane dropping and catching itself. He turned back northwest as soon as he had the room. The rain got heavier, then lighter, and the chop slowed down into long swells. The screen went to green, then to speckled green, then there was a gray place ahead of him in the overcast and a brighter one beyond it. They came out from under the back of it over Missouri a little before nine, into plain morning sun, with broken cloud below them and the whole wall standing up in the right-side windows from one end of the sky to the other.

He took his hand away from the yoke and it was stiff. He ran the numbers again: 1,400 pounds and about three hundred and thirty miles to go. He pushed the power up and went on up through 14,000 in clear air, and the boots stayed black and dry.

"Did you know it was going to close like that?" Catherine asked.

"No."

She unbuckled and went back to see to Tyler.

**10:25 local Central Time**

The front had gone through Omaha in the night. It was sixty-two degrees with a northwest wind and a washed-out blue sky, and he landed on 32 Left and taxied to Atlantic's ramp with 760 pounds in the tanks. He shut down and went around the airplane looking at the leading edges and the radome for hail, and there wasn't a mark on it.

When he came back around the nose Catherine was coming down the airstair with a bag tied shut. Tyler had come down ahead of her and stood on the ramp in his soft NASA shirt, a little gray, with his face wet where she'd washed it. He looked at the wing and at the fuel truck over by the line shack. "Is that one ours?" he asked. Sam said it was, and went to get it, and when he had it backed up to the wing Tyler was standing there with his hand out for the cable.
