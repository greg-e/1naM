# 8 — John, Day 2

Sam came into the restaurant a little after seven, the cup John had left by the couch in his hand, and stood a moment taking in the workstation cabled to the wall and the notebook open on the table and John still in the chair he'd been in all night.

"You didn't sleep," Sam said.

"I got some." John pushed the notebook across the table so it faced him. "I got this, is the point."

He gave it to him plain and complete, the way he'd promised himself he'd tell people things from here on. "Three leads. Huntsville, Phoenix, Portland. No names — I never got names, and I never got a single one of them on the line. Three addresses, three numbers that don't connect to anything anymore. What's under each one is a few lines of transcript from the night it happened. Somebody in Huntsville who argued with the triage machine for six minutes. Somebody in Phoenix who called back four times. Somebody in Portland who stayed calm enough to read out map coordinates. People who were alive and fighting, two nights ago. That's the whole of it."

Sam's face did something complicated at that — the look of a man who'd watched the lights go out region by region on his way south and was doing the math on how many more there'd be. "Dead like the phones, or dead like the people."

"I don't know. Nobody's answered since." John held his gaze. "Which is why the order's about routing, not about who can wait — I can't tell who can wait. Huntsville's barely off a straight line west of here. Then Phoenix. Portland last, because it's the far corner."

"Huntsville, then Phoenix, then Portland." Sam said it back like he was already loading it into a flight plan. "Yeah. That's the order I'd have picked too."

That settled, more or less instantly, the way John was starting to understand things got settled with Sam — not argued over, just checked against each other and confirmed.

"You fly?" Sam asked, a beat later, like it had only now occurred to him to ask.

"Private pilot. Instrument rated, current — I fly a 182 out of here most months, a lot of it in actual." John felt oddly exposed saying it, the way you'd confess a hobby nobody had asked about. "Nothing with a turbine. Nothing anywhere near this."

Something shifted in Sam's whole posture — visible relief in a man who hadn't looked capable of much a minute before. "You have any idea what that's worth right now? I've been the only pilot on earth for two days. You're not typed in a PC-12 and you're not going to be, but you can hold a heading and shoot an approach and not kill us both in a cloud, and that's the part that takes years. The airplane I can teach you. I'll check you out as we go — systems, numbers, let you fly the cruise under my eye. By the time we're through this you'll be worth having in the right seat for real, not just for company."

"I'd rather be useful than just be company," John said, and meant it more than he expected to.

Sam looked past him then, out across the ramp toward the airplane he'd flown in on. "That's the other thing. Come look at this with me."

They walked out into a bright Thursday morning. The PC-12 sat where Sam had left it the night before — a tail number off a fob on a pegboard in Halifax, a logbook he hadn't had time to read, three legs down the coast that had all gone fine. Sam looked at it the way you look at a stranger who's done you a favor. "It got me here, and I've got no complaint with it. But I took it off a dark ramp on faith, and I still don't know its history, and we're about to ask a lot more of whatever we fly than a milk run down the seaboard — long legs, hard country, and at the end of every one of them somebody hurt that we're putting in the back. I'd rather start that part in something I can actually read the logs on."

He was already walking, toward a low building on the far side of the field with a stylized mountain-and-crest logo over the door and PILATUS SALES & SERVICE stenciled beneath it. Behind the glass showroom wall, under lights that had clearly never gone off, sat a PC-12 that still had the faint chemical smell of new paint coming off it from twenty feet away — full ACE avionics suite lit on standby, not a scratch anywhere on the airframe, the kind of airplane that existed to be looked at by somebody with the money to actually order one.

"Brand new," Sam said, something almost reverent in it. "PRO model. Newer panel than the one I flew in on, and that one's current. Nobody's ever going to buy this now, and it's not doing anybody any good behind glass." He didn't wait for John to agree before he found the service door around the side, tried the handle out of habit, and moved to the small gray lockbox beside it when the door held. It took him under a minute with a multitool from his flight bag — no bypass, no dispatcher's pegboard to cross-reference, nothing like the fights either of them had already been through for far less — and the box popped open to a single key fob on its hook, tagged with the tail number in a dealer's careful sales-floor hand.

"That's it?" John said. "That easily?"

"Not everything gets to be hard," Sam said, and for the first time since John had watched him climb out of the other airplane, he sounded like he almost believed it. The fuel gauges, when he checked them from the cockpit a minute later, read full — a demo aircraft kept topped off and ready to impress a customer who was never coming, one more small mercy neither of them examined too closely.

They spent the day on it. Sam ran a full walkaround with the same unhurried thoroughness he'd have given a widebody, then took John through the panel three generations past the steam gauges in his 182 — systems, speeds, the numbers he'd need in the right seat. John slept two hours on the crew-room couch while Sam finished the preflight and moved the fuel truck. In the middle of the afternoon they broke off to load the workstation, which turned out to be a two-man lift and then some: a hundred-odd pounds of compute wrestled up through the cargo door and strapped down hard against the aft bulkhead, its power supply and the storm battery after it, the duffel with Naomi's Bible in it, the notebook. How they were going to keep the thing fed once they left the last working outlet behind was a problem for a later hour; they left it there. Dan settled into the back like he'd flown a hundred times, which as far as John knew he had not.

"Huntsville's close," Sam said, spreading a chart across his knee once they were both strapped in, working the numbers out loud the way John was learning he did everything that mattered. "Hundred and fifty miles, give or take. Half hour, thirty-five minutes in this thing. We launch in the next hour, we're on the ground there with real daylight left — hours of it, not minutes. If that math had come out different we'd be having a conversation about waiting for morning. It didn't. We go."

"And if it had?"

"Then we'd sit until first light and go in the second the sun came up, same as always." Sam glanced over, something steady and certain in it that John was starting to recognize as the whole shape of the man. "We don't launch into the thing we can already see coming. Only the ones we can't."

He ran the checklist with John following a beat behind, under the patient, exacting voice of a man who'd apparently decided teaching John to fly this airplane was every bit as urgent as flying it himself. The engine lit and spun up into a turbine whine John had only ever heard from a passenger seat, and the brand-new PC-12 rolled forward past its own showroom window, past the airplane that had carried Sam the last leg and would sit here now for as long as anyone left standing had a use for it.

"Huntsville," Sam said, lining up with the low gold light of the evening laid out ahead of them. "Then Phoenix. Then Portland."

"Then the people," John said.

"Then the people." Sam pushed the power lever forward, and the ground fell away beneath two men who'd met in person the evening before and were already, somehow, the crew they needed to be for what came next.
