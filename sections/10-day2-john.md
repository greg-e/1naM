# 10 — John, Day 1

PDK looked exactly like every other airport John had seen in the last two days — quiet in a way no airport should ever be quiet, a scatter of parked aircraft catching the late afternoon sun, not another living soul in sight — except for the one thing that made his chest go tight the second he saw it from the access road. A PC-12, sitting alone on the transient ramp, tail number he didn't recognize and didn't need to. Nobody else on earth was going to land a Pilatus at this specific airport at this specific hour. He parked crooked and didn't bother straightening it out.

Sam was already out of the aircraft by the time John crossed the ramp, standing in its shadow with both hands loose at his sides like a man who'd forgotten, for a second, what to do with them. He looked worse than John had pictured him — hollowed out around the eyes, three days of stubble, a flight suit that had clearly been slept in more than once — and also, somehow, exactly like the voice John had spent two days talking to. Dan reached him first, tail going, entirely unbothered by the fact that this was a stranger, and Sam went down on one knee in the middle of the ramp and put both hands into the dog's fur for a long moment before he stood back up and looked at John properly.

Neither of them said anything for a second. There didn't seem to be a version of *hello* that fit two men who'd already told each other the worst things they'd ever have to say over a dying phone connection.

"John," Sam said finally, like he was confirming it to himself as much as saying it out loud.

"Sam." He put his hand out, and Sam looked at it for half a second before he ignored it entirely and pulled him into something closer to a hug, hard and brief and over almost before John's arms had caught up to it. Neither of them mentioned it afterward. There wasn't a version of today that had room for embarrassment about that.

"Four o'clock," Sam said, checking a watch that had somehow survived everything, mouth doing something that wasn't quite a smile. "On the nose. I wasn't sure either of us was actually going to make it."

"Me neither," John admitted. "You look like hell."

"You should see the other guy," Sam said, and for a second something genuinely close to a laugh moved through him before it caught on everything underneath it and turned into something quieter instead.

They didn't waste long on the parts they already knew. John had lived Sam's night secondhand through a dead phone line and a locked door in Marietta; Sam had heard enough of John's last two days to fill in the rest without needing the full account standing on a ramp with daylight running out. What neither of them had said yet, out loud, in the same place at the same time, was what came next — and John gave him that first, plain and complete the way he'd promised himself he'd tell people things from now on.

"Three leads," he said. "Huntsville, Phoenix, Portland. Portland's Grace — she's hurt, but she's stable, she's expecting us, she's not going anywhere. Huntsville and Phoenix I don't have names for. Just addresses, and phone numbers that don't work anymore — not ringing, dead. Whole regions gone dark sometime yesterday, same as everywhere else."

Sam's face did something complicated at that — the particular look of a man doing math on how many more places like Newfoundland and Maine there might turn out to be. "Dead like the phones, or dead like the people."

"I don't know. That's the whole problem." John held his gaze. "That's why I think we go there first. Both of them. Grace already knows we're coming and she's got nowhere to be. Whoever's in Huntsville and Phoenix might not have that kind of time."

"Huntsville, then Phoenix, then Portland." Sam said it back like he was already loading it into a flight plan. "Yeah. That's the right order. That's the order I'd have picked."

That settled, more or less instantly, the way John was starting to understand things got settled with Sam — not argued over, just checked against each other and confirmed. It was the checking that came next that actually slowed them down.

"You fly?" Sam asked, out of nowhere, like it had just occurred to him to ask.

"Private pilot. Instrument rated. Single-engine." John felt oddly exposed saying it, the way he imagined confessing a hobby nobody had asked about. "Not current. Haven't touched a yoke in three years."

Something shifted in Sam's whole posture at that, visible relief in a man who hadn't looked capable of much relief a minute ago. "You have any idea what that's worth to me right now? I've been the only pilot on earth for two days. You're not qualified for a PC-12, not remotely, but currency's a piece of paper and the paper doesn't matter anymore. I can check you out on the airplane while we fly it. Teach you the systems, the numbers, let you fly the cruise portions under my eye. By the time we're done with this trip you'll be worth having in the right seat for real, not just as company."

"I'd rather be useful than just be company," John said, and meant it more than he expected to.

Sam looked past him then, out across the ramp, and something in his face changed again — calculating now, not emotional. "That's actually the other thing. Come look at this with me."

The aircraft Sam had flown in on sat exactly where he'd left it, and up close it told the whole story of the last two days without either of them needing to say it out loud — a long streak of grime along the belly, a scuff on one wingtip he didn't remember happening, the particular tired look of a machine that had been flown hard by someone with no ground crew and no mercy for it. Sam ran a hand along the leading edge like he was apologizing to it. "She got me here. I trust her. But we're about to ask a lot more of whatever we fly next than one more leg home, and I'd rather start this part of it in something that hasn't already been through what she's been through."

He was already walking before he finished the sentence, toward a low building on the far side of the field with a stylized mountain-and-crest logo over the door and PILATUS SALES & SERVICE stenciled beneath it in the same clean corporate lettering every dealership in the country seemed to share. Behind the glass showroom wall, sitting under lights that had clearly never gone off, was a PC-12 that still had the faint chemical smell of new paint radiating off it from twenty feet away — full ACE avionics suite lit up on standby, not a scratch anywhere on the airframe, the kind of airplane that existed to be looked at by somebody with the money to actually order one.

"Brand new," Sam said, something almost reverent in his voice. "PRO model. Newer avionics than mine, and mine's not old. Nobody's ever going to buy it now, and it's not doing anybody any good sitting behind glass." He didn't wait for John to agree before he found the service door around the side, tried the handle out of habit, and moved on to the small gray lockbox mounted beside it when the door held. It took him under a minute with a multitool from his flight bag — no bypass, no dispatcher's pegboard to cross-reference, nothing like the fights either of them had already been through for far less — and the box popped open to a single key fob sitting alone on its hook, tagged with the tail number in a dealer's careful sales-floor handwriting.

"That's it?" John said. "That easily?"

"Not everything gets to be hard," Sam said, and for the first time since John had watched him climb out of the other aircraft, he sounded like he almost believed it. The fuel gauges, when he checked them from the cockpit a minute later, read full — a demo aircraft kept topped off and ready to impress a customer who was never coming, one more small mercy neither of them examined too closely before deciding to just take it.

They moved fast after that, the particular efficient quiet of two people who'd both spent the last two days learning how to move fast without wasting motion. John's gear went across from the car — laptops, the SSDs, the hard-won satellite hotspot, a duffel that had started this whole trip in a different life — while Sam did a full walkaround on the new aircraft with the same unhurried thoroughness he'd have given a widebody, checking everything twice because twice was the only number that had kept him alive so far. Dan got settled in the back like he'd flown a hundred times before, which as far as John knew, he had not.

"Huntsville's close," Sam said, spreading a chart across his knee once they were both strapped in, working the numbers out loud the way John was already learning he did everything that mattered. "Hundred and fifty miles, give or take. Half hour, thirty-five minutes in this thing. Even accounting for everything we just did getting off the ground here, we land with real daylight left — hours of it, not minutes. If that math had come out different, we'd be having a conversation right now about waiting until morning instead of flying in blind on the first leg of this. It didn't come out different. We go tonight."

"And if it had?"

"Then we'd have found somewhere safe to sit until first light, same as always, and gone in the second the sun came up instead of the second we felt like it." Sam glanced over at him, something steady and certain in it that John was starting to recognize as the whole shape of the man. "That's the only rule that's kept me alive twice already. We don't launch into the thing we can already see coming. We only launch into the ones we can't."

He ran the checklist with John following along a beat behind him, relearning the shape of instruments he hadn't touched in three years under the patient, exacting voice of a man who'd apparently decided teaching him to fly this airplane was every bit as urgent as flying it themselves. The engine caught clean, spun up into a sound John hadn't heard from this close in a long time, and the brand-new PC-12 rolled forward past its own showroom window, past the tired, faithful aircraft that had gotten Sam this far and would sit here now for as long as anyone left standing had a use for it.

"Huntsville," Sam said, lining up on the runway with the last good light of the day laid out gold ahead of them instead of behind. "Then Phoenix. Then Portland."

"Then Grace," John said.

"Then Grace." Sam pushed the power lever forward, and the ground fell away beneath two men who had never met in person until an hour ago and were already, somehow, exactly the crew they needed to be for what came next.
