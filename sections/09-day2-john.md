# 9 — John, Day 2

They gave Catherine ten minutes to grab what she could carry from the hospital, and she used all of them, coming back across the torn stretch of fence with a duffel of supplies over one shoulder.

"There's one more stop," John said. "Portland. A woman named Grace, injured, alone, called 911 three days ago and read out her coordinates to a machine. We never got her on a line, never told her anyone was coming. We're flying there on faith that she's still where she said she was. If you'd rather stay here, set up somewhere safe, we'd understand."

Catherine looked at him like the question was slightly insulting. "I've spent three days in an empty hospital hoping somebody would eventually walk through the door needing a doctor," she said. "You're telling me there's an injured woman waiting on one, six hundred miles from here, and asking if I want to come *help her*?" She hefted the duffel higher onto her shoulder. "Absolutely. Let's go."

Sam had the chart out again before they'd finished strapping in. "Nine hundred twenty-five nautical the way the airways run it. Same altitude, but this one we do nonstop, real fuel to spare on the other end even fighting a headwind the whole way. First easy one we've had." He said it like he didn't quite trust it.

"Then let's not waste the good luck," John said, and Sam laughed and pushed the power lever forward.

The desert gave way to mountains and then, hours later, to green, and Tyler pressed his face to the window without saying anything. Catherine reached over and squeezed his shoulder once. Portland's runways came up out of a low ceiling, lights burning along the approach path. "Not the grid," Sam said, before John could ask. "Can't be, three days on. That whole zone's long dark by now. A field certified for this much traffic keeps its approach lighting on its own generator plant, fuel enough for a week or better with nothing else pulling on it." It was the first working airport lighting either of them had seen since Tuesday.

They found the rental lot at the edge of the field, keys in the consoles, a full tank, and pointed the car west toward the coast and the coordinates John had been carrying folded in his jacket pocket since PDK.

The overlook was a scrubby pull-off above a stretch of gray Pacific with cliffs dropping away on either side. Grace was sitting against a boulder a few yards off the trail, a splint of driftwood and a torn jacket sleeve lashed around one leg, a half-empty water bottle beside her. Her face went from wary to disbelieving as all four of them came into view.

"You actually came," she said, her voice rough. "I called 911. Got that — recording, that computer voice, whatever it was. Read it these coordinates off my phone about six times and never got a person. By day two I'd pretty much decided nothing on the other end had heard me."

"Something heard you," John said, crouching down to her level. "It logged the call. That's how we found the spot. I've been reading those logs for two days." He waited a second. "This is Catherine. She's a doctor. We're going to get you looked at properly."

Catherine was already working her fingers down the splint. "Tib-fib, most likely," she said after a minute, mostly to herself. "You did a decent job stabilizing it. Better than most people would manage alone on a cliff." To Grace: "It's going to hurt getting you into the car. I'm not going to pretend otherwise."

It did hurt. Grace's jaw went tight and her breath came sharp through her teeth as Sam and John lifted her between them, but she didn't make a sound beyond that, and once they had her settled across the back seat with her leg propped on a rolled jacket, her face started to unclench.

Catherine had them backtrack toward Seaside, fifteen minutes along the coast highway to a low brick building with PROVIDENCE SEASIDE on a sign out front, doors unlocked, power on. The hospital's own plant, John figured, the backup generator every ER in the country was required to keep, sized for days, with nothing in the building left to draw it down but the lights. Every light in the ER was on and nobody was in it. Catherine moved through it like she owned the place, finding a portable X-ray unit still charged, gloves and splinting supplies sealed in their wrappers. The films confirmed what she'd guessed on the cliff, a clean fibula fracture, nothing displaced, and she had Grace casted and resting within the hour.

"Best patient I've had all week," Catherine told her, taping off the last of it.

"Lowest bar you've ever cleared," Grace said, and laughed, small and cracked.

Grace's house was less than ten minutes further down the coast road, a small weathered place with a deck facing the water, and when John flipped the switch out of habit, the lights came on. "Panels and a battery wall out back," Grace said from the couch. "Islands itself off the grid automatically the second it sees a fault. I put it in myself after the third winter storm outage in a row. Told everyone I knew it was overkill." Hot water too, when Sam checked, off the same system. They took turns in long showers and came back downstairs to a kitchen that smelled like dinner.

John had found a little market two doors down still stocked, nobody at the register, and came back with enough for a real dinner: pasta, garlic, tomatoes going soft but not gone, a bottle of decent olive oil, a loaf of bread he toasted on the stove. He'd never cooked for five people in his life, and had barely cooked for one most weeks, but it steadied him to do something with his hands while everyone else sat still: Sam at the table with Dan's head in his lap, Tyler half-asleep against the window with the ocean going dark outside it, Grace with her cast up on a stack of couch cushions, Catherine pouring water for everyone.

"This is good," Sam said around a mouthful, sounding almost offended. "How do you have two people on this trip who can cook?"

"Google keeps decent knives in the office kitchen," John said. "Never stopped to notice how much I'd picked up watching other people use them."

Over the second helping Catherine set her fork down and looked around the table. "There's something I should tell all of you," she said. "My brother runs a shop outside Reno, TruckHouse. Overland vehicles, built for exactly this kind of thing. Off-grid capable, the works. I don't know if he's alive. I don't know if any of it's still standing. But if we're going to keep doing this, finding people, moving people, eventually a plane isn't going to be enough, and that's the only place I know of that was built for what's coming next."

Nobody answered right away. Outside, the tide worked at the rocks below the deck.

"Reno, then," Sam said finally. "Eventually."

"Eventually," Catherine agreed.
