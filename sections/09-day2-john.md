# 9 — John, Day 2

Catherine's things were in the back of the Subaru: a hard-sided medical bag with her name stenciled on the lid, a duffel, a case of bottled water. She carried the bag herself and let Sam take the duffel.

"There's one more," John said. "A woman on the Olympic Peninsula, west of Port Angeles, Washington. She called 911 once, Monday night their time, and read it her coordinates. The location record puts her at the Elwha River bridge on Highway 101. We never got her on a line. We don't know if she's hurt, and we don't know if she's still there. If you'd rather stay here, set up somewhere safe, we'd understand."

Catherine looked at him like the question was slightly insulting. "I've spent three days in an empty office listening for engines because I didn't know what else a doctor was for anymore," she said. "You're telling me there's a woman who called for help and nobody came, and asking if I want to go?" She shifted the bag to her other hand. "Absolutely."

Sam fueled the airplane to full off a truck on the GA ramp while John walked Catherine through the cabin and showed her where to strap in. Tyler moved his things to make room without being asked. Sam had the chart out on his knee before they'd closed the door. "Call it a thousand nautical, nonstop. Three hours forty with what the wind's been doing. Full tanks, and the most fuel to spare we've had on any leg." He said it like he didn't quite trust it. "First easy one."

"Then let's not waste it," John said, and Sam pushed the power lever forward.

They climbed out over the valley at half past one. The desert gave way to the plateau, and the plateau to the Great Basin, brown and ridged, the Sierra standing up white-topped off the left side. Catherine sat behind John with her knees drawn up and watched the ground. Tyler fell asleep. North of the Columbia the Cascades came up green and black with the volcanoes standing out of them, and past Seattle the Olympics lifted out of the haze ahead, snow in the high bowls, and the water of the Strait beyond them catching the late sun.

Port Angeles sat on a strip of flat ground between the mountains and the Strait, with the long hook of a sandspit curving out into the harbor. Wade R. Fairchild International was on the west side of town, a single main runway on a bench above the water. Sam made his low pass down runway 8 at two hundred feet and looked it over. Clear. A row of T-hangars, a few airplanes tied down on the aprons, a red-and-white medical helicopter on a pad at the far end. Nothing moving. He landed long, the way he wanted, and taxied to the terminal. 17:20 local.

The FBO was in the terminal, Citizen Air on the glass, the door unlocked. Behind the counter a pegboard held rental contract folders and a ring of keys tagged CREW CAR. The car was a white sedan out front with a full tank, and it started on the first try.

Before they left the airplane Catherine had Sam pull the blue tarp and two of the foam sleeping pads out of the cargo. "If she's down, she isn't walking out," she said, "and nobody's carrying her up a riverbank in their arms." They went in the trunk with her bag.

The Elwha bridge was ten minutes west on 101, through second-growth fir and past a scatter of houses with their porch lights out. The highway came down out of the trees and ran straight onto the bridge, a concrete span high over the river, and on it, a little past the middle, two vehicles sat where they'd stopped. A pickup had come across the center line into the westbound lane and met a small gray SUV nearly head-on, the driver's side of each front end taking the worst of it. The SUV's hood was folded up into the windshield, and its left front wheel had been driven back into the door. The hit had shoved it back and sideways against the rail. The pickup's front end had folded around the impact, and it sat skewed across both lanes.

John pulled over short of the bridge. Catherine was out before he'd set the brake.

The pickup's driver was still behind the wheel, a heavy man in a canvas coat, held upright by his belt, his head tipped against the side window. Catherine opened his door, checked him with two fingers, and then did something none of the rest of them had done: she pulled the collar of his coat back and looked at the skin of his neck and his hand where it lay on his thigh. The color was the same flushed pink John had seen on the driver in the ditch on Old Milton.

"Cherry red," she said. "That's cyanide. Or carbon monoxide." She looked at the cab, the windows, the dash. "There's no fire, no exhaust leak, he wasn't parked in a garage. It's the same as every one I've looked at since Tuesday." She closed the door on him. "Every one."

The SUV was empty. The airbags hung slack from the wheel and the roof rail. Where the driver's footwell should have been, the floor had come up and back, the pedals pushed most of the way to the seat. The driver's door had been forced open from inside, and there was blood on the frame and on the asphalt below it, old and brown. The back door behind it stood open too, and the floor behind the seat was empty except for a set of jumper cables. A trail went from there to the bridge rail and along it to the end of the span, where the rail stopped and the bank fell away through salal and alder toward the river. It was blood and scuffing, no footprints. Whoever made it had gone along sitting down.

Catherine looked into the footwell for a while and didn't say anything.

"Hello?" Sam called down. "Anybody down there?"

The river answered, and nothing else, and then, from under the bridge, a woman's voice, hoarse, the words coming in short pieces: "Took you long enough."

She was in the gravel under the first span, where the bank leveled off above the water, propped half upright against the concrete of the pier in a green Army poncho liner with a foil space blanket over it. A short, thickset woman, her black hair in a braid that had come half undone and was crusted dark along the hairline on the left. Both eyes were blacked. The skin across her cheekbones and the backs of her forearms was raw and shiny, like a bad sunburn. She held her left arm across her body with the hand tucked into the front of a hi-vis vest gone gray with dirt. Her left leg was splinted along both sides with two lengths of alder cut to size and bound with strips of a flannel shirt, the knots tied tight and square. The contents of a red duffel were laid out where her right hand could reach them: two empty water bottles and a third half full of river water, energy-bar wrappers folded flat, a first-aid kit standing open, an empty bottle of ibuprofen with its cap screwed back on. Her phone lay face down on a flat rock, dead.

"Della," she said, before anyone asked. "You're the 911 people."

"In a way," John said. He crouched down to her level. "My name's John. It logged your call. That's how we found you." He tipped his head at Catherine. "This is Catherine. She's an ER doctor."

Catherine was already on her knees beside the splint, her fingers working along it without disturbing the knots. "Who tied this?"

"I did."

"It's good." Catherine found the gap in the splint over the top of the foot, pressed a thumbnail on the big toe and watched the color come back, slowly, then laid two fingers on the top of the foot and held them there. "Wiggle your toes." Della did, a little. Catherine put the back of her hand on Della's forehead. She ran her fingers along the left collarbone, and Della's breath stopped and started again. She pressed the left side of the chest, low, and Della said through her teeth, "Yeah. There."

Catherine had a stethoscope out of the bag. She listened at four places on Della's back and longer on the left, then unzipped the vest and lifted the shirt. A band of bruise ran across Della's lower belly where a lap belt would sit, purple going green at the edges. Catherine pressed under the left ribs, and Della's whole body went rigid.

"How many of those did you take?" Catherine nodded at the ibuprofen bottle.

"Four at a time. Till it ran out. Yesterday."

"No more of that." Catherine sat back on her heels. "When did you last pee?"

Della thought about it. "This morning. Not much. Dark."

"Did you black out in the car?"

"Came to with the airbag in my lap. Don't know how long."

Catherine looked up at John. He'd never seen her face do anything she hadn't decided on, but it was doing something now. "It's the ankle, not just the fibula. Broken all the way across, I'd bet, and it's been out of place. Collarbone. Ribs on the left, two at least. She's running a fever, and there's something under those ribs I need a scanner to see." She turned back to Della. "We're not getting you up that bank under your shoulders. You're going up on a tarp."

"Fine," Della said. "Just go."

Sam went up for the tarp and the pads. They laid the pads on the tarp and rolled Della onto it in one movement on Catherine's count, Catherine holding the splinted leg, and Della made one sound and then no more. It took most of an hour. John and Sam took the head end, walking backward and uphill, dragging as much as carrying, the tarp sliding over the salal. Catherine took the foot end and the leg. Tyler went ahead with the flashlight, calling out roots and holes, with Dan on the leash. They stopped every few yards. At the top they set her down on the shoulder of the road. Her face was gray, and she was breathing fast and shallow, careful not to breathe deep. Catherine kept two fingers on her wrist and watched her for two minutes by her watch before she would let them move her again.

They reclined the sedan's front passenger seat as far back as it would go and eased her into it with the leg propped on the duffel. Catherine squeezed into the back behind her with Sam and Tyler, and Dan lay on the floor across their feet.

Olympic Medical Center was on the east side of town, a low hospital on a hill above the harbor, the main building lit while every street around it was dark. Its own plant, John figured, the backup generator every hospital in the country had to keep, with fuel for days and nothing in the building left to draw it down but the lights `[verify OMC generator]`. The ambulance doors opened for them. The emergency department was small, smaller than Catherine was used to; John watched her take in the bay count and the single trauma room at a glance. It had been a quiet Monday night when it happened. The board over the nurses' station showed one patient, in bay 4, curtain drawn. The night nurse was slumped at the station, and a tech lay on the floor of the hall beside a supply cart. Catherine looked at each of them once and went on past to the trauma room.

She worked the way John had watched Sam work the airplane, out loud and in order, using whoever was nearest. The monitor on the wall came up on the generator's power when she clipped on the leads. Heart rate 118. Temperature 101.4. Blood pressure 102 over 64. Oxygen 90. She hooked a cannula over Della's ears and turned the flowmeter on the wall to two liters, and John watched the 90 creep to 94. The medication cabinet wanted a fingerprint and a login. Sam opened it with the claw of a hammer from a maintenance closet, one drawer at a time. Catherine put an IV in each arm, filled a row of tubes from the first, fed a few drops into a handheld analyzer from a drawer, and hung a liter of saline wide open off the second.

"Hemoglobin's ten," she said, to no one in particular. "She's lost some. Kidneys are behind. That's three days of not drinking enough."

She ran an ultrasound probe over Della's belly and stopped under the left ribs. "Fluid around the spleen. Not much."

The CT scanner was down the hall, and its console came up on the generator too `[verify OMC CT on emergency power]`. Catherine had never run one without a tech, but she'd stood behind enough of them. She found the trauma protocol on the menu, had John and Sam slide Della across on a board, and stood at the console with her arms folded while the gantry turned. She read the images herself, scrolling with one finger, naming what she saw. "Spleen's torn. Small, and it's stopped. Collarbone, middle third. Ribs five, six, seven on the left. A little air outside the left lung, not enough to need a tube. Brain's clean." She scrolled back up to the spleen and looked at it a while longer.

The gash on Della's shin had been bleeding into the splint since Monday night. When Catherine cut the flannel away and lifted the alder, the skin around it was red and hot for a hand's width, and it smelled. She washed it out with a syringe and a liter of saline, cut away what was dead, and packed it open. "It's infected," she told Della. "If I close it, I seal that in." She hung a bag of broad-spectrum antibiotic and gave her a tetanus shot.

The ankle film came up in the reading room. Even John could see it was wrong: the leg bones broken on both sides of the joint, and the foot shifted back under them. "Three breaks," Catherine said. "Both sides and the back. She got it most of the way back in line herself." She numbed it with a long needle straight into the break and waited ten minutes by the clock on the wall. Then she put Sam at the head of the stretcher with his hands locked around Della's thigh above the bent knee. "Hold. Don't let her come toward me." She took the heel in one hand and the front of the foot in the other, pulled, drew the foot forward, and turned it in, and John heard the ankle move. Della said one word, loud. Catherine held the foot where it was while John wet rolls of plaster in a basin of warm water and handed them to her one at a time. She laid them down the back of the leg and in a U under the heel and up both sides, wrapped them, and held them with her palms until they set. The film after showed the bones back in line.

"No cast," she said. The skin over the ankle was tight and shiny, with blisters standing up on it. "It's too swollen. If I cast that tonight, I'm cutting it off by morning. This wants a surgeon with plates and screws, and I'm not one. We'll cast it when the swelling's down, in a week or so, and it'll heal a little crooked." She looked at Della. "You'll limp. Probably for good."

"I've been limping since Monday," Della said.

Catherine put the left arm in a sling. For the pain she gave morphine through the IV, not the anti-inflammatories she kept in her own pocket: not with the spleen, she said. She handed Della a clear plastic cylinder with a mouthpiece on a hose. "Breathe in on that till the ball comes up. Ten times an hour while you're awake. It's going to hurt. Do it anyway, or you'll have pneumonia by Sunday."

Then she went down the questions on a clipboard, the way John supposed she'd done a hundred thousand times. Allergies, none. Medications, none.

"Alcohol?"

Della looked at the ceiling. "Some."

"How much is some?"

"More than I'd tell a doctor."

Catherine took Della's right hand, held it out flat, and watched it. It was steady. She wrote something down and didn't comment.

By nine Della was asleep in the trauma room with the monitor beeping steady and two bags running. Nobody talked about a motel. Catherine wasn't going to leave her, and nobody was going to leave Catherine. John and Sam wheeled the stretcher out of bay 4, the sheet drawn up over the man on it, to an empty room at the end of the hall, closed the door, and carried the nurse and the tech to the room beside it and laid them on the beds. John wrote the room numbers and what they'd found on a paper towel and taped it to the counter at the nurses' station, and that was the end of what they could do about that.

He and Sam took the car back out to the field with Tyler and Dan for the sleeping bags, the stove, and everyone's bags, and to tie the airplane down for the night. There was a grocery on the way back with its doors unlocked and its coolers dark. John came out with pasta, garlic, a jar of olives, tomatoes going soft but not gone, a bottle of olive oil and a loaf of bread. He set up the camp stove from REI on a folding table under the ambulance canopy, where the smell would go out into the night instead of into the department. He'd never cooked for five people outdoors in his life. It steadied him to have something to do with his hands.

They ate in the trauma room. Sam sat on the floor against the wall with Dan's head in his lap. Tyler sat on a rolling stool. Catherine raised the head of the stretcher, and Della woke up enough to eat half a bowl, slowly, the fork in her right hand.

"This is good," Catherine said, around a mouthful of pasta, sounding almost offended. "Where did you learn to cook like this?"

"Waffle House, then sixteen years of living alone and getting bored of my own cooking," John said.

Afterward, with the plates stacked on a counter and the monitor beeping, Della told it, flat and in order, the way she'd have written up a fault. She'd been driving home from a late shift. She was a civilian mechanic at the Coast Guard air station out on the Hook, diesels and electrical, and she'd stayed late on a generator. Westbound on 101, a little before eleven. "Right before he came over the line I couldn't get a breath. Like somebody sat on my chest. I remember thinking I was having a heart attack at thirty-two. Then his lights were in my lane." She'd figured afterward it was panic. "It wasn't the ribs. The ribs are a knife. That was a weight, and then it was gone." She'd come to with the airbag in her lap, her foot turned wrong under the pedals, the pickup's engine still running, and the man in it hadn't answered. Her door had taken her good shoulder to open. She'd dragged the emergency bag out from behind the seat and called 911 from the asphalt and gotten the voice, and read it her coordinates off her map app, and it had told her help was being dispatched. Then she'd looked at the sky and decided she wasn't waiting on the bridge in the open, and she'd gone down the bank to the water on her backside with the bag dragging behind her. Down there she'd gotten the boot off and pulled the foot back straight, because it was going gray, and cut the splints with her pocketknife. The poncho liner had come out of the Army with her. Monday night to Thursday. She'd heard no cars and no airplanes until theirs, and when she'd heard it, she'd heard it go over low and come back around, and she'd known it was landing at Fairchild.

Catherine had gone still when Della said *couldn't get a breath*. She didn't say anything.

Over the second helping Catherine set her fork down and looked around the room. "There's something I should tell all of you," she said. "My brother runs a shop outside Reno. Basin and Range Overland. Overland vehicles, campers built to live in anywhere, off the grid. I don't know if he's alive. I don't know if any of it's still standing. But if we're going to keep doing this, finding people, moving people, eventually an airplane isn't going to be enough, and that's the only place I know of that was built for whatever comes next."

Nobody answered right away. Through the open ambulance doors, out past the harbor, a buoy bell rang, once and then again.

"Reno, then," Sam said finally. "Eventually."

"Eventually," Catherine agreed.
