# 6 — Wade, Day 1

Wade woke at six-thirty, the way he did every morning, to the generator running in the side yard, a steady drone under the window that hadn't been there when he went to bed. Sam was already up, at the kitchen counter in yesterday's clothes with the coffee made and two mugs poured, one of them meant for Wade. From the look of the pot he'd been up a while.

Wade showered and made the brain shake, blueberry, avocado, beet, spinach. Sam watched him run the blender. "Every day?" "Every day." "Huh." Sam passed on the offer of a glass, so Wade got out eggs and the bacon that needed eating before the refrigerator quit for good, and cooked him a plate.

"You were up late," Sam said while the bacon was going.

"Till eleven." Wade went down the hall for the legal pad and laid it on the counter between them. "I should tell you what I do first. It's the reason any of the rest of it is on here."

Sam waited.

"Systems architect, thirty years of it. The last stretch at Google, running the public-sector side out of the D.C. office. My team stands up their big model for the federal agencies, the ones that run the machinery under everything. FAA, DOT, Coast Guard, NOAA, Homeland Security, FEMA." He laid the names down flat, a list he'd recited in more briefings than he could count. "You don't build for an agency without a way into its live systems to test against. Flight data, weather, traffic, maritime. I've spent a career inside those pipelines. Helped design a good number of them."

"So from one chair," Sam said slowly, "you can see whatever's still switched on."

"Close to all of it. And yesterday morning, before you landed, I saved everything I could reach, every hour on the hour. Last night I finally looked at it." He turned the pad around so Sam could read it. "I wrote four questions on this yesterday morning. What's happening and how far it goes. Are there other survivors. How it happened. Why I'm alive. I didn't answer any of them last night. I've got something under every one."

He took the first one first. Every traffic camera in every city he'd checked, the same picture: cars stopped where they'd stopped, nothing moving, fewer cameras answering every hour. Ships on autopilot in every ocean, holding their courses until something got in the way. A bulk carrier run up on the Yucatán.

"The airplanes still up," Wade said. "Long-haul. I watched them drop off one at a time. I wrote down to ask you about the fuel. That Air India I saw yesterday morning, Delhi to Newark, past Newark and still headed south over the Carolinas around eight."

Sam thought about it over his coffee. "Fourteen, fifteen hours on the schedule. They load the trip, plus contingency, an alternate, and thirty minutes final reserve. They don't tank it full. He'd have been out by eight-thirty, nine. Off the Georgia coast, if it held south."

"That's where it went off the map. Between nine and ten."

Sam nodded and didn't say anything else about it.

"Then the grid," Wade said. "That's the one with a clock on it." He told him about the four interconnections and the coal running out of the hoppers and the gas plants tripping one by one, the frequency sagging until the relays started cutting load, the model putting the Eastern collapse around midnight. "Twelve-fifty. That's the generator you heard."

"And it's the one you were waiting on yesterday," Sam said. "That's why you stayed home and saved everything. You knew the pipes were going to close."

"Not when. I knew they would." Wade pointed at the date boxed on the page. "This is the one that matters. Plant Vogtle sits about a hundred and fifty miles southeast of this house. Four reactors. They scram themselves the second the frequency goes bad, control rods drop into the core and the reaction stops, automatic, no operator required, and that part takes care of itself. The part that doesn't is the spent fuel sitting in the cooling pools, still hot, still needing pumps to keep water moving over it. The pumps run on the site's emergency diesels and batteries, three days, maybe a week, with nobody left alive to refuel them. Best real number I've got is somewhere between ten and twenty-one days after the plant trips before a pool like that can boil down far enough to expose the rods to open air. After that it's a regional contamination event."

"Fallout?" Sam said. "What do you mean?"

"Nothing like a bomb. Think Fukushima, not Hiroshima. The fuel gets hot enough, the cladding catches, and what comes out is airborne and waterborne: cesium, iodine, strontium, into the soil and the water table for years, drifting downwind over however many miles the weather that week decides to give it."

Sam didn't say anything for a second, working through it the way Wade imagined he worked through a checklist. "And that's the early end."

"That's the early end. So whatever else we decide, we can't still be in this house when that date comes due. We need to head west. West of every reactor like it."

At a quarter past seven Dan was at the door with his leash in his mouth. Two miles, same as every morning; nobody had explained the new rules to him. "You coming?" Wade asked. Sam was already reaching for his shoes. Wade folded the pad into his back pocket.

They walked out into first light, the same light Wade had watched come up over this street for sixteen years, Dan ranging out to the end of the leash and back. The driveways were still full. The streetlights were out. There were fewer dogs behind the doors than yesterday, a bark here and there, and nothing at all from the Morrisons' house. They turned the corner at the bottom of the street. Dan stopped to work a patch of grass and Wade let him.

"Number three," Wade said. "How. I don't know how. I know when." He told him about the beacons, the earliest of them within a few minutes of two a.m. Eastern all over the world, a helicopter outside Seoul in the middle of the afternoon, a light plane in Bavaria at eight in the morning. "It didn't start somewhere and spread. It didn't wait for dark. It happened everywhere in the same few minutes, to people and nothing else. The dogs are fine. The machines are fine."

"Theresa and Ruth were fine at twelve-thirty," Sam said. "Ruth took my seat, and I briefed her myself. And when I came forward neither of them had picked up a mic. Two pilots, wide awake in the seats, and nobody called anybody." He walked a few steps. "Whatever it was, they didn't have time."

Wade wrote that on the pad against his palm while he walked: *DAL27 crew: awake, no call.*

"Four?"

"Almost nothing. I tried to find something you and I have that Harry didn't, or Elena and Rosa. We were asleep, they were asleep. Your two in the seats were awake. You were seven miles up and I was in my bed. It isn't where and it isn't awake or asleep. I couldn't find one thing we've got that they didn't."

Sam didn't say anything.

They were on Old Milton now, the long grade up toward the light that wasn't working.

"Number two," Sam said.

"You," Wade said. "That's the only sure thing I had. Then eleven posts on social media since two a.m., all over, no locations on any of them. I answered every one. One wrote back. A man in Porto, Portugal. Alone. Then the 911 calls. That agent that answered me on the Parkway answered everybody. Every call's in a log somewhere, a callback number and a location. Thousands of county systems I've got no business in. The model won't go near them unless I take the safety layer off. I helped write that layer." He walked a few steps. "I took it off last night. About eight-thirty. It's been running all night."

Sam looked over at him.

"Read only," Wade said. "911 records only. Calls after two. Nothing written back, nothing kept that the search doesn't need. Every system it touches gets logged. I wrote the scope down before I did it. In a test harness it takes three signatures, and there's a line on that page that says witness, and it's blank."

"You could have woken me up."

"You'd been up since three in the morning, and you'd just put your family to bed. No."

"Who's that rule there to protect?" Sam said.

"The counties. The callers. Anybody whose records are on those servers. That's the point of it. The rule doesn't care whether I mean well. Everybody who ever broke one meant well."

"The callers dialed 911 because they wanted somebody to come find them," Sam said. "And there's nobody at the consoles. You know ninety-one point three."

"Pilot in command." Wade knew it the way every pilot knew it, from the written and from the checkride. "In an in-flight emergency requiring immediate action, the pilot in command may deviate from any rule of this part to the extent required to meet that emergency."

"I went through it at three-thirty yesterday morning. Turned an airliner off its route with no clearance from anybody, landed it heavy at Edmonton, and took an airplane off a ramp there that belongs to somebody I've never met. None of that's legal on a normal day. I'd do every bit of it again." He started down the far side of the hill. "You wrote the extent down. That's more than I did."

They walked a while without talking. A dog barked behind a fence two houses down and Dan answered it once.

"So what do we do," Sam said. "Lay it out."

Wade had already laid it out to himself at eleven the night before, standing at the generator with the flashlight. "We can't stay. That one's settled. We could pick a field out west, somewhere clear of every reactor, and go. I don't like it. We'd be flying somewhere just to be somewhere."

"I don't fly anywhere I don't have a reason to land," Sam said.

"Or we wait on the search. If it turns anybody up, we go to them, and we keep going west from there."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we pick a field and go anyway, and keep looking from wherever we land." Wade folded the pad. "We've got an airplane."

"We've got an airplane sitting on the ramp at Epps with about fourteen hundred pounds in it," Sam said. "Fuel's in a truck at every airport in the country, and nobody to drive the truck. That's us now." He looked over. "You fly a 182."

"Out of PDK. Instrument-rated. I've never touched a turbine."

"Then you're about to learn one."

Back at the house they went into the office. Wade set the legal pad on the desk, turned to the page with the scope on it, and held out the pen. Sam read it through, wrote *S. Reyes* on the witness line, and dated it the sixth.

The machine had finished. Its instances had spent the night working county and regional dispatch systems across the country, and a lot of those systems had dropped off before morning when their own power failed, taking their logs with them. Across the ones it had reached, the model put the calls placed after two a.m. at somewhere over four thousand, most of them alarm panels and crash sensors and phones calling on their own. Three had survived every filter Wade had set.

A repeat caller in Huntsville, Alabama, sixteen calls. Another in Phoenix, Arizona, four calls. And a single call from a cell phone whose location record put it at the Elwha River bridge on Highway 101, west of Port Angeles, Washington, out on the Olympic Peninsula.

"No names," Wade said, reading off the screen. "Three locations, three callback numbers, and the carriers still show all three as live accounts."

He dialed Huntsville first. No ring, no busy tone, just a flat recorded failure, the sound a call made when there was nothing to carry it.

He set the phone down. "The towers run on batteries when the grid goes. Four hours, eight, depending on the site `[verify]`. Huntsville's are done." He looked at the other two numbers and didn't dial them. "So is everybody's, by now, or close to it. If we want to talk to these people, we go to them."

"Huntsville, Phoenix, Port Angeles," Sam said.

They spent the rest of the morning loading Wade's car. They'd worked it out on the walk: whatever they found in three places that far apart, they weren't coming back to this house ahead of the date boxed on the pad. The dev box went first, unplugged, wrapped in a fleece, and packed in his flight bag with its power brick and the external drives. Then the battery station Wade kept for storms, a five-kilowatt-hour Jackery the size of a carry-on suitcase, with wheels and a pull handle and a hundred and thirty-odd pounds of lithium inside it `[verify: Explorer 5000 Plus weight]`. He'd bought it for the hours between an outage and the generator, and for the fridge. They rolled it down the driveway and lifted it into the back of the car between them. Its folding solar panels went in flat on top `[verify: panel count and wattage in the Solar Generator kit]`. Full, it would run the box for two days on its own `[verify: ~100 W draw]`, and the panels could fill it again anywhere the sun came out. His logbook and headset. Clothes for a week. Dan's food, all of it. Naomi's Bible from the office desk, and the photo of her from his nightstand. He stood a minute in the doorway of her study, and then he closed the door.

Before they left he went out to the side yard and turned the generator's controller from AUTO to OFF. The engine ran down and stopped, and the house behind him went dark. He locked the front door and put the key on his ring with the others.

Sam asked for one stop on the way. "We've got an airplane and a computer. We don't have a first-aid kit worth the name or a way to make water safe." There was an REI off Perimeter Center, ten minutes off the route.

The parking lot was as empty as every lot they'd passed. The automatic doors didn't move. Sam found the seam between the panels, got his fingers into it, and walked one panel back along its track by hand. Past the first few yards of daylight from the front windows the store was dark, and warm, the air conditioning off since the night before. They took headlamps off the rack by the registers and went down the aisles with them.

Sam came back with an armful: a water filter, a first-aid kit heavier than anything most people kept in a car, a folding knife, thermal layers in both their sizes, wool socks. "Figured you're not the type to think about socks until your feet are already wet," he said, and dropped it all on the checkout counter. Wade didn't argue. He added his own armful, a camp stove and a case of fuel canisters, a tarp, two more headlamps and a box of batteries, and they filled two hiking packs and carried them out to the car. They left the door standing open behind them.

The PC-12 sat on the Epps ramp where Sam had shut it down the afternoon before, C-GKPX in black on the white fuselage. Nothing else on the field had moved.

The restaurant upstairs over the Epps hangar still had power. Sam asked how. "Some piece of the grid around here came through the collapse in balance," Wade said. "Whatever's still generating is matched to whatever's still drawing, so nothing tripped. It's an island. It won't last, and there's no telling when it goes." The walk-in freezer was still holding temperature. Wade made burgers in the kitchen and a stack of sandwiches for later, and fed Dan the trimmings, while Sam spread a chart across a table by the window. "Huntsville's a hundred and fifty miles, give or take," Sam said. "Forty minutes."

Wade was looking past him, out the window, down the row of hangars to the one with the Pilatus name across its doors `[verify: Pilatus dealer at PDK]`. "That's the Pilatus dealer," he said. "I park beside it every Saturday. Two weeks ago they had a brand-new one in there with the doors open. A PRO, the newest model. I stood at the tape line and looked at it for twenty minutes."

"We have an airplane," Sam said.

"I know. It's two hundred yards. Humor me."

The big doors were shut and the walk-in door beside them was locked. Sam went back to the Epps shop for a pry bar and had it open in under a minute, the frame splitting at the strike plate. Inside it was dark and warm, the only light coming through the translucent panels high in the doors. The airplane sat in the middle of the floor, white with a gray stripe, N612PR on the tail, clear protective film still on the leading edges of the wings.

The sales office was through a door at the back of the hangar. A delivery folder lay on the desk: a company in Charlotte, delivery Friday the eighth. Sam read the name on it and put the folder back where it had been. The keys were in the top drawer on a ring tagged with the tail number, and the document pouch and logbooks were in a file box beside them. Sam sat in the sales manager's chair with a headlamp and went through the logbooks page by page. Thirty-one point four hours total time, airframe and engine: acceptance flights at the factory in Switzerland, the ferry across the Atlantic, the completion center in Colorado, one leg to Atlanta `[verify PC-12 delivery path]`. No open squawks. Every inspection signed off.

They climbed into the cockpit with the headlamps, Sam in the left seat. He looked at the panel for a long time without touching anything. "Honeywell I know," he said. "This I don't."

"I do." Wade put a finger on the glass of the nearest display. "My 182 has a G1000. This is its big brother. Same company, same logic, same way it wants you to think. I'll know where the pages are." He reached up to the overhead panel and tapped a guarded button `[verify placement]`. "And there's this."

Sam read the placard. EMERGENCY AUTOLAND.

"If the pilot stops flying, it notices," Wade said. "Nobody touches the controls for long enough, it asks once, and then it takes the airplane `[verify PRO auto-activation]`. Or anybody in a passenger seat lifts the guard and pushes it. It picks the nearest runway long enough for the airplane, flies the approach, lands, brakes to a stop, and shuts down the engine. It needs GPS and a runway in its database. That's all it needs."

Sam was quiet for a while. Every year for ten years he'd sat through incapacitation drills in the simulator, the check airman slumping over the controls on cue. There had always been another pilot. "Theresa and Ruth were awake," he said. "Two of them in the seats, and neither one got a word out." He looked at the button. "Anything happens to me between here and wherever we end up, you've got eleven hundred hours in a 182 and a turbine you've never started."

"Then I push that," Wade said. "Somebody designed it for a passenger who can't fly. I build safety layers. I'd like to keep one."

Sam looked out through the door panels toward the Epps ramp, where the other airplane sat in the sun. "That one got me home. I know what's in its journey log and nothing else. This one's got thirty hours on it and every page signed." He took the airplane's flight manual out of the pouch and opened it on his knee. "Give me an hour with the book."

He read it the way he had at Edmonton, normal procedures front to back, then the limitations, then the emergency procedures. The engine was the same Pratt & Whitney he'd flown out of Edmonton, with the same electronic control behind the power lever. Most of what was new was the panel, and Wade sat in the right seat with the Garmin pilot's guide from the pouch and answered what Sam asked him. An hour and ten minutes later Sam closed the manual. "All right."

The hangar doors had a hand crank on the operator for when the power failed `[verify]`. Wade cranked while Sam watched the gap widen, and it took the two of them trading off twenty minutes to get the doors open past the wingspan. A battery tug sat on its charger in the corner, still near full. Sam towed the airplane out into the sun, across to the Epps ramp, and parked it off the left wingtip of C-GKPX.

Before they loaded anything he climbed into the Canadian airplane one last time. He got the journey log out of the seat pocket and filled in the two legs he'd flown, Edmonton to Minneapolis and Minneapolis to Peachtree: block times, fuel uplift, pilot in command, his name and his certificate number. He set the parking brake, locked the door, and hung the keys on the board in the Epps line office.

Then the fuel. Epps's Jet-A truck sat in its spot by the fuel farm with the keys in it. Sam drove it around to the new airplane and talked Wade through the fueling: the bonding cable clipped on first, Wade on the ladder with the nozzle, one wing and then the other, Sam watching the meter. The gauges had shown a little under half. It took about two hundred gallons to bring it to full `[verify]`. Sam drained a sample from each sump into a clear cup and held it up to the light. Clear and bright, no water.

They moved the Jackery out of Wade's car and up through the cargo door, a two-man lift again, and strapped it to the floor rings forward of the aft bulkhead, the solar panels flat beside it. Wade's flight bag with the dev box in it went on the floor behind the right seat, where he could reach it. Then the packs, Wade's bags, the dog food. Sam did the weight and balance on the legal pad from the numbers in the airplane's own manual: two pilots, the cargo, a forty-five-pound dog on the cabin floor, full fuel. It came out inside the envelope with room to spare `[verify: PC-12 PRO useful load with full fuel]`. He did it a second time anyway.

The rest of the afternoon they spent in the cockpit with the doors open for the air. The panel was close enough to the one in Wade's 182 that the screens and menus came to him on their own. Nothing else did: the autothrottle, the single power lever, the touchscreen controllers on the pedestal, the speeds he'd need to start memorizing. Sam walked him through the engine start twice with the battery off, hands only, calling each step, until Wade could say it back without the card.

At a quarter past seven Sam looked at the sun over the hangars and said it was time. Forty minutes to Huntsville, an hour back on the clock going west, and they'd be on the ground there with the sun still up.

Dan settled on the cabin floor behind the cockpit. Sam ran the checklist aloud with Wade following on the card. The engine lit and spun up into a turbine whine Wade had only ever heard from a passenger seat. Without thinking Sam keyed the mic. "Peachtree Tower, Pilatus Six One Two Papa Romeo at Epps, taxi for departure." Then he looked over at Wade with a wry grin. "Habit."

The windsock out past the hangars was lifting gently out of the north. Sam taxied out past C-GKPX at the edge of the ramp to runway 3 Right, still announcing on the tower frequency, lined up, and pushed the power lever forward.
