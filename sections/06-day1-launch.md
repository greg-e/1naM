# 6 — Will, Day 1

Will woke at six-thirty, the way he did every morning, to the generator running in the side yard, a steady drone under the window that hadn't been there when he went to bed. Sam was already up, at the kitchen counter in yesterday's clothes with the coffee made and two mugs poured, one of them meant for Will. From the look of the pot he'd been up a while.

Will showered and made the brain shake, blueberry, avocado, beet, spinach. Sam watched him run the blender. "Every day?" "Every day." "Huh." Sam passed on the offer of a glass, so Will got out eggs and the bacon that needed eating before the refrigerator quit for good, and cooked him a plate.

"You were up late," Sam said while the bacon was going.

"Till eleven." Will went down the hall for the legal pad and laid it on the counter between them. "I should tell you what I do first. It's the reason any of the rest of it is on here."

Sam waited.

"Systems architect, thirty years of it. The last stretch at Google, running the public-sector side out of the D.C. office. My team stands up their big model for the federal agencies, the ones that run the machinery under everything. FAA, DOT, Coast Guard, NOAA, Homeland Security, FEMA." He laid the names down flat, a list he'd recited in more briefings than he could count. "You don't build for an agency without a way into its live systems to test against. Flight data, weather, traffic, maritime. I've spent a career inside those pipelines. Helped design a good number of them."

"So from one chair," Sam said slowly, "you can see whatever's still switched on."

"Close to all of it. And yesterday morning, before you landed, I saved everything I could reach, every hour on the hour. Last night I finally looked at it." He turned the pad around so Sam could read it. "I wrote four questions on this yesterday morning. What's happening and how far it goes. Are there other survivors. How it happened. Why I'm alive. I didn't answer any of them last night. I've got something under every one."

He took the first one first. Every traffic camera in every city he'd checked, the same picture: cars stopped where they'd stopped, nothing moving, fewer cameras answering every hour. Ships on autopilot in every ocean, holding their courses until something got in the way. A bulk carrier run up on the Yucatán.

"The airplanes still up," Will said. "Long-haul. I watched them drop off one at a time. I wrote down to ask you about the fuel. That Air India I saw yesterday morning, Delhi to Newark, past Newark and still headed south over the Carolinas around eight."

Sam thought about it over his coffee. "Fourteen, fifteen hours on the schedule. They load the trip, plus contingency, an alternate, and thirty minutes final reserve. They don't tank it full. He'd have been out by eight-thirty, nine. Off the Georgia coast, if it held south."

"That's where it went off the map. Between nine and ten."

Sam nodded and didn't say anything else about it.

"Then the grid," Will said. "That's the one with a clock on it." He told him about the four interconnections and the coal running out of the hoppers and the gas plants tripping one by one, the frequency sagging until the relays started cutting load, the model putting the Eastern collapse around midnight. "Twelve-fifty. That's the generator you heard."

"And it's the one you were waiting on yesterday," Sam said. "That's why you stayed home and saved everything. You knew the pipes were going to close."

"Not when. I knew they would." Will pointed at the date boxed on the page. "This is the one that matters. Plant Vogtle sits about a hundred and fifty miles southeast of this house. Four reactors. They scram themselves the second the frequency goes bad, control rods drop into the core and the reaction stops, automatic, no operator required, and that part takes care of itself. The part that doesn't is the spent fuel sitting in the cooling pools, still hot, still needing pumps to keep water moving over it. The pumps run on the site's emergency diesels and batteries, three days, maybe a week, with nobody left alive to refuel them. Best real number I've got is somewhere between ten and twenty-one days after the plant trips before a pool like that can boil down far enough to expose the rods to open air. After that it's a regional contamination event."

"Fallout?" Sam said. "What do you mean?"

"Nothing like a bomb. Think Fukushima, not Hiroshima. The fuel gets hot enough, the cladding catches, and what comes out is airborne and waterborne: cesium, iodine, strontium, into the soil and the water table for years, drifting downwind over however many miles the weather that week decides to give it."

Sam didn't say anything for a second, working through it the way Will imagined he worked through a checklist. "And that's the early end."

"That's the early end. So whatever else we decide, we can't still be in this house when that date comes due. We need to head west. West of every reactor like it."

At a quarter past seven Dan was at the door with his leash in his mouth. Two miles, same as every morning; nobody had explained the new rules to him. "You coming?" Will asked. Sam was already reaching for his shoes. Will folded the pad into his back pocket.

They walked out into first light, the same light Will had watched come up over this street for sixteen years, Dan ranging out to the end of the leash and back. The driveways were still full. The streetlights were out. There were fewer dogs behind the doors than yesterday, a bark here and there, and nothing at all from the Morrisons' house. They turned the corner at the bottom of the street. Dan stopped to work a patch of grass and Will let him.

"Number three," Will said. "How. I don't know how. I know when." He told him about the beacons, the earliest of them within a few minutes of two a.m. Eastern all over the world, a helicopter outside Seoul in the middle of the afternoon, a light plane in Bavaria at eight in the morning. "It didn't start somewhere and spread. It didn't wait for dark. It happened everywhere in the same few minutes, to people and nothing else. The dogs are fine. The machines are fine."

"Theresa and Ruth were fine at twelve-thirty," Sam said. "Ruth took my seat, and I briefed her myself. And when I came forward neither of them had picked up a mic. Two pilots, wide awake in the seats, and nobody called anybody." He walked a few steps. "Whatever it was, they didn't have time."

Will wrote that on the pad against his palm while he walked: *DAL27 crew: awake, no call.*

"Four?"

"Almost nothing. I tried to find something you and I have that Harry didn't, or Elena and Rosa. We were asleep, they were asleep. Your two in the seats were awake. You were seven miles up and I was in my bed. It isn't where and it isn't awake or asleep. I couldn't find one thing we've got that they didn't."

Sam didn't say anything.

They were on Old Milton now, the long grade up toward the light that wasn't working.

"Number two," Sam said.

"You," Will said. "That's the only sure thing I had. Then eleven posts on social media since two a.m., all over, no locations on any of them. I answered every one. One wrote back. A man in Porto, Portugal. Alone. Then the 911 calls. That agent that answered me on the Parkway answered everybody. Every call's in a log somewhere, a callback number and a location. Thousands of county systems I've got no business in. The model won't go near them unless I take the safety layer off. I helped write that layer." He walked a few steps. "I took it off last night. About eight-thirty. It's been running all night."

Sam looked over at him.

"Read only," Will said. "911 records only. Calls after two. Nothing written back, nothing kept that the search doesn't need. Every system it touches gets logged. I wrote the scope down before I did it. In a test harness it takes three signatures, and there's a line on that page that says witness, and it's blank."

"You could have woken me up."

"You'd been up since three in the morning, and you'd just put your family to bed. No."

"Who's that rule there to protect?" Sam said.

"The counties. The callers. Anybody whose records are on those servers. That's the point of it. The rule doesn't care whether I mean well. Everybody who ever broke one meant well."

"The callers dialed 911 because they wanted somebody to come find them," Sam said. "And there's nobody at the consoles. You know ninety-one point three."

"Pilot in command." Will knew it the way every pilot knew it, from the written and from the checkride. "In an in-flight emergency requiring immediate action, the pilot in command may deviate from any rule of this part to the extent required to meet that emergency."

"I went through it at three-thirty yesterday morning. Turned an airliner off its route with no clearance from anybody, landed it heavy at Edmonton, and took an airplane off a ramp there that belongs to somebody I've never met. None of that's legal on a normal day. I'd do every bit of it again." He started down the far side of the hill. "You wrote the extent down. That's more than I did."

They walked a while without talking. A dog barked behind a fence two houses down and Dan answered it once.

"So what do we do," Sam said. "Lay it out."

Will had already laid it out to himself at eleven the night before, standing at the generator with the flashlight. "We can't stay. That one's settled. We could pick a field out west, somewhere clear of every reactor, and go. I don't like it. We'd be flying somewhere just to be somewhere."

"I don't fly anywhere I don't have a reason to land," Sam said.

"Or we wait on the search. If it turns anybody up, we go to them, and we keep going west from there."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we pick a field and go anyway, and keep looking from wherever we land." Will folded the pad. "We've got an airplane."

"We've got an airplane sitting on the ramp at Epps with about fourteen hundred pounds in it," Sam said. "Fuel's in a truck at every airport in the country, and nobody to drive the truck. That's us now." He looked over. "You fly a 182."

"Out of PDK. Instrument-rated. I've never touched a turbine."

"Then you're about to learn one."

Back at the house they went into the office. Will set the legal pad on the desk, turned to the page with the scope on it, and held out the pen. Sam read it through, wrote *S. Reyes* on the witness line, and dated it the sixth.

The machine had finished. Its instances had spent the night working county and regional dispatch systems across the country, and a lot of those systems had dropped off before morning when their own power failed, taking their logs with them. Across the ones it had reached, the model put the calls placed after two a.m. at somewhere over four thousand, most of them alarm panels and crash sensors and phones calling on their own. Three had survived every filter Will had set.

A repeat caller in Huntsville, Alabama, sixteen calls. Another in Phoenix, Arizona, four calls. And a single call from a cell phone whose location record put it at the Elwha River bridge on Highway 101, west of Port Angeles, Washington, out on the Olympic Peninsula.

"No names," Will said, reading off the screen. "Three locations, three callback numbers, and the carriers still show all three as live accounts."

He dialed Huntsville first. No ring, no busy tone, just a flat recorded failure, the sound a call made when there was nothing to carry it.

He set the phone down. "The towers run on batteries when the grid goes. Four hours, eight, depending on the site `[verify]`. Huntsville's are done." He looked at the other two numbers and didn't dial them. "So is everybody's, by now, or close to it. If we want to talk to these people, we go to them."

"Huntsville, Phoenix, Port Angeles," Sam said.

They spent the rest of the morning loading Will's car. They'd worked it out on the walk: whatever they found in three places that far apart, they weren't coming back to this house ahead of the date boxed on the pad. The workstation went first, a two-man lift, a hundred-odd pounds of compute in a case built to sit still, then its power supply and the battery station Will kept for storms. The external drives. His flight bag, logbook, and headset. Clothes for a week. Dan's food, all of it. Naomi's Bible from the office desk, and the photo of her from his nightstand. He stood a minute in the doorway of her study, and then he closed the door.

Before they left he went out to the side yard and turned the generator's controller from AUTO to OFF. The engine ran down and stopped, and the house behind him went dark. He locked the front door and put the key on his ring with the others.

Sam asked for one stop on the way. "We've got an airplane and a computer. We don't have a first-aid kit worth the name or a way to make water safe." There was an REI off Perimeter Center, ten minutes off the route.

The parking lot was as empty as every lot they'd passed. The automatic doors didn't move. Sam found the seam between the panels, got his fingers into it, and walked one panel back along its track by hand. Past the first few yards of daylight from the front windows the store was dark, and warm, the air conditioning off since the night before. They took headlamps off the rack by the registers and went down the aisles with them.

Sam came back with an armful: a water filter, a first-aid kit heavier than anything most people kept in a car, a folding knife, thermal layers in both their sizes, wool socks. "Figured you're not the type to think about socks until your feet are already wet," he said, and dropped it all on the checkout counter. Will didn't argue. He added his own armful, a camp stove and a case of fuel canisters, a tarp, two more headlamps and a box of batteries, and they filled two hiking packs and carried them out to the car. They left the door standing open behind them.

The PC-12 sat on the Epps ramp where Sam had shut it down the afternoon before, C-GKPX in black on the white fuselage. Nothing else on the field had moved.

The restaurant upstairs over the Epps hangar still had power, the only building on the field that did. Sam asked how. "Some piece of the grid around here came through the collapse in balance," Will said. "Whatever's still generating is matched to whatever's still drawing, so nothing tripped. It's an island. It won't last, and there's no telling when it goes." The walk-in freezer was still holding temperature. Will made burgers in the kitchen and a stack of sandwiches for later, and fed Dan the trimmings, while Sam spread a chart across a table by the window. "Huntsville's a hundred and fifty miles, give or take," Sam said. "Forty minutes."

Then the work. Epps's Jet-A truck sat in its spot by the fuel farm with the keys in it. Sam drove it around to the airplane and talked Will through the fueling: the bonding cable clipped on first, Will on the ladder with the nozzle, one wing and then the other, Sam watching the meter. The airplane had come in from Minneapolis with about 1,400 pounds aboard. It took a little under two hundred gallons to bring it back to full `[verify burn]`. Sam drained a sample from each sump into a clear cup and held it up to the light. Clear and bright, no water.

They moved the workstation out of Will's car and up through the PC-12's cargo door, a two-man lift again, and strapped it to the floor rings forward of the aft bulkhead, its power supply and the storm battery after it. Then the packs, Will's bags, the dog food. Sam did the weight and balance on the legal pad from the numbers in the airplane's own handbook: two pilots, the cargo, a forty-five-pound dog on the cabin floor, full fuel. It came out inside the envelope with room to spare `[verify: PC-12 NGX useful load with full fuel]`. He did it a second time anyway.

The rest of the afternoon they spent in the cockpit with the doors open for the air. Sam took Will through the panel, three generations past the round gauges in his 182: the big displays, the autopilot, the single power lever and the condition lever beside it, the speeds he'd need to start memorizing. He walked him through the engine start twice with the battery off, hands only, calling each step, until Will could say it back without the card.

At a quarter past seven Sam looked at the sun over the hangars and said it was time. Forty minutes to Huntsville, an hour back on the clock going west, and they'd be on the ground there with the sun still up.

Dan settled on the cabin floor behind the cockpit. Sam ran the checklist aloud with Will following on the card. The engine lit and spun up into a turbine whine Will had only ever heard from a passenger seat. Without thinking Sam keyed the mic. "Peachtree Tower, Pilatus Kilo Papa X-ray at Epps, taxi for departure." Then he looked over at Will with a wry grin. "Habit."

The windsock out past the hangars was lifting gently out of the north. Sam taxied to runway 3 Right, still announcing on the tower frequency, lined up, and pushed the power lever forward.
