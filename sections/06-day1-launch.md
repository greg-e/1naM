# 6 — Will, Day 1

Will woke a little after six to the generator running in the side yard, a steady drone under the window that hadn't been there the night before. Sam was already up, at the kitchen counter with two mugs of coffee poured, one of them meant for Will.

Will showered and made the brain shake, blueberry, avocado, beet, spinach. Sam watched him run the blender. "Every day?" "Every day." "Huh." Sam passed on the offer of a glass.

Dan was at the door with his leash in his mouth before Will reached for it. Two miles, same as every morning; nobody had explained the new rules to him. "You coming?" Will asked. Sam was already reaching for his shoes.

They walked in the same low gold light Will had watched come up over this street for sixteen years, Dan ranging out to the end of the leash and back. The driveways were still full. The streetlights were out. There were fewer dogs behind the doors than yesterday, a bark here and there, and nothing at all from the Morrisons' house. At the top of the hill on Old Milton Will stopped to get his breath, and Sam stopped beside him and coughed into his elbow, and neither of them mentioned it.

Back at the house they went into the office. The machine had finished. Its instances had spent the night working county and regional dispatch systems across the country, and a lot of those systems had dropped off before morning when their own power failed, taking their logs with them. Across the ones it had reached, the model put the calls placed after two a.m. at somewhere over four thousand, most of them alarm panels and crash sensors and phones calling on their own. Three had survived every filter Will had set.

A repeat caller in Huntsville, Alabama, sixteen calls. Another in Phoenix, Arizona, four calls. And a single call from a cell phone whose location record put it at the Elwha River bridge on Highway 101, west of Port Angeles, Washington, out on the Olympic Peninsula.

"No names," Will said, reading off the screen. "Three locations, three callback numbers, and the carriers still show all three as live accounts."

He dialed Huntsville first. No ring, no busy tone, just a flat recorded failure, the sound a call made when there was nothing to carry it.

He set the phone down. "The towers run on batteries when the grid goes. Four hours, eight, depending on the site `[verify]`. Huntsville's are done." He looked at the other two numbers and didn't dial them. "So is everybody's, by now, or close to it. If we want to talk to these people, we go to them."

"Huntsville, Phoenix, Port Angeles," Sam said.

They spent the rest of the morning loading Will's car. They'd worked it out on the pad the night before: whatever they found in three places that far apart, they weren't coming back to this house ahead of the date at the bottom of the page. The workstation went first, a two-man lift, a hundred-odd pounds of compute in a case built to sit still, then its power supply and the battery station Will kept for storms. The external drives. His flight bag, logbook, and headset. Clothes for a week. Dan's food, all of it. Naomi's Bible from the office desk, and the photo of her from his nightstand. He stood a minute in the doorway of her study, and then he closed the door.

Before they left he went out to the side yard and turned the generator's controller from AUTO to OFF. The engine ran down and stopped, and the house behind him went dark. He locked the front door and put the key on his ring with the others.

Sam asked for one stop on the way. "We've got an airplane and a computer. We don't have a first-aid kit worth the name or a way to make water safe." There was an REI off Perimeter Center, ten minutes off the route.

The parking lot was as empty as every lot they'd passed. The automatic doors didn't move. Sam found the seam between the panels, got his fingers into it, and walked one panel back along its track by hand. Past the first few yards of daylight from the front windows the store was dark, and warm, the air conditioning off since the night before. They took headlamps off the rack by the registers and went down the aisles with them.

Sam came back with an armful: a water filter, a first-aid kit heavier than anything most people kept in a car, a folding knife, thermal layers in both their sizes, wool socks. "Figured you're not the type to think about socks until your feet are already wet," he said, and dropped it all on the checkout counter. Will didn't argue. He added his own armful, a camp stove and a case of fuel canisters, a tarp, two more headlamps and a box of batteries, and they filled two hiking packs and carried them out to the car. They left the door standing open behind them.

The PC-12 sat on the Epps ramp where Sam had shut it down the afternoon before, C-GKPX in black on the white fuselage. Nothing else on the field had moved.

The restaurant upstairs over the Epps hangar still had power, the only building on the field that did. Sam asked how. "Some piece of the grid around here came through the collapse in balance," Will said. "Whatever's still generating is matched to whatever's still drawing, so nothing tripped. It's an island. It won't last, and there's no telling when it goes." The walk-in freezer was still holding temperature. Will made burgers in the kitchen and a stack of sandwiches for later, and fed Dan the trimmings, while Sam spread a chart across a table by the window. "Huntsville's a hundred and fifty miles, give or take," Sam said. "Forty minutes."

Then the work. Epps's Jet-A truck sat in its spot by the fuel farm with the keys in it. Sam drove it around to the airplane and talked Will through the fueling: the bonding cable clipped on first, Will on the ladder with the nozzle, one wing and then the other, Sam watching the meter. The airplane had come in from Minneapolis with about 1,400 pounds aboard. It took a little under two hundred gallons to bring it back to full `[verify burn]`. Sam drained a sample from each sump into a clear cup and held it up to the light. Clear and bright, no water.

They moved the workstation out of Will's car and up through the PC-12's cargo door, a two-man lift again, and strapped it to the floor rings forward of the aft bulkhead, its power supply and the storm battery after it. Then the packs, Will's bags, the dog food. Sam did the weight and balance on the legal pad from the numbers in the airplane's own handbook: two pilots, the cargo, a forty-five-pound dog on the cabin floor, full fuel. It came out inside the envelope with room to spare `[verify: PC-12 NGX useful load with full fuel]`. He did it a second time anyway.

The rest of the afternoon they spent in the cockpit with the doors open for the air. Sam took Will through the panel, three generations past the round gauges in his 182: the big displays, the autopilot, the single power lever and the condition lever beside it, the speeds he'd need to start memorizing. He walked him through the engine start twice with the battery off, hands only, calling each step, until Will could say it back without the card.

At a quarter past seven Sam looked at the sun over the hangars and said it was time. Forty minutes to Huntsville, an hour back on the clock going west, and they'd be on the ground there with the sun still up.

Dan settled on the cabin floor behind the cockpit. Sam ran the checklist aloud with Will following on the card. The engine lit and spun up into a turbine whine Will had only ever heard from a passenger seat. Without thinking Sam keyed the mic. "Peachtree Tower, Pilatus Kilo Papa X-ray at Epps, taxi for departure." Then he looked over at Will with a wry grin. "Habit."

The windsock out past the hangars was lifting gently out of the north. Sam taxied to runway 3 Right, still announcing on the tower frequency, lined up, and pushed the power lever forward.
