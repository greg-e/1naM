# The Seventeen

### 9/12/2028, 07:30 local Pacific Time - John

Catherine took the splint off in the sick bay with a pair of trauma shears, and the three of them looked at the ankle. The swelling had gone down to where John could see the bones again, and the skin had gone from purple to a yellow-green that ran down into the foot. The cut on the shin was pink at the edges and dry.

"Better," Catherine said. "That's a cast today."

"What color?" Della said.

"White. I've got plaster."

She had laid it all out on a tray the night before: a roll of stockinette, rolls of cotton padding, six rolls of plaster in their foil, a basin of water she tested with her wrist. Sam held the leg up by the heel and the knee, as she showed him, with the foot at a right angle. Catherine rolled the stockinette up over the foot and the shin, then the padding, overlapping by half and going thicker over the ankle bones. She dipped the first roll of plaster, squeezed it once, and started at the toes.

"Don't let it drop," she told Sam. "If it drops, the foot sets wrong."

"It won't drop."

John held the basin. The plaster went on gray and smelled like a wet basement. Catherine worked fast, smoothing each turn with the heel of her hand, and when the roll was gone she started another. She turned the stockinette back at the top and the toes and laid the last roll over it. Then she put her palms on either side of the ankle and held them there, molding it, not talking.

Della watched the clock on the bulkhead. "How long?"

"Ten minutes to set. A day before it's hard. You don't put that foot down for six weeks, and you don't knock it on anything for two days."

"I want to go over to the station today," Della said.

Catherine kept her hands where they were.

"When they move them," Della said. "I want to be there. You won't know who anybody is."

"We'll read the name tapes."

"Half of them were asleep. They won't be in uniform." Della looked at John. "I know where everybody slept."

Catherine took her hands away and pressed a thumbnail into the plaster at the toes. "After lunch. In a wheelchair, with the leg up, and you stay outside. Not one thing on your lap heavier than a clipboard."

"Okay."

"I mean it about the lap."

"I said okay."

Tyler came in with his notebook while Catherine was cleaning the basin. He looked at the cast a long time.

"Is it hard yet?"

"No," Della said. "Don't touch it."

"Can I write on it later?"

"Tomorrow."

He wrote the day's water at the top of his page and went out with Dan. Sam had told him at breakfast that he and the dog had the ship while the rest of them were at the station, and that he was to stay aboard. He'd said okay and then asked how long.

### 09:10 local Pacific Time

They had used up the clinic's body bags on *Active*'s five. Catherine had brought twelve more from the hospital on Saturday in the bed of the pickup, and the station's supply room had four. John counted them on the floor of the hangar office and came up one short.

"There's a stretcher bag in the boat," Sam said. "Orange. I'll get it."

They wore the N95s and two pairs of gloves, and Catherine had put a smear of something mint under each of their noses from a tube in her bag. It helped for about a minute.

They started with the gate booth, because Peña was alone and close to the road. Then the watch floor. Boyd was heavier than he looked through the window, and it took John and Sam together to get him off the chair and into the bag on the floor without letting him fall. The second man had slid down in his chair until his knees were against the desk. He had a lanyard with a badge on it, and John read the name off it and wrote it down: *Abernathy, OS2*.

Upstairs in the ops building was the duty officer's room, a bed and a desk and a phone. The duty officer was a woman in a gray T-shirt, on top of the covers, with her reading glasses folded on the desk beside the phone. John didn't read anything off her. He wrote *duty officer's room* and the time.

The hangar was worse, because it had been shut up and warm. The alert aircrew slept in a bunkroom off the back of it, four racks, four people, two in flight suits and two in PT gear. Their boots were lined up by the door with the laces open. In the hangar itself the night maintenance crew had been working on the middle helicopter with its cowlings off and a stand beside the engine. Two of them were on the floor by the stand. The third was at a bench along the side wall, with his head down on his arms beside an open manual.

Sam stood in the middle of the hangar after they had the first two in bags and looked up at the helicopter.

"They were doing a phase inspection," he said. "Middle of the night, so it'd be ready for the morning."

There were four more in a bunkroom over the boat shed, the boat crew on duty, and one in the galley of the station building, a young man in a white apron who had been mopping. The mop was still in the bucket.

That made seventeen. They brought them out in the Stokes litter, one at a time, and laid them in a row on the apron in the shade of the hangar, with the zippers closed and a tag on each handle where Catherine had written the place they had been found.

### 13:00 local Pacific Time

Catherine pushed the wheelchair along the apron, and John walked beside it with the clipboard. Della's leg stuck out ahead of her on the raised footrest in its white cast, with a folded towel under the heel. She had her good hand on the armrest and her sling arm across her stomach.

They stopped at the first bag. John read the tag. "Gate booth."

"Peña. Rafael. Seaman." Della waited for him to write it. "He was saving for a truck."

"Watch floor, left chair."

"That's Boyd. Thomas Boyd. Chief." She waited again. "Watch floor, the other one, that's Abernathy. Kelsey. Second class."

John wrote. Catherine moved the chair along.

"Duty officer's room."

"Lieutenant Harker. Anne Harker. She had the duty Monday night." Della looked at the bag. "She let me park in her spot when it rained."

They did the aircrew next. Della went down the four tags without looking at John: the two pilots by name and rank, the flight mechanic, the rescue swimmer, who was twenty-three. She knew which rack each of them took. John wrote it all and checked the spelling with her. Her voice stayed the same the whole way. Catherine had stopped pushing and was standing behind the chair with her hands on the grips.

"Hangar floor, by the stand," John said. "Two."

"Kowalski and Fenn. Fenn's the tall one." She closed her eyes and opened them. "Was."

"Hangar bench, side wall."

Della didn't say anything. John looked up from the clipboard.

"Which bench?"

"The fourth one from the door."

"That's Sandoval's." She said it quietly and then she said it again, louder, for the clipboard. "Sandoval. Miguel. AMT1. Night maintenance lead." She looked toward the hangar doors. "Was there a thermos on that bench? Green. Tape on it."

John went in and found it, on the bench beside the open manual, a dented green thermos with a strip of masking tape around the lid and *D.J.* written on it in marker. He brought it out. Della took it with her good hand and set it on her lap, on top of the towel beside the cast, and held it there.

"He filled it for me when he came on," she said. "Every night. I never asked him to. I'd come up off the boats at seven and it'd be on my bench, full." She turned the thermos so the tape faced her. "Monday I left early. I took the other one."

Nobody said anything. Catherine looked down at the thermos and didn't take it off her lap.

Della looked at the next bag for a long time. Then she held her good hand out to John, and he didn't know what she wanted until she said, "Give me that," and took the clipboard and laid it on the thermos and held the pen ready.

"Read me the tag," she said.

He read it. She named the four from the boat crew and the cook, and wrote each one down herself in her block capitals, slowly, with the clipboard braced against the thermos, and her face didn't change at all, and her writing got smaller and smaller down the page.

When it was done she handed the clipboard back.

They took the seventeen back along the spit in four loads in the bed of the pickup, past the pilot station and the boat launch, to the lumber sheds at the landward end, and laid them in a row beside *Active*'s five. That made twenty-two. John wrote the seventeen names in the station's own log on the watch floor, under the last entry Boyd had made, with the places and the time, and signed it.

### 16:30 local Pacific Time

Catherine asked John to drive her to the hospital for dressings and IV fluid. On the way up the hill she ran through what Della would need for six weeks in plaster, and what Tyler would need, and what she didn't have for any of them, and wrote it on the back of an envelope against her knee.

At the hospital she went in first. She had the gray lockbox from the ship under her arm. The medication room hadn't locked since the first night, when she'd forced the cabinet with the claw of a hammer, and she went in there alone and shut the door while he brought a cart around from the loading dock. When he came back through the emergency department with the cart she was out again, with the lockbox.

She sent him to central supply with the envelope. It took him twenty minutes to find all of it on the shelves. When he came back down she wasn't at the cart.

He found her at the nurses' station. The staff board on the wall behind the counter was a whiteboard ruled in black tape, the same as it had been on Wednesday night when they came in with Della, and he hadn't looked at it then. *Mon 9/4 Nights*. A charge nurse's name, two more nurses, a tech, the rooms each of them had, a doctor on call from home. Someone had drawn a sun with sunglasses in the corner beside *Happy Labor Day*.

Catherine stood with her back to him and her right hand flat on the board beside the names, and her forehead against the board above her hand. She wasn't making any sound. Her shoulders went up and came down.

John backed out through the double doors without letting them swing. He stood in the hallway with the cart for a minute. Then he pushed it into the frame of the door hard enough to rattle the bottles, and said, "Catherine? I've got your list," and gave it a few seconds before he went in.

She was at the counter with the envelope, checking the cart off against it.

"You got the big tape," she said. "Good." She didn't look at him and didn't need to. On the way out she stopped at the board and capped a marker that had been lying open in the tray since Monday night, and put it back.

### 9/13/2028, 13:15 local Pacific Time

John had brought Della her coffee at seven and asked her where the jumper was. "My bench in the shop," she said. "Gold bag. The work order's on it." That was all she said about it, and when he looked in at eight she was asleep again.

He went for it at noon. It had been on her bench in a gold bag with the work order taped to it since the thirtieth of August. The fourth bench along, Sandoval's, was clear.

The tower stood at the end of the station's lot on a concrete pad, a gray lattice going up a hundred and seventy feet, with a ladder up one leg inside a cage and a steel cable running down the middle of the ladder. The wind was out of the west at six or eight knots, and there was high cloud.

Sam had found the climbing gear in a locker in the boat shed: a full-body harness, a lanyard with two hooks, and a sleeve that clamped onto the ladder's cable. He laid it out on the concrete and went over it against the label sewn into the harness, the webbing on both shoulders, the stitching, every buckle. The inspection tag said March. He put the sleeve on the cable upside down, and it wouldn't go on. Right side up it went on, and when he hung his weight from it, it locked and wouldn't move. He read the label out loud to John twice: the sleeve rides up with you, you don't unclip from it until the lanyard is around the leg up top, two hooks, one always on.

The work order had a sketch on the back. The antenna was at a hundred and fifty feet on a standoff arm on the north leg, with a platform at one forty, and the jumper ran from the antenna down to the main line at the platform.

John had the haul rope, run through a pulley Sam had clipped to the cage at the bottom, with a canvas bag tied to the end holding the jumper, the tape, a wrench, and a rag. Catherine stood off to one side with her bag on the concrete. Tyler was on *Active*'s flight deck with the binoculars and Dan, where Sam had put him, and John could see the small figure at the rail.

Sam started up, slow, three points on the ladder, the sleeve sliding up the cable at his chest. At fifty feet he stopped and took a breath and went on. At a hundred he stopped longer. At the platform he swung his lanyard hook around the leg and clipped it, then the second, and keyed the radio.

"I'm at the platform."

"Take a minute," Catherine said.

He took it. John watched him up there against the cloud, and his own hands were wet inside his gloves on the rope.

"Okay," Sam said. "I've got the connector. The boot's split down one side. It's green inside."

"Bring it down," John said.

He got it off at the antenna, then at the platform. He put the old jumper in the bag and John let it down, hand under hand, and untied it and turned the connector to the light. The pin was furred with green.

John tied on the new one and Sam hauled it up. The sheet in the gold bag said hand tight and then a quarter turn with the wrench, and the work order said how to tape it: rubber tape first, stretched and wrapped from the bottom up so the water ran off it, then black tape over that. Sam had John read it up to him a line at a time. It took him most of an hour.

"Come down," John said. "Same as you went up."

John went up the back stairs to the comms room while Sam was coming down. The gray box on the rack had been showing one dim light since Sunday. Now it had two, and the second one was flickering. He plugged in the laptop.

The lines came up faster than he could read them.

He keyed the handheld. "Sam. It's working."

He asked the model from the laptop.

**AIS receiver 1, tower, 150 ft: running. Signal since 14:52.**
**15 vessels reporting in last 10 minutes. Farthest: 38 nm W, off Pillar Point. Course or speed changes by helm after 23:06 Sept 4: 0.**
**New contacts since receiver 1 restored: 6. Alerts sent: 6.**

His phone had been buzzing in his pocket since he'd come up the stairs. He took it out and turned the volume down and left the alerts on.

At the bottom of the tower Sam was sitting on the concrete with the harness still on and his back against the cage.

That evening, while John was taking her plate, Sam came down to the sick bay with the old jumper. Della had slept most of the afternoon. She took the connector in her good hand and turned it to the light and looked into it.

"Yep," she said. "There's your August."

She held it up to him. "Want it?"

"No," Sam said.

She put it in the pocket of her flannel shirt.
