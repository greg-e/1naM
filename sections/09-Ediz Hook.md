# Ediz Hook

**06:40 local Pacific Time, Thursday**

Catherine had the thermometer in Della's ear when John came across the hall with his blanket around his shoulders. "A hundred point two," she said. "It was a hundred and one four." She had spent the night on the stool and her glasses were pushed up in her hair. She ran the ultrasound probe under Della's ribs and looked at the screen for a while, then fed another few drops of blood into the handheld analyzer.

"So when do I get out of here?" Della asked. Her eyes had swollen further and she had to tip her head back to see anyone.

"That spleen can start bleeding again any time in the next few days. If it does I want to be down the hall from that scanner, but I haven't got a surgical team here. Saturday, if your numbers hold."

Sam was in the doorway with Dan. "Then we'll go out and get the ship ready. What do we need to know?"

Della shut her eyes for long enough that John thought she wasn't going to answer. Catherine put the plastic cylinder in her hand and made her do ten on it first. John took a clipboard off the end of the stretcher, turned her chart sheet over, and wrote on the back.

The reactor was behind the hangar, inside a double fence, and they could leave it alone. There was a status panel on the fence by the gate, and if every light on it was green that was all anybody could do for it. The cutter's shore power came off the pier in four cables, with a meter for it in her control room. The freezer had a thermometer in the door, and it should read ten below. She said it in pieces, a few words at a time, and then she stopped.

Catherine went down the hall and came back with a cart. A box of body bags from the morgue, two boxes of N95s, gloves, gowns, a bottle of hand gel. "There's five on that ship. I'm not bringing her aboard until they're off it."

"Where do we put them?" Sam asked.

Nobody said anything until Della did. "Not on the base. Not in a reefer, you need those for food. There's old lumber sheds at the foot of the Hook, by the mill. They've got doors."

"What about Huntsville?" Catherine asked. She was writing on a strip of tape and didn't look up.

"I'm not flying off and leaving you two here with no one to lift anything," Sam said. "Once she's aboard we'll talk about it."

Della worked her right hand into the pocket of the hi-vis vest hanging off the stretcher rail and came out with a lanyard. "My badge. There's doors it'll open."

John took the aviation handheld out of his flight bag, set it to 122.9, and showed Catherine the push-to-talk. They wheeled Della to the end bay, which had a window looking north over the roofs to the harbor, and stood the radio on the sill.

**08:20 local Pacific Time**

They stopped at the field first. Citizen Air's flight school had a row of handhelds in a charging rack, and John took the two that still showed a charge and called from the ramp. "Della, John, radio check."

It took a few seconds. "Loud and clear," Catherine's voice said.

Sam looked at the airplane and at the gauge on the Jet-A truck and left both for tomorrow. He wasn't moving the airplane onto a runway he hadn't seen.

Marine Drive ran along the waterfront past the port's yards. The Victoria ferry sat at her dock downtown with her ramp down and a line of cars waiting to board, a few of them with people in them. At the west end of the harbor the road went through the mill, the stacks with nothing coming out of them and the logs piled higher than the 4Runner's roof, and past a row of long gray sheds with sliding doors. John slowed down and they both looked. Then the land narrowed and the road went out along the top of the spit, the Strait on the left with Vancouver Island a low line across it and the harbor on the right, flat and gray, with the town and the Olympics behind it. Gulls got up off the road ahead of them and settled again behind. Dan stood on the back seat with his nose against the window.

They passed the boat launch, a trailer still on the ramp, and the pilot station. At the gate a young man in a blue uniform sat on the stool in the booth with his chin on his chest and his hand hanging at his side, the same flushed pink. Sam found the release and walked the arm up. John opened his notebook and wrote *gate, 1.*

They came around the end of the hangar and the pole lights along the road were burning in the daylight, with no engine running anywhere. Every place John had seen with power since Tuesday had a generator running behind it. Here he could hear gulls and a ventilation fan on the roof of the admin building.

He drove around behind the hangar first. The double fence enclosed a concrete pad not much bigger than a tennis court, a low windowless building with a row of finned radiators along one side, and nothing else. He got out and stood at the panel with the clipboard. Every light was green. OUTPUT 1.1 MW. MODE ISLAND. He took a picture of it and stayed there longer than Sam thought he needed to.

"John."

He got back in the car and called it in. "Della, John. All green. Eleven hundred kilowatts."

"That's about right."

*Active* lay port side to the pier with her bow toward the end of the Hook, white with the red slash on her side, as long as a city block and taller than anything else on the harbor. Four thick black cables came off a pier box and up over her rail. John put his glove on one as he passed it and it was warm.

He shut Dan in the 4Runner with the windows cracked. They put on the masks and the gloves at the foot of the brow and walked up it, the deck moving a little under them, and stepped aboard.

The quarterdeck watch was at the small stand just inside, a woman with petty officer's crows on her collar, lying on her side on the deck with one arm under her and her radio still on her belt. The overhead lights were on. The air was moving and it was cool, and a phone on the bulkhead showed a green lamp. The log was open on the stand with a pen in the gutter. The last entry, in neat block capitals, read *2245 ROUNDS COMPLETE ALL SECURE.* Taped to the bulkhead was a watch bill. John copied the names onto the clipboard: WHITLOCK at the quarterdeck, TRAN the duty engineer, HAAS, ORTEGA, NYGAARD.

Under the mask it smelled the way Cutter had, but thin, with the fans carrying it off. They went down two decks by a wide steel stairway and aft to the control room.

Tran sat at the console in the control room, slid down in the chair, a heavy kid with a flashlight in his shirt pocket. The screen in front of him was still lit. John leaned past him and read the alarm list, a few dozen lines, most of them a bilge sensor and a door. Near the top, time-stamped 04:12 Wednesday, was SHORE POWER UNDERVOLTAGE, and on the next line, one and a half seconds later, SHORE POWER NORMAL. He read that twice. That was the Western grid going down, and the station had dropped off it and picked itself back up in a second and a half.

The freezer door in the provisions flat had a dial thermometer set in it. It read ten below.

It took them about three hours to get five people off the ship. They lifted Tran out of the chair and Sam straightened his legs. Haas was in the galley, sitting on an upturned milk crate against the reefer door with a phone in her lap. Ortega and Nygaard were asleep in their racks in berthing, one deck apart. Each one went into a bag on the spot, and John took the ID card from each and wrote where they'd been found. Sam found a Stokes litter, a wire basket on a rail, clipped to a bulkhead in the hangar, and they strapped each bag in and carried it up the stairs and out across the brow, one of them at each end. Tran was the hardest, two decks and a heavy man. The back of the 4Runner held two at a time with the seats folded, so it took three trips to the foot of the Hook. On the first one Sam slid back the door of the nearest shed. It held racks of dressed fir and smelled like a lumberyard. They cleared a rack at waist height and put the five of them along it, side by side, with a tag on each zipper, and rolled the door shut.

They walked the runway after that. John went back up to the quarterdeck before they left. He stood at the stand for a while, then turned to the page after Whitlock's last line and wrote the date and the time, the five names, where each had been found and where each had gone. He signed it, and added his name again in capitals because nobody would know the signature.

It was a little after four when they got back up the hill to the hospital. The smell had reached the ED, and Catherine had shut the doors at both ends of the hall and put towels along the bottoms. John handed Della the clipboard. She read the five names on it with her head tipped back, and the clipboard shook in her hand, a fine fast shake that rattled the clip. She laid it flat on the blanket to finish. Her face and neck were wet. The room wasn't warm.

Catherine took the clipboard and gave it back to John. She held Della's right hand out flat, the way she had the first night, and watched it.

"When did you last have a drink?"

Della looked at the ceiling. "Monday. A couple beers at my aunt's for Labor Day, before I went in."

"And before Monday?"

"Every night."

Catherine went down the hall to the medication room and came back with a small syringe, and pushed it into the port on the IV line. "That's Ativan. It'll bring the shakes down."

"I don't have the shakes."

Catherine didn't answer that. She wrote the time on the chart sheet and ruled a new column beside it.

**10:30 local Pacific Time, Friday**

They had walked the Hook's runway the afternoon before, all forty-five hundred feet of it, and found a pile of driftwood off the east end and a scatter of clamshells the gulls had dropped. Sam kicked the shells off the centerline for about a quarter of it and gave up. Now he backed the Jet-A truck up to 2PR's wing at the field, John clipped the bonding cable and ran the meter, and they took on enough to fill both tanks. Dan took his spot on the cabin floor beside the Jackery.

Sam walked around the airplane, climbed in, and sat down in the right seat.

John stood in the cabin door with his hand on the frame.

"It's five miles," Sam said. "You've got more hours in that panel than I do. Get in."

John got in the left seat and pulled the belts down. He had called the checklists across three states and watched every start. He ran this one himself with Sam reading, watched the torque and the temperature come up where they were supposed to, and taxied out to 26 a little fast and heavy on the brakes. The wind was out of the west at eight knots. "Power lever up slow, and you're going to need more right rudder than you've ever used," Sam said. "More. More than that."

The 182 had never pulled like this. The nose swung left anyway and John stood on the pedal and got it back to the centerline, and the runway went by a lot faster than he was used to, and at 85 knots Sam said "Now," and it flew. Sam pulled the power back with one finger, left the gear down, and pointed. "Right turn. Stay at a thousand. Don't go looking for it, it's right there."

The spit curved out into the water, with *Active* white against the pier near the end of it. John came around over the Strait and flew east along the outside of the spit with the runway off his right wingtip. "Low pass first," Sam said. "You do it and I'll tell you if I don't like it." John turned around the end of the Hook and flew down the runway at two hundred feet and 110 knots, a little high and a little north of it, and the gulls came up off the pavement all at once. He climbed back to pattern altitude, came around downwind over the harbor, turned base off the end of the spit, and lined up on 26 again.

At five hundred feet Sam put his hand on the yoke. "That's good. My airplane."

"Your airplane."

It was eleven minutes by the clock on the panel, and John's shirt was wet.

They shut down on the ramp in front of the hangar, chocked it, and tied it down at three points. The hangar doors were shut and they left them shut. The seventeen at the station would have to wait until the ship was done. A white pickup with a Coast Guard shield on the door stood by the line shack with its keys on the visor. They got the Jackery down the airstair on two planks, walked it across the ramp on its own wheels, and took it up the brow with a line from the deck. The dev box John carried himself.

They spent the afternoon making the ship a place somebody could live. John found bleach and a mop and did the places where someone had been lying, and then did them again, and by evening it smelled mostly of fuel oil and floor wax. In the wardroom he cleared a corner of the long table and plugged in the dev box. It was the first time since his own office that it had run on anything but a battery, and he left the Jackery on the deck beside it with its panels still folded. Sam worked his way down the officers' passageway opening doors. The captain's cabin had a bunk, a desk, and its own head, with family photographs in a rail along the bulkhead. He looked at them and closed the door, and took the one across from the executive officer's. John took the next one along. Before they left he walked Dan down the passageway and showed him where he'd be sleeping.

They got back up the hill after dark. Della was awake in the end bay with the blanket pushed down off her and her hair dark with sweat at the temples. Catherine's new column on the chart sheet had a number in it for every hour since Thursday afternoon. John read the last few upside down, fourteen, seventeen, fifteen, and didn't know what they meant, except that she had written them.

"She hasn't slept," Catherine said.

"I can hear you," Della said. "What time is it?"

"Nine-twenty," John said.

Catherine sent them to bed on the gurneys across the hall. John lay with the blanket over his shoulders and listened. Della asked the time again at ten, and at a quarter to eleven, and some time after that she said something he couldn't make out and Catherine's voice answered her, low, and kept on for a while. When he got up at five the stool was still pulled up beside the stretcher and Catherine was on it.

**08:50 local Pacific Time, Saturday**

Della had gone to sleep a little after four. It was ninety-nine four at six that morning and a hemoglobin of ten point one. Catherine ran the probe under the ribs one more time and let them see it, the dark line of fluid no wider than it had been Wednesday night. "All right," she said. "We go."

By then the whole hall smelled, and there were flies on the window in the end bay. Sam had taken the white pickup down to the fire station at first light and come back driving an ambulance, a big square rig with the department's name on the side and a cot on rails. "Two of them in the bunk room," he said. "Keys were on the dash." Catherine went through the back of it cabinet by cabinet.

She had been loading the pickup since before dawn, off a list two pages long: oxygen cylinders with their regulators, cases of saline, the antibiotics, plaster and fiberglass rolls and a cast saw, crutches, a wheelchair and a shower chair, a portable ultrasound, the handheld analyzer and every cartridge for it she could find. The drawers from the medication cabinet she brought out one at a time and packed into a gray lockbox. That box she carried out herself and put in the front of the ambulance by her feet.

The brow had been steep on Thursday at low water. Sam had taken a tide table off the wall at the pilot station, and high water was at 11:52. "We'll have her on wheels," he said. "Let's be there at eleven-thirty."

Getting her onto the cot took the three of them on Catherine's count, with Della holding her own sling against her chest. John drove. He kept it under twenty on Marine Drive with Catherine calling out the bumps from the back before he could see them, and he took the rail spur at the mill at about the speed of a man walking. There were no windows on the sides of the box. "Where are we?" Della asked through the pass-through.

"Coming up on the mill," John said. "Passing the sheds."

She didn't say anything else until the gate, and then she said, "Who's in the booth?"

"I don't know his name yet. Young, dark hair."

"Peña," Della said. Catherine's voice said something low. John drove through under the raised arm.

The tide was up, and the brow lay almost level from the pier to the rail. They left her on the cot and rolled it across, John at the head and Sam at the foot, Catherine alongside with a bag of saline up in one hand and the other flat on Della's chest. Dan barked from somewhere down the passageway. Coming aboard, Della turned her head toward the stand with the log on it, and turned it back.

The sick bay was on the main deck, down a passageway wide enough for the cot with room to walk beside it. The floors were clean and the lights were bright, and the wheels hardly made a sound. The whole ship was less than two years old.

It was a small white room with two bunks, an exam table, an X-ray head on a track, and more locked cabinets than John expected. They got her across from the cot to the lower bunk on Catherine's count. Catherine checked the splint and the lines, clipped on a pulse ox, and stood looking at the number with her hand on Della's wrist, and then she looked at the room. She started opening cabinets. A steel safe with a keypad was bolted to the deck under the counter, and she stopped in front of it.

"H.S. had the combination," Della said. "Chief Dorsey. He's on leave in Spokane."

Catherine looked at it a little longer. "It can wait," she said, and went on to the next cabinet.

**18:00 local Pacific Time**

The galley was all stainless steel, built to feed a hundred and twenty people four times a day. John had scrubbed it down on Friday. Under the hood, past the kettles and the convection ovens, was a griddle four feet long. He had looked at it for some time on Friday and hadn't said anything to anyone. He lit it now and held his hand over it until it was ready.

He'd brought up a twenty-pound bag of shredded potatoes, two flats of eggs, and a slab of bacon that took a saw, and he put the potatoes down thin and scattered and left them alone. Dan lay across the galley door with his chin on the coaming, watching. Sam came through in a clean Coast Guard work shirt with no name on it and stood over the griddle with a coffee.

"I went by the fence," Sam said. "Eleven hundred kilowatts, all green. The ship's pulling about a third of it."

John loaded four plates and Sam took two of them, and they went along the passageway. Catherine had pulled a stool up beside the lower bunk. Della was raised on a pillow with the sling across her chest and the plastic cylinder on the blanket beside her, and the swelling had come down enough to see both of her eyes.

John set a plate on the blanket in front of her. She ate a few bites of the potatoes with her right hand and put the fork down, and Catherine didn't push her.

**19:30 local Pacific Time**

Catherine set the plate on the counter and looked at Sam.

"We need to talk about Huntsville," she said.

"What's in Huntsville?" Della asked.

John told her. There was a third lead in the 911 logs, an address in Huntsville, Alabama, sixteen calls and no recording. He had tried the number Wednesday morning and it wouldn't connect.

John got out the pocket notebook and turned back to Tuesday night. "Browns Ferry is about twenty miles west of the Huntsville airport. Three reactors. The window I got on the pools opens on the sixteenth. Most days the wind there is out of the west or southwest."

Sam picked his iPad up off the exam table and worked it for a minute. "Eighteen hundred miles. Omaha for fuel. Off at first light, we're there a little after four their time, and back here Monday before dark."

"I'm going with you," Catherine said. "If they're hurt they need a doctor." She looked at Della's numbers on the monitor, then at John. "Her numbers have held, but she's not out of danger. You're staying with her."

"All right," John said.

"You won't hear from us once we're over the Cascades," Sam said to him. "If we're not back by Tuesday night, well, it's been really good knowing you."

John looked at Sam, "You'll be back."

Catherine went to the first cabinet and started taking down bags of saline. "John, I'm going to write all this down, and you're going to read it back to me."
