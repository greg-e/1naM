## 3. Port Angeles Harbor: open items resolved

### 3.1 Non-anchorage area — RESOLVED `[SRC]`

**33 CFR 110.230**, Port Angeles Harbor: a single **non-anchorage area** (no vessel may anchor "at any time"; transit by the most direct route only; no dragging, seining or fishing that might foul underwater installations). The boundary, from the regulation's text:

| Point | Regulation (DMS) | Decimal |
|---|---|---|
| Start, on shore | 48°07′03.83″N, 123°24′20.67″W | 48.1177°N, 123.4057°W |
| 2 | 48°07′38.43″N, 123°24′04.67″W | 48.1273°N, 123.4013°W |
| 3 | 48°07′36.03″N, 123°23′50.67″W | 48.1267°N, 123.3974°W |
| 4, on shore | 48°06′56.73″N, 123°24′08.67″W | 48.1158°N, 123.4024°W |

It is a small nearshore box at the **southeast corner of the harbor**, ~0.8–1.4 nm south of Ediz Hook Light (the shape of an underwater-installation protection zone; the city marks it with shoreline signs). **The suggested anchor position (48.1300°N, 123.4170°W) is ~0.65 nm west-northwest of the nearest corner — outside it.** `[DERIVED]`

Other constraints from the Coast Pilot `[SRC]`: log booming grounds in the north part, extending more than 1 nm from the west shore (harbor ~2.5 nm long → booming grounds roughly west of about 123.44°W; the anchor position is ~1 nm east of that `[DERIVED]`); a 5-fathom rock at 48°07′22″N, 123°13′18″W **(this longitude is ~10 nm east of Ediz Hook Light — appears to be a typo in the Coast Pilot text or a different feature; ignore for this harbor, `[VERIFY]`)**; deadheads/sinkers inside the Hook; Puget Sound VTS asks for notification before anchoring.

### 3.2 Bridge-remote anchor release — RESOLVED (mostly) `[SRC]`

Sources: Wikipedia, *Anchor windlass*; seamanship references (theseafarer.com, knowledgeofsea.com):

- A bridge-mounted remote for the windlass exists, but "it is **not normally possible to operate a windlass, in a safe manner, entirely from the bridge**": windlasses need a chain stopper to absorb load and a safety cable/pin to prevent accidental release.
- Normal practice is a **forecastle party** clearing the brake and stoppers and standing by the windlass, in constant communication with the bridge.
- Two methods: **"letting go"** (anchor falls on the brake — fast, fine in moderate depth) vs **"walking back"** (lowered under power — slower, controlled). Guidance on when to walk back rather than let go depends on depth; the harbor's ~15 fathoms (~27 m) is in the range where many masters prefer walking back `[VERIFY]`.

**For the prose:** Yohan has to stop the engines, go forward alone, clear the stoppers, and work the windlass by hand — a real, physical, solitary scene that can't be done from the bridge. `[DERIVED]` from the above. He would also need the anchor **lighting/day shapes** (an anchor ball or anchor light) shown and the bridge left ready to back the engines; not a scene requirement.

### 3.3 The ship is an off-grid power, fuel and radio plant `[SRC]` / `[DERIVED]`

- **Fuel:** a typical container ship in "hotel mode" (main engine stopped, generators running) burns **~3–8 t/day** (sources: transportgeography.org; maritimepage.com; a *Scientific Reports* transpacific-crossing study). A ship three days out of Yokohama on a 10-day passage still carries thousands of tonnes.
- **So:** the *Pacific Tender* can run its own generators (lights, heat, hot water, galley, laundry, a hospital room, a water-maker) for **months** on a tiny fraction of her remaining fuel. `[DERIVED]`
- **Boats:** SOLAS requires rescue-boat and lifeboat davits to launch by **gravity or stored mechanical power independent of the ship's supply**, operable by one person (sources: Lloyd's Register class news 17/2023; Marine Insight; Fountom). So Yohan can put a boat in the water alone, with no shore power. The details of his ship's actual boats are unknown `[VERIFY]`.
- **Radio:** a GMDSS radio room (VHF, SSB, satellite terminal) — the seed of the ISS ham link; a 40 m-plus mast height for antennas. (Yohan already used VHF, SSB and a satellite terminal in Section 9.)

## 6. ISS line of sight

### 6.1 The orbital geometry `[SRC]` + `[DERIVED]`

- The ISS orbits at ~51.6° inclination, ~400 km up `[VERIFY]`. Its ground track **spends the most time near its northern and southern limits** (~51.6°). Observers at 45–50°N — Port Angeles is at ~48.1°N — get **many passes, often high in the sky**, either just north or just south of the site, always moving west to east.
- **This corrects an earlier note of mine** in `research-compendium.md` §3.7, which said passes at ~48°N run lower than farther south. It is close to the opposite: passes are frequent and often high; what matters is a low horizon **in every direction**, because a pass can arrive in the northern or southern sky.
- Visual spotting needs the ISS in sunlight while the observer is in twilight or dark (dawn/dusk passes) `[SRC]` ham doc.
- The ham doc's rule for radio: passes above ~30° give the longest, cleanest contact; below ~20° buildings and terrain often block it entirely `[SRC]`.

### 6.2 Terrain `[DERIVED]` — angles are estimates; check on a topo map `[VERIFY]`

| Site type | Sky assessment |
|---|---|
| **Elwha valley (Grace's house, 48.04°N)** | Enclosed by foothills and ridges close on the south and west — likely a high horizon, poor. (Unlike Mount Angeles, the valley walls are within a few miles.) |
| **Port Angeles waterfront / hillside** | North: open water (Strait). South: **Mount Angeles (~6,450 ft) ~8–9 nm away → about 7–8° elevation** — below the ~20° radio threshold and near the ~10° a visual observer would want cleared |
| **Ediz Hook** (flat spit, water on both sides) | Best horizon on the harbor: water north and south. Mount Angeles ~9 nm south, ~7° |
| **Sequim / Dungeness Valley** (flat prairie, ~17 mi east) | Olympics front 10–20 mi south → ~4–7°; **driest, sunniest ground on the peninsula** (§7). Very open sky |
| **Lake Crescent Lodge / Sol Duc Hot Springs** (Olympic NP lodges, ~20–30 mi west) | Mountain lake and river valleys with 2,000–4,000 ft walls within a mile or two → 25–35° `[VERIFY]` — poor; the lodges fail R3 |
| **The ship's bridge / monkey island** | ~40 m above the water; horizon limited only by Mount Angeles (~7°) and the Hook. Excellent |

**So R3 alone rules out the Elwha valley and the national-park lodges, and favors the waterfront/Hook, Sequim–Dungeness, and the ship.** Whatever they choose also needs a clear mast or roof location for a VHF/UHF antenna.

## 7. Winter clock

### 7.1 Solar `[SRC]` (Washington Solar Authority, TurbineGenerator.org, others; Seattle as a proxy)

- Seattle: ~**1.5–2.2 peak sun hours** in December vs ~**5.5** in July; annual average ~3.7. December output is roughly **25–40% of summer**.
- Off-grid designers in the Pacific Northwest "use solar sized for autumn through spring, with a **propane or diesel generator for the 2–3 worst winter months**."
- **Rain shadow:** Sequim–Dungeness gets ~15 in/yr, Port Angeles low-20s in, Seattle 37–38 in `[SRC]` (Peninsula Daily News; Sequim real-estate/weather sources). Port Angeles is "almost as sunny as Sequim" by the Peninsula Daily News's headline. Sept–Oct on the Strait side are good solar months; November is the pivot.
- Approximate monthly peak sun hours for a Puget Sound–like site: Sept ~3.5–4, Oct ~2–2.5, Nov ~1.3–1.8 `[VERIFY]` (general knowledge; I have not sourced these).

### 8.3 Consequences of the choice

- **Bodies come first.** The station is a 24-hour SAR, watch and alert operation: at 2:00 AM Eastern (11:00 PM Pacific) there are duty crews — watchstanders, helicopter and boat alert crews, and the Transit Protection System's alert forces — and probably some cutter crews aboard. **Under the craft rule ("check who'd actually be present at 2:00 AM"), they are dead on station.** The group has to find and lay them to rest before living there; this is a real, humane scene, and a good vehicle for the group's dynamics (Catherine's expertise, John's and Sam's first Coast Guard ties, Tyler kept away from it). **Author (2026-09-20): 17 Coast Guard crew.**
- **Weapons.** The station holds small arms and armed force-protection boats. The story need not use them; noting them so a character doesn't stumble on a "surprise."
- **Yohan's ship** anchors close by: ~0.65 nm. The 41-ft utility boat, or a cutter's small boat, gets the group out to her; Yohan can stand watch on the anchor from the station's windows and radio, which also softens the dragging-anchor risk (§7.3).
- **Comfort and dignity.** "Comfortable" is now a matter of effort: a small crew converting a working base to a home is a strong "keep the full cast engaged" setup (Grace and Yohan on power; Catherine on water and hygiene; Sam on the boat, fuel and the PC-12 on the Hook; John on radios and the data model; Tyler on the ISS watch).
- **The radio scene.** The Coast Guard radios here are where John can answer Yohan's hail on VHF ch 16 (§9) — the FBO-radio echo with Sam.

**Where the dead are on Day 9–10 (arrival `[CANON]`).** Web-sourced forensic timelines (several are cleaning-company and student guides — treat as approximate): bloat begins ~3–5 days after death (later in cool weather); **active decay, with the strongest odor**, follows; **advanced decay starts ~10–25 days**; skeletonization takes weeks to years depending on temperature; an exposed body in a temperate climate can be reduced to bone in roughly a year. For the story: the health danger is water and hygiene, not the corpses; the emotional danger is real.

### 8.10 The abandoned container ship: the worst case, and does it matter? (author: "the container ship is abandoned")

**Where she sits:** anchored at ≈ 48.1300°N, 123.4170°W, ~0.65 nm SSW of the Coast Guard station. Port Angeles Harbor is "protected from all except east winds, which occasionally blow during winter" `[SRC]` Coast Pilot; in southeast gales "the wind is not usually felt but some swells roll in."

**Which way she would drift `[DERIVED]`:** in an **east gale** she drags **west** — toward the harbor's west shore, the log booming grounds and the mill and port wharves, about 1.7 nm away. The group's camp on the Hook is **north-east of her, i.e., upwind, and physically safe** in that case. A wind from the north-northeast would push her toward the town waterfront; a south or southwest wind toward the Hook (both are shielded by the mountains and the Hook). **The yacht should not be moored where a dragging ship could reach it.**

**Worst cases, most likely first:**

| # | What happens | Precedent / basis | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Nothing, for a long time** | Laid-up ships sit at anchor for months to years | She rusts; her lights, and her AIS transponder, go dark once the power dies. A landmark and a grave. **Most likely** |
| 2 | **She drags in a winter east gale and grounds** on the soft bottom off the west shore, or strikes wharves | High-windage container ships drag in strong wind; the harbor's bottom is soft and shoals are gentle `[DERIVED]` | Hull damage and a possible fuel leak; a container stack shifts; port infrastructure damaged. The group is upwind |
| 3 | **Containers go overboard** | *Rena* (2011): many containers fell overboard, hazards to shipping, toxins washing up **six years later** `[SRC]` Maritime NZ/TAIC | Floating debris that damages small boats — the **yacht** and the station's boats are at risk. Some cargo is toxic |
| 4 | **Fire** in the containers | Lithium-ion batteries and misdeclared goods are the biggest cause of container-ship fires; "a major container-ship fire occurs roughly every 17 days worldwide" `[SRC]` Motorship/Marine Link; *X-Press Pearl* (2021) burned for nearly two weeks at 1,500 °C with 25 t of nitric acid aboard `[SRC]` | With no one aboard and no firefighting, a fire burns until it is spent — a smoke plume over the harbor for weeks; toxic; can't be stopped. Lower risk than for a crewed ship (no powered reefers, no crew), but lithium thermal runaway needs no power `[DERIVED]` |
| 5 | **Worst: grounded, holed, fuel spill, then breakup** | *Rena* (2011): 236 m, 1,368 containers, **1,733 t of heavy fuel oil**, ~200–350 t spilled, **~50 km of coastline oiled**; she **broke in two after three months**; salvage cost ~$700 million and ran to 2016 `[SRC]` | In a small enclosed harbor, an oil scar on the Hook's beach and the harbor shore for years; no cleanup crews exist. **Unlikely, but this is the worst** |

**Does it matter?** It depends what you want it to do:

- **To the group's survival — mostly no.** They are upwind on land, with their own fuel and rainwater; the harbor is not their drinking water. The **exposed assets are the yacht and the station's boats** (mooring choice), any harbor fishing or clamming, and the air on a fire day.
- **To the world — very little.** Thousands of unattended hazards now sit around the planet (spent-fuel pools, chemical plants, refineries, tank farms). The book already uses the nuclear pools as the big one; a ship in a harbor is a small local one. It fits the theme of "nobody is minding the store."
- **To the story — only if you make it.** It can be background dread, or it can be **a set piece**: a November east gale, the ship dragging across the harbor while the group watches from the Hook, and the crew's tomb going down (Yohan's emotional payoff). It can also be a **clock** (a reason to be ready), or something Yohan decides to prevent.

**What Yohan can do once, before he leaves her `[DERIVED]`:** let go the **second anchor** and pay out more chain (a ship on two anchors with good scope holds in much more wind); stop the engine and isolate the fuel; close the watertight doors; leave only battery-powered anchor lights (a ship dark at night is a hazard to the group's boats); and record the cargo manifest — the ship carries thousands of containers of goods `[VERIFY]` that could be a future supply source, opened one at a time by boat. For the couple of months the story needs, a shut-down two-anchor ship is very likely to sit.

**And the 22 crew `[DERIVED]`:** if they are still aboard, sea burial before he leaves is a natural, dignified beat and removes one loose end (Q-28).
